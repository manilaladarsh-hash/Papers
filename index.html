01 · The core idea

Keep direction.
Lose the repeating pattern.

Ask two separate questions: is there a preferred axis, and does the density repeat in space?

Nematic: a direction without a density wave.

Translation symmetry is preserved; the axis-interchange symmetry is broken.

Translations: preservedRotation: C₄ → C₂

Schematics of symmetry and correlations, not pictures of elongated electrons. The headless line segments stand for a preferred correlation axis; their drawing grid is not charge order. The checkerboard shown is a C₄-symmetric density wave in both directions. Ideal clean states are assumed.

Kivelson 1997 preprint · PDF pp. 1–4 Fradkin 2010 · PDF pp. 3–7 Nie 2014 · PDF p. 1

A headless direction

For a nematic director, θ and θ + 180° describe the same state. A 90° turn exchanges the two axis choices. Nematicity concerns rotation symmetry; superconductivity concerns a different order parameter.

03 · Inside the Cu–O plane

A square model can
choose rows over columns.

Kivelson, Fradkin and Geballe find a nematic regime in the three-band Emery model at strong coupling.

Nematicity need not make stripes.

At the model's x = 1 endpoint, every unit cell can choose the same oxygen orientation. Cells stay equivalent, but x and y become inequivalent.

Oₓ is occupied; Oᵧ is empty. This is a uniform nematic insulator in the model.

Filled circles indicate holes, not electron density. x = 1 means two holes per Cu unit cell in this model; it is not a typical experimental cuprate doping. This endpoint has a charge gap, so the drawing does not depict conducting channels.

04 · What survives disorder?

Lose the phase.
Keep the preference.

A density wave needs its peaks to line up over long distances. A nematic only needs a persistent preference between the two axes.

Geometry

Vestigial nematic is possible

Position is scrambled; direction survives.

⟨ψₓ⟩ = ⟨ψᵧ⟩ = 0N ∝ ⟨|ψₓ|²⟩ − ⟨|ψᵧ|²⟩ ≠ 0

ψₓ and ψᵧ describe density modulation along x and y. N is a composite measure of which modulation is stronger. Broken-up stripes illustrate correlations, not an experimental image or a simulated phase boundary.

Regime cartoons assume low temperature and an incommensurate CDW that favors stripes. “Weak” and “strong” have no calibrated values here. In strict 2D, any nonzero random field prevents macroscopic Ising nematic long-range order; finite domains can still look oriented.

Nie 2014 · PDF pp. 1–4 Carlson 2006 · PDF p. 1

A limit worth remembering

The authors explicitly identify their saddle point's ordered solution in disordered strict 2D as an artifact. Layer coupling is essential to the finite-disorder long-range-order argument.

They also distinguish incommensurate CDWs from commensurate ones: discrete translation breaking can survive weak disorder in circumstances where the incommensurate phase cannot. Nie 2014 · PDF pp. 4–5

01

Melting stripes

Electronic Liquid Crystal Phases of a Doped Mott Insulator

Kivelson, Fradkin & Emery · 1997 preprint · 5 PDF pages

Stripes can lose their positional order while retaining a preferred direction. That intermediate state is an electronic nematic.

StripesSuperconductivityStrong coupling
Open original PDF ↗Link to this note
Question, methods, results & limits

The question

Can an array of conducting stripes avoid locking into an insulating charge-density wave, and instead support a conducting or superconducting liquid crystal?

System and conditions

A two-dimensional array of metallic stripes in a doped antiferromagnet; the main calculation assumes a spin gap on each stripe. Nd-doped LSCO, LSCO and YBCO enter as experimental motivation, not new measurements.

What the authors did

A theory of coupled elastic strings and one-dimensional charge modes. The authors average over transverse quantum fluctuations and analyze interstripe CDW and Josephson couplings perturbatively.

Central results · authors' claims

  • Bending changes neighboring stripes' arc lengths differently, dephasing their density modulations. In the model, CDW phase locking is eliminated in the bulk to all orders in the stated perturbative expansion. Kivelson 1997 preprint · PDF pp. 1–2
  • Close encounters enhance the average Josephson coupling without the arc-length dephasing that affects finite-wavevector CDW order. For Kc > 1/2 and nonzero pair coupling, the smectic is globally superconducting; Kc < 1/2 with sufficiently weak coupling admits a gapless, non-Fermi-liquid state. Kivelson 1997 preprint · PDF pp. 1–3
  • The proposed phase diagram contains crystal, smectic, nematic and isotropic regimes. Its horizontal axis is transverse zero-point fluctuation energy, not a calibrated hole-doping axis. A twofold lattice field rounds the nematic transition into a crossover. Kivelson 1997 preprint · PDF pp. 3–4

Interpretation

Authors' proposal: electronically generated stripes are light enough to fluctuate strongly, creating phases that conventional heavy molecular solids would not readily support. Their cuprate interpretation is suggestive and explicitly allows alternative readings of the neutron peaks.

Limitations and boundaries

  • The ordered-stripe calculation neglects dislocations and overhangs. The later nematic phase diagram uses symmetry arguments and liquid-crystal analogies, rather than solving the full cuprate Hamiltonian.
  • The superconducting result depends on the stripe charge parameter and coupling; nematic order alone does not imply superconductivity. Lattice symmetry and interplane effects change the idealized phase behavior.

Why it matters here

Our synthesis: this is the clearest starting point for separating stripe position, stripe orientation and superconducting phase coherence.

02

Distorting a metal

Quantum Theory of a Nematic Fermi Fluid

Oganesyan, Kivelson & Fradkin · 2001 · arXiv v2 · 7 PDF pages

A metal can become nematic by spontaneously distorting its Fermi surface. Its soft orientational mode can strongly disrupt ordinary quasiparticles.

Fermi surfaceQuantum criticalityCollective modes
Open original PDF ↗Link to this note
Question, methods, results & limits

The question

What happens to collective modes and single-electron excitations when an isotropic Fermi liquid becomes a nematic metal?

System and conditions

Primarily spinless fermions in a rotationally invariant two-dimensional continuum with a quadrupolar interaction. Three-dimensional and lattice extensions are discussed; no particular cuprate composition is calculated.

What the authors did

Introduce a symmetric traceless quadrupolar field, integrate out fermions, find saddle points, compute Gaussian collective modes, then calculate the one-loop electron self-energy.

Central results · authors' claims

  • In this paper's normalization, the isotropic saddle point is stable for 2 NF F2 > −1. Violating that condition produces a quadrupolar Fermi-surface distortion and nematic order. Oganesyan 2001 · PDF pp. 1–2
  • The soft longitudinal critical mode has dynamic exponent z = 3. In the ordered continuum nematic, the orientational Goldstone mode is Landau overdamped for generic propagation directions, with special symmetry directions supporting longer-lived modes. Oganesyan 2001 · PDF pp. 3–4
  • At one loop, the generic scattering rate scales as |ε|^(2/3), with a strong angular factor. Along principal axes the rate instead scales as |ε|^(3/2). Lattice anisotropy gaps the orientational mode and can restore an anisotropic Fermi liquid below a crossover energy. Oganesyan 2001 · PDF p. 5

Interpretation

Authors' conclusion: damping by the particle-hole continuum signals breakdown of the Fermi-liquid expansion over most of the continuum nematic Fermi surface. They do not settle whether the ultimate low-energy state remains a non-Fermi liquid or develops another order.

Limitations and boundaries

  • The Gaussian and one-loop results are approximation-dependent. The paper warns that fixed-parameter perturbation theory may fail at very low energy; its conclusions are not an exact solution of all nematic metals.
  • A continuum Goldstone mode is different from an Ising nematic on a crystal lattice. The cuprate ARPES comparison is speculative, and the authors note an orientation mismatch in their simplest picture.

Why it matters here

Our synthesis: this supplies the momentum-space route and explains why a nematic metal can have unusual dynamics even without a static stripe density wave.

03

A microscopic mechanism

Quasi-one-dimensional dynamics and nematic phases in the two-dimensional Emery model

Kivelson, Fradkin & Geballe · 2004 · Physical Review B · 7 PDF pages

A square-symmetric Cu–O model can produce one-dimensional charge motion and spontaneously choose rows over columns.

Emery modelStrong couplingCu–O plane
Open original PDF ↗Link to this note
Question, methods, results & limits

The question

Can a microscopic model with fourfold symmetry be shown to support a nematic ground state in a controlled limit?

System and conditions

The three-band Emery model on a Cu–O plane: Cu sites form a square lattice and O sites occupy bond centers. x is additional holes per Cu unit cell, with total hole count 1 + x; this model's x = 1 endpoint is not ordinary experimental cuprate doping.

What the authors did

Project into a low-energy subspace at strong repulsion with direct O–O hopping tpp = 0. Prove row/column quasiparticle conservation to leading order and exploit one-dimensional solutions; map a classical limit to an Ising model.

Central results · authors' claims

  • A hole attempting to bend from a row into a column incurs a Vpp-related energy cost. In the strict strong-coupling, tpp = 0 limit, charge dynamics is confined to rows or columns; the relevant conserved quantities include quasiparticle structure, not just bare hole counts. Kivelson 2004 · PDF p. 2
  • At x = 1, uniform unit cells choose horizontal or vertical oxygen occupation. The two ground states break 90° rotational symmetry, preserve translations and have a charge gap: a nematic insulator. Kivelson 2004 · PDF p. 3
  • At small x, the fully nematic energy contains a Fermi-pressure term W x³. An isotropic arrangement gains Fermi-pressure energy but adds an effective crossing repulsion Veff x²; at sufficiently small x that repulsion favors nematicity. Quantum effects stabilize uniform nematic regimes near x = 0 and x = 1. Kivelson 2004 · PDF p. 4

Interpretation

Authors' conclusion: nematicity exists in a controlled strong-coupling regime of a plausible cuprate model. This establishes a mechanism and an existence result, rather than demonstrating that real cuprates occupy this exact parameter regime.

Limitations and boundaries

  • The paper explicitly notes that cluster estimates give tpp/t about 1/3 in real materials, whereas the proof uses tpp/t → 0. Appendix proofs require additional inequalities among repulsions.
  • Corrections permit two-dimensional hopping and may replace the strictly one-dimensional non-Fermi-liquid behavior at the lowest energies. Weak corrections need not remove nematic symmetry breaking; long-range Coulomb forces also alter phase separation.

Why it matters here

Our synthesis: it shows why a nematic need not be literally a collection of visibly separated stripes, and why 'nematic' specifies symmetry more broadly than 'nematic Fermi fluid'.

04

Domains leave a signal

Hysteresis and Noise from Electronic Nematicity in High-Temperature Superconductors

Carlson, Dahmen, Fradkin & Kivelson · 2006 · Physical Review Letters · 4 PDF pages

Local nematic domains can reveal themselves through hysteresis and slow resistance noise even when their average orientation cancels.

DisorderTransportHysteresisNoise
Open original PDF ↗Link to this note
Question, methods, results & limits

The question

How can one detect local nematic tendencies in a disordered sample without macroscopic orientational long-range order?

System and conditions

A two-dimensional square-lattice model of nematic patches with two orientations, random local orienting fields, ferromagnetic alignment coupling J and an applied orienting field h. LSCO hysteresis and YBCO nanowire noise are motivating observations from other experiments.

What the authors did

Glauber updates of a random-field Ising model plus a resistor network for each patch configuration. Macroscopic resistances are calculated with a bond-propagation algorithm.

Central results · authors' claims

  • For typical model configurations, normalized resistance anisotropy Ra closely follows mean orientation m. This is a robust approximate relation, not an identity for arbitrary domain arrangements; finite-size and finite-temperature deviations are noted. Carlson 2006 · PDF p. 2
  • The published hysteresis example uses 100 × 100 patches, T = 0, disorder width Δ = 3J, local resistance ratio r = 2 and field step J/N. Nested loops show return-point memory; incongruent subloops indicate interactions. Carlson 2006 · PDF pp. 2–3
  • A 6 × 6 example at T = 0.5J, Δ = 2J and r = 2 shows telegraph-like resistance switches from a correlated four-patch cluster. The cited YBCO experiment used a 250 × 500 nm nanowire at 100 K, with about 0.25% switches on roughly 50-second timescales; these are not simulated physical time units. Carlson 2006 · PDF pp. 3–4

Interpretation

Authors' proposal: test transport hysteresis, cluster noise and anticorrelated fluctuations of Rxx and Ryy to look for nematic domains. The paper connects a domain model to measurable quantities; it does not establish that every noise event is nematic.

Limitations and boundaries

  • Random fields preclude macroscopic Ising nematic long-range order in strict 2D. Layer coupling and uniform orthorhombic fields change the situation.
  • The model contains no explicit magnetic moments, so comparisons with LSCO magnetization hysteresis are suggestive. It also does not uniquely identify the microscopic origin of resistance noise.

Why it matters here

Our synthesis: domain averaging can hide a signal. A controlled orienting field and the dynamics of domains can expose information that a single zero-field anisotropy value misses.

05

The experimental bridge

Nematic Fermi Fluids in Condensed Matter Physics

Fradkin, Kivelson, Lawler, Eisenstein & Mackenzie · 2010 · Annual Review · 26 PDF pages

The same broken symmetry connects very different materials. Reliable detection requires separating an electronic tendency from lattice bias and domain averaging.

SymmetryQuantum HallSr₃Ru₂O₇CupratesEvidence
Open original PDF ↗Link to this note
Question, methods, results & limits

The question

What defines an electron nematic, how can point-like electrons form one, and what evidence supports it in different correlated materials?

System and conditions

A review spanning high-Landau-level GaAs/GaAlAs two-dimensional electron systems, the bilayer ruthenate Sr₃Ru₂O₇, cuprates and Fe-based superconductors. Its evidence and open questions reflect the literature available in 2010.

What the authors did

Synthesize symmetry arguments, stripe-melting and Pomeranchuk mechanisms, collective-mode theories, and previously published transport, thermodynamic, neutron and surface-probe experiments.

Central results · authors' claims

  • Nematic order breaks an axis-interchange symmetry while preserving translations. In its pure form it also preserves inversion and time reversal. Stripe melting and quadrupolar Fermi-surface distortion are complementary routes to this symmetry pattern. Fradkin 2010 · PDF pp. 3–8
  • The quantum-Hall examples show very large directional resistance differences near half-filling of Landau levels N ≥ 2. A cited ν = 9/2 example has a resistance ratio about 100 at low temperature; anisotropy becomes essentially absent above roughly 150 mK. Device resistance and intrinsic resistivity must be distinguished. Fradkin 2010 · PDF pp. 9–13
  • In very clean Sr₃Ru₂O₇, transport anisotropy is revealed by an in-plane field component in a phase around 7.8–8.1 T, with maximum Tc about 1.2 K. Bulk thermodynamics supports a distinct phase; a large structural distortion does not explain the observed anisotropy. Fradkin 2010 · PDF pp. 14–19
  • YBCO's orthorhombicity both aligns domains and provides an anisotropic background. Strong temperature and doping trends, together with independent probes, motivate the electronic interpretation. Cuprate evidence is presented with more caveats than the clearest quantum-Hall and ruthenate cases. Fradkin 2010 · PDF pp. 19–21

Interpretation

Authors' synthesis: nematicity is a common language for spatial symmetry breaking, with mechanisms and transport magnitude dependent on each material. The review leaves quantum criticality, domain-wall scattering and several microscopic explanations open.

Limitations and boundaries

  • This is a 2010 review rather than a current survey or a new experiment. The illustrated data belong to the original experimental studies cited there.
  • Anisotropy alone does not establish spontaneous nematicity. Crystal fields, domains, disorder and measurement geometry can obscure or mimic the expected response; the quasiparticle critical theory also has unresolved beyond-leading-order issues.

Why it matters here

Our synthesis: use this as the organizing map, then return to the other five papers for a specific mechanism or a sharper test.

06

What survives disorder

Quenched disorder and vestigial nematicity in the pseudogap regime of the cuprates

Nie, Tarjus & Kivelson · 2014 · PNAS · 6 PDF pages

Disorder can destroy long-range stripe position while leaving its discrete choice of direction intact. This remaining symmetry breaking is vestigial nematic order.

DisorderVestigial orderCharge orderNMR
Open original PDF ↗Link to this note
Question, methods, results & limits

The question

What remains of an incommensurate stripe CDW when weak quenched disorder disrupts its long-range order?

System and conditions

A layered tetragonal model favoring a unidirectional incommensurate CDW, with weak interplane coupling and Gaussian random fields. Cuprate experiments motivate the model; the LTT structure of LBCO explicitly favors one orientation per plane.

What the authors did

Landau–Ginzburg–Wilson theory, replicas, a large-component self-consistent Gaussian/saddle-point approximation, and a correspondence with random-field Ising nematic order.

Central results · authors' claims

  • For the incommensurate random-field CDW considered, arbitrarily weak disorder removes conventional long-range stripe order in d ≤ 4. Its discrete nematic component can still order in a layered three-dimensional system below a nonzero critical disorder strength. Nie 2014 · PDF pp. 1–2
  • A vestigial state can have ⟨ψx⟩ = ⟨ψy⟩ = 0 but unequal average squared amplitudes. The paper's model combines thermal scattering TG with a disorder-pinning term σ²G²; finite-width scattering peaks need not uniquely reveal the clean-limit stripe or checkerboard pattern. Nie 2014 · PDF pp. 1–4
  • The nematic critical disorder tends to zero as interplane coupling vanishes. The authors identify the saddle point's ordered solution in strict 2D with disorder as an artifact. They propose strain-response tests and comparisons of in-plane oxygen NMR quadrupolar broadening. Nie 2014 · PDF pp. 3–5

Interpretation

Authors' proposal: look for the more robust vestigial symmetry to infer tendencies of an ideal clean system. They argue this may help interpret pseudogap CDW and nematic observations; it is not a proof that every cuprate pseudogap is caused by one nematic mechanism.

Limitations and boundaries

  • The model favors stripes over checkerboards and treats an incommensurate CDW. Commensurate CDW order breaks translations discretely and has a different disorder response.
  • Detailed phase boundaries use large-component and saddle-point approximations. The cited Supporting Information is not contained in this six-page PDF and was not independently reviewed. Strain, lattice orthorhombicity and slow equilibration can round observed signatures.

Why it matters here

Our synthesis: this reconciles short-range charge correlations with an orientational signature and gives a focused connection to oxygen NMR. A powder-averaged signal does not automatically isolate in-plane x/y symmetry.

Quick self-check

Three distinctions to keep.

Does nematicity require static stripes?

No. It specifies a pattern of rotation symmetry breaking. A Fermi-surface distortion or unequal intra-unit-cell oxygen response can realize it without a stripe density wave.

Fradkin 2010 · PDF pp. 3–8
Does zero bulk anisotropy exclude local nematicity?

No. Oppositely oriented domains can cancel. Local probes, orienting fields and dynamics can provide additional information.

Carlson 2006 · PDF pp. 1–4
Can weak disorder leave nematic long-range order in 2D?

In a strictly 2D random-field Ising nematic, no. In a coupled-layer 3D system, a finite-disorder vestigial nematic phase can survive under the paper's conditions.

Nie 2014 · PDF pp. 1–4
