Muon spin rotation measurements of heterogeneous field response in overdoped La2-xSrxCuO4

Applied magnetic field reveals a heterogeneous response in overdoped LSCO even when its mean local field barely shifts.

The research question: Is overdoped LSCO magnetically uniform in an applied field?

In two minutes

The whole guide at a glance

  1. Transverse-field muons probe overdoped LSCO crystals with x = 0.15, 0.19, 0.25 and 0.30 in applied fields from 0.2 to 7 T.
  2. The precession frequency tracks the mean field; an extra exponential damping measures how widely local fields spread.
  3. That extra rate grows roughly linearly with field and on cooling; a Curie–Weiss form describes it but is not unique.
  4. At x = 0.30, 2 K and 5 T the spread is about 5 mT while the mean stays within 0.5 mT of the applied field, and it is much larger for H ∥ c.
  5. Heterogeneity is established; dopant-induced staggered islands are a qualitative proposal, not a real-space image.

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

Applied magnetic field reveals a heterogeneous response in overdoped LSCO even when its mean local field barely shifts. External fields from 0.2 to 7 T are applied mainly along the c axis, with an additional x = 0.30 crystal oriented to test an in-plane field. A nearby calcium-carbonate reference independently calibrates the applied field. On cooling, the muon precession loses amplitude more rapidly: a distribution of local fields becomes broader. Fourier transforms reveal substantial symmetric broadening but very little displacement of the mean. This matters because the overdoped region was often treated as a uniform Fermi liquid, while the observed response varies strongly from one nominally equivalent local site to another.

Terms to understand before starting
  • LSCO: layered La2-xSrxCuO4; nominal x is Sr per formula unit
  • Local probe: a distribution can matter even when the bulk average barely changes
Muon polarization
Ensemble average direction of implanted muon spins, inferred from positron asymmetry.
Local field distribution
Range of magnetic fields sampled at stopping sites; its width differs from its mean.
Transverse field
Applied field perpendicular to the initial muon spin, producing a precession signal.
Longitudinal field
Applied field along the initial muon spin, useful for testing weak quasistatic transverse fields.
Motional narrowing
Rapidly changing local fields average over the probe window and can reduce relaxation.
Curie response
Approximately inverse-temperature magnetic susceptibility associated with an effective moment contribution.

Mean and width constrain different things

The oscillation and its damping should be read as different pieces of evidence. Each muon precesses at a frequency proportional to its local field. If many sites have nearly the same field, the oscillations remain in phase longer; if their fields differ, the summed oscillation damps faster. The Fourier presentation makes this distribution easier to recognize: broadening concerns the spread of frequencies, while a displacement of the central position concerns the mean field. The source’s particularly useful overdoped comparison has a large width without an equivalently large average shift.

Part 1

The samples and the central observation

Part 2

What the fitting parameters mean

The asymmetry is fitted by an oscillatory cosine multiplied by a fixed Gaussian decay and an additional exponential. The frequency tracks the mean field. The fixed Gaussian term accounts for nuclear dipoles and apparatus field variation; the exponential rate λ characterizes the extra field-induced width. The paper notes that a root exponential better represents some low-temperature shapes and that the usual fit becomes imperfect for x = 0.30 below 10 K. Longitudinal-field results from related measurements constrain relaxation from transverse fluctuations, supporting the interpretation of the transverse-field decay as a largely static local distribution under the measurement conditions. Relaxation is therefore not automatically a direct spin fluctuation rate.

PDF p. 2 · PDF p. 4

Part 3

Field, temperature and doping trends

The additional rate is approximately linear in applied field. Its slope grows on cooling and can be summarized by C(x)/(T - Θ(x)) plus a temperature-independent background. The authors emphasize that other temperature functions can describe the data equally well; a good Curie-Weiss fit is a characterization rather than a unique theory. Across the combined dataset, C grows approximately with Sr concentration. Θ changes markedly near optimal doping. The x = 0.15 crystal has a somewhat larger response than x = 0.19, so the result is not a perfectly monotonic sequence at every condition. At high overdoping, the increasing field response continues while superconducting Tc decreases.

Part 4

Why the width is not simply a distribution of bulk dopings

For x = 0.30 at 2 K and 5 T the source reports an approximately 5 mT full width, while the mean is within 0.5 mT of the applied field. The size of the local distribution exceeds what shifting among known LSCO bulk doping responses would easily explain. Consequently, a patch model of different hole concentrations alone is incomplete here. A superconducting-patch explanation that correlated with Tc in underdoped samples also does not straightforwardly explain why the overdoped broadening grows as Tc weakens. These are tests of particular mechanisms, not a rejection of electronic inhomogeneity itself or a quantitative measurement of local hole concentration.

Part 5

The proposed staggered response and its uncertainty

The authors explore emergent isolated moments and weak ferromagnetic clusters using moments constrained by bulk magnetization. These models have difficulty reproducing both the large local relaxation and the marked field-direction anisotropy. A small staggered cluster around a Sr dopant offers a qualitative alternative: opposite spins give large nearby fields while partially cancelling the total moment. The paper suggests that dopant disorder and magnetic field stabilize such regions, and that shrinking correlations can reconcile increasing uniform Curie response with reduced antiferromagnetic neutron weight. It explicitly calls for field- and disorder-dependent neutron studies. Keep the secure observation, spatially heterogeneous field response, distinct from the proposed microscopic staggered-island explanation.

Part 6

How to compare the evidence

A useful reading exercise is to imagine two distributions with the same mean field: one narrow and one broad. Their bulk magnetizations can look similar, but implanted muons precess at a wider spread of frequencies in the second case and lose ensemble polarization faster. The experiment measures precisely this distinction, with strong orientation dependence providing another constraint. It does not follow that every broadening source is a spontaneous ferromagnet or that the proposed islands have been photographed. Compare the temperature dependence, field dependence and anisotropy jointly before choosing a microscopic model. In particular, a Curie-Weiss-like fit to the width describes a growing response; it cannot, by itself, specify the spatial pattern or establish a zero-field ordering transition.

Part 7

Read polarization before naming the magnetic state

A muon is a tiny local magnetic compass, but the recorded signal combines many compasses. The measured asymmetry follows their average spin polarization as a function of time, usually in microseconds. A static field makes an individual spin precess; a collection of different static fields makes those spins gradually lose phase agreement. The resulting envelope can therefore decay even if each field is perfectly stationary. Conversely, rapidly fluctuating fields can average away and produce weak relaxation. These possibilities explain why the words relaxation, freezing and order cannot be substituted for one another. The fitting function describes a distribution and a time window before it identifies a microscopic arrangement.

The initial polarization direction and the applied field direction matter separately. A field transverse to the spin produces precession; a field parallel to the spin changes sensitivity to weak transverse components and can decouple a static distribution. The paper chooses a particular geometry to test a particular question. Reading the time traces requires keeping that geometry attached to the fitted rate. Also separate the mean precession frequency from the envelope: the former tracks an average field, while the latter tracks its spread or time dependence. A narrow mean shift can coexist with a substantial local distribution.

Part 8

Connect a local distribution to bulk response

A magnetometer adds contributions across the specimen, while the muon experiment detects the distribution of fields at stopping sites. Opposite contributions can cancel in the bulk sum and still leave a large field at some individual sites. A small average response therefore does not imply that every microscopic environment is nonmagnetic. Equally, a broad muon distribution is not a photograph of where the moments sit. Converting it into a cluster size, moment concentration or real-space pattern requires a model of stopping sites, couplings and spatial arrangement. The supplied article compares such models with several independent observations rather than treating one damping rate as a complete structural solution.

The most useful reading order is measurement, fit, model and conclusion. First identify what actually changes in the asymmetry trace under the stated control. Next identify which fit component captures the change. Then ask what physical distribution could produce that component, and which competing distributions the other data disfavor. This prevents a descriptive temperature fit from becoming an unearned claim about a unique ordered state. Temperature, Sr composition, field, holder background and accessible time range all belong to the result. Comparisons with another paper become meaningful only after matching those conditions; a positive induced response and a zero-field null can both be correct.

Part 9

The Fourier distribution is the bridge from time to space

The oscillation and its damping should be read as different pieces of evidence. Each muon precesses at a frequency proportional to its local field. If many sites have nearly the same field, the oscillations remain in phase longer; if their fields differ, the summed oscillation damps faster. The Fourier presentation makes this distribution easier to recognize: broadening concerns the spread of frequencies, while a displacement of the central position concerns the mean field. The source’s particularly useful overdoped comparison has a large width without an equivalently large average shift. That is why a uniform susceptibility picture is inadequate even if the specimen has a modest net magnetization.

The fitted Gaussian and exponential components also have distinct roles. The fixed Gaussian contribution represents an established background broadening in the chosen analysis; the additional exponential decay describes the field-dependent electronic response. It should not be reinterpreted automatically as a single electronic fluctuation rate. The fit becomes imperfect below 10 K in the highest-doped crystal, which is a warning against forcing every trace into a single universal functional shape. Field and temperature scans, plus the parallel-versus-perpendicular comparison, carry more microscopic information than one best-fit parameter at one temperature.

Part 10

Test a dopant-island proposal against three constraints

The proposed staggered island is useful because neighboring contributions can oppose each other in the bulk sum yet generate sizable local fields near individual muons. It can therefore address the width-versus-mean discrepancy more naturally than simply assigning each patch a different uniform bulk susceptibility. However, this is a hypothesis that must also explain the increasing response with Sr doping and the strong field-direction anisotropy. A model that reproduces only the low-temperature width has not solved the whole experiment. The paper explicitly evaluates alternatives involving moments and ferromagnetic clusters before favoring the staggered scenario as a qualitative possibility.

One subtle result is the relationship to superconductivity. A local-superconducting-patch explanation motivated by underdoped samples does not transfer automatically to these overdoped crystals, where the field response grows while bulk Tc weakens. That does not prove local pairing is impossible; it shows that a Tc-based correlation from a different regime is insufficient here. Likewise, the fitted Curie-Weiss-like temperature form is a compact description of growing response rather than a unique test of isolated moments. The strongest conclusion is extensive magnetic heterogeneity induced in applied field. The microscopic islands, their precise dimensions and their persistence after removing the field are not independently imaged by this experiment.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Why does a transverse-field result not contradict the zero-field null study?

The applied field can create or stabilize a response absent in zero field.

Is a Curie-Weiss fit unique evidence of isolated moments?

No. The paper notes alternative temperature fits and tests isolated-moment models separately.

Why can staggered islands reconcile local and bulk probes?

Opposite local contributions can partially cancel in bulk magnetization while retaining sizable local fields.

Can a broad distribution have nearly zero mean shift?

Yes. Positive and negative deviations can cancel in the average while retaining a large width.

Why does increasing broadening as Tc weakens challenge a simple superconducting-patch account?

It breaks the particular correlation used to motivate that account in underdoped samples; it does not exclude every possible local pairing effect.

Is λ in the fitted TF envelope automatically a fluctuation frequency?

No. It describes additional damping in a chosen model; static field-distribution broadening can contribute without a unique dynamic rate.
