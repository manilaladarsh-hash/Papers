Ferromagnetic order beyond the superconducting dome in a cuprate superconductor

The supplied 2020 LCCO thin-film study combines hysteretic transport, magnetization and trained Kerr measurements as evidence for low-temperature ferromagnetic order beyond its superconducting dome.

The research question: Are highly overdoped electron-doped LCCO films simple nonmagnetic metals?

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

This paper reports magnetic order in electron-doped LCCO films beyond their superconducting dome. Resistance decreases in field, depends on direction and remembers the sweep history below about four kelvin. Magnetization and thermopower loops provide separate checks, while oppositely trained polar Kerr signals detect a magnetic component. The combination supports the ferromagnetic interpretation more strongly than one negative magnetoresistance curve. Thin-film backgrounds, moment direction and sample controls remain essential. The ordered films can still have quadratic metallic resistivity. The authors propose competition with superconductivity and a nearby ferromagnetic quantum-critical endpoint, but those consequences are interpretations requiring more work rather than directly mapped zero-temperature transitions.

Terms to understand before starting
  • LCCO: Electron-doped La2−xCexCuO4 thin films in the source.
  • Magnetoresistance: A change of resistivity with applied magnetic field.
  • Anisotropic magnetoresistance: A resistance response dependent on the relative magnetic and current directions.
  • Hysteresis: Different branches at the same control value after different histories.

A descriptive explanation, step by step

Follow the full argument.

Part 1

What makes these films a useful comparison

LCCO is an electron-doped cuprate whose Ce concentration can be increased past the end of superconductivity in thin films. The paper focuses on x = 0.18 and 0.19, where the studied samples are nonsuperconducting and show low-temperature quadratic resistivity, and compares them with x = 0.17. The conceptual challenge is that a T² metal beyond a superconducting dome is often assumed to be a nearly conventional, nonmagnetic Fermi liquid. The authors ask whether a magnetic ordered state has been overlooked at very low temperature. The scope matters: these are specified films, substrates and doping values. The paper does not measure every overdoped cuprate, and the words 'beyond the dome' do not by themselves specify the phase behavior of all hole- and electron-doped materials.

Part 2

Follow the magnetoresistance evidence carefully

For x = 0.18 and 0.19 the transverse magnetoresistance becomes negative and strongly hysteretic below about 4 K. Its dependence on field orientation relative to current and magnetization is also examined. In comparison, the superconducting x = 0.17 film has positive normal-state magnetoresistance in the measured regime. A field-sweep loop matters because it reveals history dependence characteristic of magnetic domains rather than merely a smooth change of scattering rate. However, negative magnetoresistance can have several origins, so the authors do not rely on its sign alone. They compare the hysteresis with magnetization and thermopower measurements. Thermopower provides a particularly useful check against an explanation based only on electrical current heating in the resistance measurement, since it probes a different transport response.

Part 3

Magnetization and Kerr supply independent checks

After substrate background subtraction, the magnetization shows a low-field hysteresis with a coercive scale comparable to the transport loop. The reported moment at 2 K is approximately 0.06–0.08 Bohr magneton per formula unit. The field-direction comparison indicates predominantly in-plane moments. Kerr measurements use a zero-area-loop Sagnac interferometer, designed to reject reciprocal optical effects. Cooling with opposite small perpendicular training fields reverses the Kerr signal below roughly 4 K, while zero-field cooling produces no discernible polar Kerr signal. These conditions are not contradictory: a predominantly in-plane moment may have a field-trained out-of-plane component detectable by polar Kerr. The optical data should therefore be read with the training direction and background subtraction, rather than reduced to 'Kerr is nonzero in every measurement.'

Part 4

How controls support an intrinsic-film interpretation

The main text reports reproduction over more than twenty films grown on three substrates, including transport loops on different substrates and Kerr measurements on LSAT. This helps challenge an explanation tied to only one substrate or one sample. The quantitative background procedures, materials methods and additional control figures are referred to a separate supplement, which is not included among the supplied PDFs. The lesson therefore attributes those controls to the main text without claiming an independent inspection of the missing supplement. Taken together, hysteretic transport, magnetization and trained Kerr response are presented as compelling evidence for low-temperature ferromagnetic order in the studied overdoped films. The inference relies on the combined experimental pattern and its controls, rather than a single response that uniquely labels the microscopic state.

Part 5

Keep the quantum-critical claim at its proper level

The authors connect their ferromagnetic phase to earlier transport work near the dome endpoint, where a resistivity exponent around 1.6 had been reported. They regard this as suggestive of ferromagnetic critical fluctuations and a possible superconducting/ferromagnetic quantum critical point. That exponent was not newly established across an entire phase boundary by the measurements in this paper. Nor does observing magnetism prove a quantitative mechanism for the loss of Tc or superfluid density. The source explicitly calls for further work on the nature and consequences of the magnetic phase. Read the historical 2020 conclusion in two parts: the films show a strong combined magnetic signature; the proposed competition and criticality are an explanation to test. This distinction allows the surprising result to inform wider cuprate questions without turning its extrapolations into universal facts.

Part 6

Three separate questions inside one magnetoresistance curve

A negative magnetoresistance means that applying field reduces resistance relative to the zero-field value. An anisotropic magnetoresistance means the response changes with the orientation of current relative to the magnetic state or applied field. Hysteresis means a forward sweep and a reverse sweep do not retrace the same branch. These are distinct statements. A negative sign alone can have several microscopic origins, whereas reproducible low-field history dependence and the appropriate directional response give additional information. The paper combines all three in nonsuperconducting electron-doped LCCO films.

The comparison with x = 0.17 is especially useful because that film lies inside the superconducting dome and has different normal-state magnetoresistance. At x = 0.18 and 0.19, negative and hysteretic behavior develops below roughly four kelvin. Read the temperature and field direction attached to each curve before pooling them. The chemical Ce fraction is the source's sample label, not a precisely calibrated universal planar carrier density for all cuprates. The observations locate the reported magnetic phase in these films; they do not independently establish where every neighboring composition orders.

Part 7

Magnetic and thermoelectric checks reduce a transport-only ambiguity

Magnetization records a magnetic response directly, while thermopower records a voltage generated by a temperature gradient. The source finds low-field history dependence in both in addition to resistance. Comparable coercive scales and disappearance of the loops near the reported onset make a coherent magnetic interpretation stronger than a resistance loop alone. Thermopower hysteresis also argues against current heating as the origin of the transport hysteresis, because that measurement does not require the same electrical-current protocol. It remains necessary to attach the specimen and field orientation to each comparison.

The magnetization is a thin-film signal measured on a much larger substrate background, so the stated subtraction is essential. The in-plane moment saturates more readily than the out-of-plane moment, motivating predominantly in-plane ordering. That orientation matters for interpreting a polar Kerr signal, which is sensitive to a different magnetic component. The study reports reproduction across more than twenty films and three substrates, reducing a simple substrate-specific explanation. The underlying detailed controls and supplementary figures are referenced externally and are not included in this three-page supplied file; the guide can explain the reported checks without claiming to re-read unavailable measurements.

Part 8

Training a polar Kerr signal tests a particular component

The optical experiment measures a tiny nonreciprocal polarization rotation with a zero-area-loop Sagnac method designed to reject reciprocal effects such as ordinary birefringence. Field cooling from above the onset in opposite small perpendicular fields produces opposite signs of the zero-field Kerr response on warming. That sign reversal is meaningful: it ties the detected optical component to magnetic training rather than a fixed instrumental offset. The source explicitly states its background subtraction and averaging windows, so those processing choices accompany the onset inference.

Zero-field cooling produces no discernible polar Kerr response in the compared measurement. This does not erase the transport and magnetization evidence. Predominantly in-plane moments can lack a net polar component, while perpendicular training cants or selects a component detectable optically. The Kerr probe therefore tests a specified orientation and history, not every possible magnetic domain. Its scale and reported onset are compared with the other probes rather than converted into an exact universal moment per Cu. Reading the polar geometry prevents the false conclusion that a null signal in one protocol demonstrates no magnetic state.

Part 9

An ordered film and a critical endpoint are different levels of conclusion

The reported combination of transport, magnetization, thermopower and trained optical response supports itinerant ferromagnetic order in the examined overdoped nonsuperconducting films. Their quadratic low-temperature resistivity is compatible with that magnetic metal, so a T² law is not proof that correlations or magnetism disappear beyond the superconducting dome. The source cannot grow higher Ce fractions reliably, and superconductivity obscures the lowest-temperature transport study at lower fractions. These experimental bounds matter before extending the phase across an unmeasured interval.

A separate earlier transport scaling near the dome end motivates a ferromagnetic quantum-critical scenario. The authors connect that prior evidence with the newly reported ordered phase and propose competition with d-wave superconductivity. They do not directly measure a continuous transition at zero temperature or uniquely determine that critical point's universality. The suggestion that competition explains reduced transition temperature or superfluid density requires additional work. The paper's relevance is that it challenges a simple uncorrelated-overdoped picture in this material. Generalizing to all hole- and electron-doped cuprates is an open inference, not a new measurement in the supplied article.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Which doping range supplies the main magnetic evidence?

The studied nonsuperconducting x = 0.18 and 0.19 LCCO films, below about 4 K.

Why can trained polar Kerr be present despite mainly in-plane moments?

A perpendicular training field can cant a component out of plane; zero-field cooling need not produce a polar signal.

Is the proposed QCP itself directly mapped in this report?

No. It is inferred as a possibility using the magnetic evidence and earlier transport work.

Why can a null polar Kerr result coexist with magnetism?

Predominantly in-plane moments need not give a net polar component; perpendicular training changes the component detectable optically.

What does thermopower hysteresis add?

It provides a history-dependent response in a measurement distinct from current-driven resistance, supporting the argument against current-heating artifacts.

Does a T² resistivity law exclude ferromagnetism?

No. The reported itinerant magnetic films retain a quadratic metallic regime; the exponent alone does not establish an uncorrelated state.
