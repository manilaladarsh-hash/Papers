Cu NMR and NQR Studies of High-Tc Superconductor La2-xSrxCuO4

Cu NMR/NQR finds composition-dependent residual spin response; the proposed gapless d-wave explanation depends on a phenomenological DOS model.

The research question: How do local spin response and relaxation evolve through superconductivity from x = 0.075 to 0.24?

In two minutes

The whole guide at a glance

  1. Eight LSCO powders from x = 0.075 to 0.24 (Tc ≈ 18–38 K) are studied with zero-field Cu NQR, Cu NMR and La NQR.
  2. The spin Knight shift (uniform response) grows with x while the antiferromagnetic enhancement of 1/(T₁T) weakens: two different questions.
  3. Below Tc there is no Hebel–Slichter peak; the low-temperature plateau at x = 0.10 shrinks strongly in a field and is traced partly to a magnetic minority.
  4. At x = 0.20–0.24, 1/(T₁T) becomes nearly constant and a residual spin shift remains: low-energy excitations survive in the superconductor.
  5. A gapless d-wave residual density of states fits both observables together, but that explanation depends on a phenomenological model.

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

Cu NMR/NQR finds composition-dependent residual spin response; the proposed gapless d-wave explanation depends on a phenomenological DOS model. Their corresponding transition temperatures are about 20, 27, 30, 36, 38, 29, 24 and 18 K. They combine zero-field Cu NQR, finite-field Cu NMR and selected La NQR. Powders were ground below approximately 20 micrometres to reduce radio-frequency shielding. This practical step matters because losing access to nuclei below Tc can imitate an electronic change. The Cu spectrum has A and B environments and two isotope contributions; B is weak or absent near x = 0.10 and becomes visible at larger x. Its proposed relation to local structural environments is an interpretation, not a unique microscopic identification.

Terms to understand before starting
  • LSCO: layered La2-xSrxCuO4; nominal x is Sr per formula unit
  • Local probe: a distribution can matter even when the bulk average barely changes
NQR
Nuclear quadrupole resonance selected by the local electric-field gradient without an applied magnetic field.
Electric-field gradient
Spatial variation of the electric field at a nucleus; sensitive to structure and electronic charge.
Hyperfine form factor
Momentum-dependent weighting of electronic spin fluctuations at a selected nuclear site.
T1
Longitudinal spin-lattice recovery time of nuclear populations.
T2
Timescale governing loss of transverse echo coherence; it limits signal accessibility.
A/B environment
Two spectrally distinct local Cu quadrupole environments, each also containing isotope contributions.
Knight shift
Field-induced resonance displacement with spin and orbital contributions.

Compare low-temperature echoes

Look for: The x = 0.10 NQR relaxation plateau is strongly reduced by an applied field.

Above Tc the Cu relaxation rate is strongly enhanced in lower-doped samples. A Curie-Weiss form for 1/(T1T) describes the growth of antiferromagnetic correlations, with the fitted Weiss temperature increasing with doping. The authors extrapolate the Weiss scale toward zero near the low-doping superconducting boundary. Meanwhile, the in-plane spin Knight shift increases with x. These observations are compatible because shift weights the uniform susceptibility whereas relaxation sums low-frequency fluctuations over momentum with hyperfine weighting.

A minority magnetic contribution can dominate a low-field relaxation channel.

Fit two observables together

Look for: A model residual DOS is used to calculate both Knight shift and relaxation.

For x = 0.20 to 0.24, relaxation becomes approximately proportional to T at low temperatures, so T1T approaches a constant. At x = 0.24 the entire observed NMR line shifts below Tc relative to the normal-state line, rather than leaving an obvious unchanged normal component. Picture 5 / 8

Use two distinct low-energy weightings

Look for: The spin Knight shift constrains a thermally weighted spin susceptibility, while relaxation in the quasiparticle model depends on the available low-energy states through a different weighting.

The spin Knight shift constrains a thermally weighted spin susceptibility, while relaxation in the quasiparticle model depends on the available low-energy states through a different weighting. Comparing both with one proposed DOS is therefore more restrictive than fitting either curve alone. Before doing so, the measured shift must be separated into spin, orbital and superconducting diamagnetic contributions. A nonzero total shift cannot simply be labeled residual spin susceptibility.

The source's inferred residual DOS is consequently conditional on this decomposition and on the phenomenological gap form used to relate the two nuclear observables.

Picture 7 / 8

Electric frequency and magnetic relaxation answer different questions

Look for: NQR uses the electric-field gradient at a quadrupolar nucleus to select a resonance without an external field.

NQR uses the electric-field gradient at a quadrupolar nucleus to select a resonance without an external field. The gradient describes how the local electric potential changes around that nucleus; electronic occupations, neighboring ions and structural distortions can all affect it. A shift in NQR frequency therefore identifies a changed local electric environment before it establishes a changed hole concentration. Once a frequency slice is selected, the recovery of nuclear magnetization probes the fluctuations that exchange energy with those nuclei.

The distinction explains why the article examines both spectral shape and recovery instead of assigning a magnetic phase to every peak.

Nuclear recovery and echo survival are separate clocks

Spin-lattice recovery describes how the populations of nuclear levels return toward equilibrium after excitation. Its timescale is T1. Echo decay describes how the transverse spin signal loses coherence before it can be detected; its characteristic times involve T2 and sometimes separate Gaussian and exponential components. A nucleus can have a measurable recovery only if its echo survives the pulse sequence and receiver dead time. This means that the published rate may describe a selected subset of nuclei rather than every Cu in the material.

Rapid fluctuations, slow glassy fields, broad frequency distributions and overlap can affect those two measurements differently.

Part 1

What the wide composition series measures

Part 2

Normal-state antiferromagnetic and uniform responses

Above Tc the Cu relaxation rate is strongly enhanced in lower-doped samples. A Curie-Weiss form for 1/(T1T) describes the growth of antiferromagnetic correlations, with the fitted Weiss temperature increasing with doping. The authors extrapolate the Weiss scale toward zero near the low-doping superconducting boundary. Meanwhile, the in-plane spin Knight shift increases with x. These observations are compatible because shift weights the uniform susceptibility whereas relaxation sums low-frequency fluctuations over momentum with hyperfine weighting. Extracting the spin shift requires an orbital background and a correction for quadrupolar effects; the assumed in-plane orbital shift is about 0.24 percent. The nearly constant c-axis shift is dominated by the orbital contribution and hyperfine cancellation.

Part 3

Why the low-doping relaxation plateau needs caution

The rate falls below Tc without a Hebel-Slichter coherence peak, then becomes relatively flat at low temperature in some underdoped samples. The x = 0.10 plateau changes markedly between NQR and finite-field NMR. Broadening of Cu and La lines and non-single-exponential recovery provide evidence for a magnetic minority contribution. The authors attribute the zero-field plateau partly to nuclear spin diffusion toward slowly fluctuating magnetic regions, rather than directly reading it as a superconducting density of states. Recovery curves can contain short, intermediate and long components; the paper defines operational time intervals for these components. They are not automatically spatial volume fractions, and the selected T1 must remain attached to the recovery procedure.

Part 4

What the higher-doping measurements support

For x = 0.20 to 0.24, relaxation becomes approximately proportional to T at low temperatures, so T1T approaches a constant. At x = 0.24 the entire observed NMR line shifts below Tc relative to the normal-state line, rather than leaving an obvious unchanged normal component. Both A and B environments show superconducting changes. A finite residual spin Knight shift accompanies the Korringa-like relaxation. The combination supports residual low-energy excitations within the measured superconducting response and argues against explaining everything as an easily resolved normal-metal admixture. It does not alone fix gap symmetry, disorder strength or the spatial origin of the residual states. Shielding, orbital subtraction and distributions of relaxation remain relevant.

Part 5

The proposed gapless model and its limits

The authors introduce a phenomenological DOS with a finite residual value near zero energy and a nodal contribution above it. Their angular-gap approximation is a convenient proxy for nodal behavior, not the exact two-dimensional cos(2φ) form. Shared parameters are compared with Knight shift and relaxation: for x = 0.24 the residual DOS is roughly half the normal value and 2Δ is about 4.5 kBTc; x = 0.20 has a smaller residual fraction near 0.13 and a larger fitted gap ratio near 8. The paper proposes disorder from mixed structural environments as a cause. It also warns that the fitted gap may not equal a microscopic gap and neglects detailed DOS smearing. A note added in proof reports a conflicting zero-residual-shift result at x = 0.20. Treat the conclusions as sample-dependent historical evidence; the paper’s unusual definition of overdoping is not a modern phase-boundary definition.

Part 6

How to compare the evidence

The paired Knight-shift and relaxation analysis provides a useful internal check. A residual spin shift samples low-energy spin susceptibility, whereas residual relaxation depends on a weighted low-frequency spectrum and, in the quasiparticle model, on powers of the remaining density of states. Reproducing both with one residual-DOS parameter is more restrictive than fitting one curve alone. It is still conditional on orbital-shift subtraction, magnetic-field effects and the assumed gap function. The different short-, middle- and long-recovery intervals also warn that selected nuclei may sample different environments. Compare these sample-specific residual responses with the calorimetric γ in source 14 rather than insisting that every LSCO specimen must have an identical residual fraction. The source itself notes a conflicting later spin-shift report.

Part 7

Electric frequency and magnetic relaxation answer different questions

NQR uses the electric-field gradient at a quadrupolar nucleus to select a resonance without an external field. The gradient describes how the local electric potential changes around that nucleus; electronic occupations, neighboring ions and structural distortions can all affect it. A shift in NQR frequency therefore identifies a changed local electric environment before it establishes a changed hole concentration. Once a frequency slice is selected, the recovery of nuclear magnetization probes the fluctuations that exchange energy with those nuclei. This second measurement can be predominantly magnetic even though the first selection was electric. The distinction explains why the article examines both spectral shape and recovery instead of assigning a magnetic phase to every peak.

Magnetic relaxation is filtered. The electronic spin fluctuations must produce a field at the nucleus, so hyperfine couplings and the geometry of neighboring spins weight the momentum-dependent response. Different nuclei, orientations or local environments can weight the same electronic system differently. A large Cu rate does not automatically mean a similarly large uniform susceptibility, because antiferromagnetic fluctuations can dominate its weighted sum. Conversely, two unequal rates need not mean two unequal carrier densities if their couplings differ. The scientifically informative step is to combine a charge-sensitive environment label with its measured spin response, keeping the selection and the inferred electronic cause separate.

Part 8

Nuclear recovery and echo survival are separate clocks

Spin-lattice recovery describes how the populations of nuclear levels return toward equilibrium after excitation. Its timescale is T1. Echo decay describes how the transverse spin signal loses coherence before it can be detected; its characteristic times involve T2 and sometimes separate Gaussian and exponential components. A nucleus can have a measurable recovery only if its echo survives the pulse sequence and receiver dead time. This means that the published rate may describe a selected subset of nuclei rather than every Cu in the material. Rapid fluctuations, slow glassy fields, broad frequency distributions and overlap can affect those two measurements differently.

A recovery with several components also needs assignment before interpretation. Multiple nuclear levels, two Cu isotopes, overlapping A and B resonances, or different electronic environments can all produce more than one time dependence. A fitted exponential coefficient is not automatically the volume fraction of an electronic phase. When comparing different temperatures, check whether the spectrometer follows the moving resonance and whether the excitation bandwidth samples the same relative part of the line. When comparing different papers, check the pulse separation, frequency choice and fitted recovery convention. These procedural details explain why an apparent disagreement between rates can expose a selection effect rather than a contradiction about the whole spin system.

Part 9

Residual shift and residual recovery test different powers of the spectrum

The spin Knight shift constrains a thermally weighted spin susceptibility, while relaxation in the quasiparticle model depends on the available low-energy states through a different weighting. Comparing both with one proposed DOS is therefore more restrictive than fitting either curve alone. Before doing so, the measured shift must be separated into spin, orbital and superconducting diamagnetic contributions. A nonzero total shift cannot simply be labeled residual spin susceptibility. The source's inferred residual DOS is consequently conditional on this decomposition and on the phenomenological gap form used to relate the two nuclear observables.

The x = 0.24 line comparison provides a complementary check. The observed line shifts as a whole below Tc, rather than leaving a plainly separable unchanged normal component. Both A and B responses change in the superconducting state. This disfavors an especially simple explanation in which the entire residual signal is a resolved normal-metal admixture. It does not rule out disorder, smaller-scale variations or unobserved populations. The paired curves and whole-line behavior are useful because they constrain different parts of the same argument without turning the model into a direct image of a unique gapless state.

Part 10

Do not use the underdoped plateau as the same residual-DOS signal

The low-doping recovery plateau and the higher-doping nearly T-linear recovery have different controls and proposed explanations. In x = 0.10 the plateau changes substantially between zero-field NQR and applied-field NMR. Broad lines and non-single-exponential recovery support a magnetic minority contribution, and the article discusses nuclear spin diffusion toward slowly fluctuating regions. Those observations warn that a low-temperature nuclear rate can remain finite for reasons beyond ordinary superconducting quasiparticles. The operational short, intermediate and long recovery intervals are part of that analysis rather than automatically separate electronic volume fractions.

At higher doping the authors instead compare a residual quasiparticle DOS model with both shift and relaxation. They propose mixed structural environments as a possible cause, while acknowledging that the fitted gap need not equal a microscopic gap and that spectral smearing is simplified. The note added in proof then reports a conflicting residual-shift result at x = 0.20. Including that note is essential to understanding the status of the conclusion: the source provides historically important sample-dependent evidence and a joint model test, not a universal residual fraction required of every LSCO specimen. Comparison with calorimetry should retain different sample preparations, backgrounds and probe weightings.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Why is no coherence peak not a complete gap-symmetry proof?

Strong correlations, lifetime effects and disorder can affect the peak; several independent observations constrain the gap model.

Why compare NQR with NMR at x = 0.10?

Field sensitivity helps expose a magnetic relaxation channel that can obscure superconducting quasiparticle behavior.

Is the residual DOS directly imaged?

No. It is inferred through a model tested against Knight shift and relaxation.

Why does fitting shift and recovery jointly add a constraint?

They weight the low-energy spectrum differently, so one model must account for both rather than fitting one response in isolation.

What does the whole shifted x = 0.24 line disfavor?

A simple resolved unchanged normal-metal contribution accounting for all residual response; it does not eliminate every disorder or spatial-mixture scenario.

Why retain the note added in proof?

It reports conflicting residual-shift evidence and limits any claim of a universal x = 0.20 residual fraction.
