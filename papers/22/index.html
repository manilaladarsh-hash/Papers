Hysteresis and Noise from Electronic Nematicity in High-Temperature Superconductors

Local nematic domains can reveal themselves through hysteresis and slow resistance noise even when their average orientation cancels.

The research question: How can one detect local nematic tendencies in a disordered sample without macroscopic orientational long-range order?

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

Disorder can leave many locally nematic patches whose average direction cancels. This paper asks how transport could reveal them. It gives each patch two possible axes, couples neighboring patches and adds frozen local biases. Each orientation configuration becomes a directional resistor network. Field sweeps generate hysteresis and nested memory; thermal switching of a correlated group can produce telegraph-like resistance noise. Typical simulated resistance anisotropy follows the average orientation, although network geometry prevents an exact identity. The authors propose testing the history of minor loops and opposite resistance changes along two axes. These signatures motivate experiments, but the model does not supply a microscopic copper spin mechanism or turn simulation updates into measured seconds.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Nematic patch: A coarse-grained region with a locally preferred electronic axis.
  • Random field: A frozen local bias favoring one of the two patch orientations.
  • Metastability: Persistence of a configuration until a change makes switching favorable.
  • Resistor network: A connected spatial calculation of current paths from local directional resistances.

A descriptive explanation, step by step

Follow the full argument.

Part 1

Research question and system

How can one detect local nematic tendencies in a disordered sample without macroscopic orientational long-range order? A two-dimensional square-lattice model of nematic patches with two orientations, random local orienting fields, ferromagnetic alignment coupling J and an applied orienting field h. LSCO hysteresis and YBCO nanowire noise are motivating observations from other experiments. Glauber updates of a random-field Ising model plus a resistor network for each patch configuration. Macroscopic resistances are calculated with a bond-propagation algorithm.

PDF p. 1

Part 2

Separate the spin model, network and observable

The Ising variable labels the orientation of a local nematic patch; it does not represent a microscopic Cu spin. Neighboring variables interact, local random fields pin them, and a uniform field aligns their preference. The electrical observable is then obtained by solving an anisotropic resistor network associated with that configuration. A mean orientation can correlate well with resistance anisotropy without determining it exactly, because spatial connectivity matters.

Hysteresis means the configuration retains history while the field is swept. Return-point memory compares a return to a previously visited extremum; incongruent minor loops can reveal interactions. Telegraph noise records switches between metastable configurations, possibly involving several correlated patches. A small grid that flips independently in a teaching widget is not the published network simulation. Nor do Monte Carlo update counts supply an experimental time in seconds without a kinetic model. The paper proposes useful signatures of local nematic tendencies, while disorder, competing fluctuators and measurement geometry limit uniqueness.

Part 3

Results and interpretation

For typical model configurations, normalized resistance anisotropy Ra closely follows mean orientation m. This is a robust approximate relation, not an identity for arbitrary domain arrangements; finite-size and finite-temperature deviations are noted.

The published hysteresis example uses 100 × 100 patches, T = 0, disorder width Δ = 3J, local resistance ratio r = 2 and field step J/N. Nested loops show return-point memory; incongruent subloops indicate interactions.

A 6 × 6 example at T = 0.5J, Δ = 2J and r = 2 shows telegraph-like resistance switches from a correlated four-patch cluster. The cited YBCO experiment used a 250 × 500 nm nanowire at 100 K, with about 0.25% switches on roughly 50-second timescales; these are not simulated physical time units.

Authors' proposal: test transport hysteresis, cluster noise and anticorrelated fluctuations of Rxx and Ryy to look for nematic domains. The paper connects a domain model to measurable quantities; it does not establish that every noise event is nematic.

Part 4

The random-field Hamiltonian is a domain model

Coarse-grain the electronic system into patches larger than the microscopic unit cell. Each patch has a local preferred conducting direction, represented by a variable σ equal to +1 or −1. Neighboring patches gain energy when their orientations agree, through a positive coupling J. A uniform field h biases the whole sample toward one orientation, while each patch has its own frozen random bias hi. The source chooses a Gaussian distribution of those local fields. A patch can therefore prefer an orientation opposed to the current global field when its local pinning or the influence of its neighbors is strong enough.

This Hamiltonian models orientational order and its metastability. It contains neither an explicit copper magnetic moment nor superconducting phase dynamics. Comparisons with magnetic hysteresis in other cuprate experiments are motivations rather than a microscopic magnetic calculation. In a strictly two-dimensional random-field Ising system, macroscopic orientational long-range order is unavailable, but finite domains and strong local correlations can remain. The practical question becomes how a transport experiment can detect those domains even when the average orientation is small or zero.

Part 5

How a patch configuration becomes an electrical measurement

Associate each patch with four resistors, one connection along each lattice direction. The patch orientation interchanges its higher and lower directional resistances. Joining the patches creates a spatial resistor network through which current can take many paths. The ratio r specifies the local resistance contrast. Computing Rxx and Ryy therefore requires solving that whole network; replacing it with the arithmetic mean of local resistances would discard how bottlenecks and parallel paths are arranged. The source uses a bond-propagation method to obtain the macroscopic resistance for a given spin configuration.

The authors compare a normalized resistance anisotropy Ra with the mean orientation m. Their normalization removes the maximum contrast associated with the chosen local r, and the two quantities closely track each other for typical simulated configurations. Their relationship is approximate, not a theorem that any two configurations with equal m must have identical resistance. Finite sample size and temperature can change the correspondence. The mapping also presupposes that nematic patches have the chosen local transport anisotropy; it does not calculate that local anisotropy from a microscopic Cu–O Hamiltonian.

Part 6

Why a field sweep records history

At zero temperature the simulated patch orientations are updated as the uniform orienting field is increased or decreased. A patch can remain metastable until its random field, neighbors and global field combine to make a reversal favorable. Reversing one patch can make neighboring reversals favorable too, producing a collective event. Because the state depends on the route taken through the field values, the increasing and decreasing sweeps need not give the same resistance at the same h. This is the hysteresis loop. The field is an orienting control variable in the model; it should not be assigned a laboratory unit or a unique strain calibration without an additional mapping.

The displayed large simulation has 100 by 100 patches, zero temperature and a disorder width of 3J. These choices demonstrate the mechanism rather than reproduce a specific measured loop. The field step and resistance contrast are also part of that calculation. A visually similar experimental loop is suggestive, but a convincing identification would require the correct conjugate control, sample geometry, reproducibility and alternatives. Explicit crystal anisotropy and three-dimensional coupling can alter the strict two-dimensional limit discussed by the source.

Part 7

Return-point memory and interacting minor loops

A minor loop is formed by reversing a field sweep before reaching saturation and then returning to an earlier extremum. Return-point memory means that the system recovers the configuration associated with that previous extremum when the nested sweep returns there. Following the arrows and reversal points in Figure 2 is more informative than simply observing a closed-looking curve. The property belongs to the specified driven random-field model and update conditions; thermal relaxation can blur ideal closure in a real experiment.

Figure 3 asks a second question. Two subloops can cover the same field interval yet have different shapes because they were reached through different larger histories. This incongruence indicates that surrounding patches influence the switching thresholds, rather than all patches behaving as independent identical hysteretic elements. Return-point memory and subloop congruence are consequently distinct tests. Memory concerns recovering a visited extremum; congruence compares paths between common extrema reached in different environments. Observing one does not automatically establish the other. Together they provide a more structured way to test interacting domains than assigning all slow transport changes to nematicity.

Part 8

What telegraph noise adds and what its clock cannot tell you

At finite temperature a small correlated group can fluctuate between metastable orientations. Its network resistance then switches between a few levels, producing telegraph-like noise. In the source's small example, a four-patch cluster in a six-by-six system produces identifiable resistance switches, while many faster single-patch fluctuations form a different background. A macroscopic sample averages over many regions, so narrowing a device toward a domain scale can make a collective switch easier to see. This supplies a plausible connection to the separately cited YBCO nanowire observation.

Monte Carlo update counts are not seconds. They order the simulation and specify its dynamics, but matching a measured 50-second scale requires a kinetic model and calibration. The paper proposes measuring both orthogonal resistances: reversal of a nematic direction tends to raise one while lowering the other. Such anticorrelation can help distinguish a directional fluctuator from a process that changes conductivity similarly in both directions, including some superconducting fluctuations. It remains a proposed discriminant with geometry and other fluctuators to consider. A single noisy resistance trace is therefore insufficient to identify a nematic domain or its microscopic origin.

Essential equations, meaning and assumptions

H = −J Σ⟨ij⟩ σi σj − Σi (h + hi) σi

Symbols and units: σi = ±1: patch orientation; J, h, hi: energies; hi frozen random orienting field; ⟨ij⟩ neighboring patches.

Physical meaning: Alignment, global bias and local pinning compete in the coarse-grained Hamiltonian.

Symbols and units: N: patch count; r > 1: local high/low resistance ratio; Rxx, Ryy in the same resistance units; m and Ra dimensionless.

What the evidence supports—and where it stops

  • Random fields preclude macroscopic Ising nematic long-range order in strict 2D. Layer coupling and uniform orthorhombic fields change the situation.
  • The model contains no explicit magnetic moments, so comparisons with LSCO magnetization hysteresis are suggestive. It also does not uniquely identify the microscopic origin of resistance noise.

What is the central research question?

How can one detect local nematic tendencies in a disordered sample without macroscopic orientational long-range order?

What is measured or assumed?

Glauber updates of a random-field Ising model plus a resistor network for each patch configuration. Macroscopic resistances are calculated with a bond-propagation algorithm.

What limits the interpretation?

Random fields preclude macroscopic Ising nematic long-range order in strict 2D. Layer coupling and uniform orthorhombic fields change the situation. The model contains no explicit magnetic moments, so comparisons with LSCO magnetization hysteresis are suggestive. It also does not uniquely identify the microscopic origin of resistance noise.

Why can two configurations with the same m have different resistance?

The network depends on how domains connect current paths and create bottlenecks, not only on the average orientation.

What does an incongruent minor loop test?

Different loops over the same field interval reached through different histories indicate influences from interacting surrounding patches.

What proposed measurement strengthens a nematic-noise interpretation?

Simultaneous orthogonal resistance changes can test the anticorrelated pattern expected for a directional switch, while alternatives and device geometry still matter.
