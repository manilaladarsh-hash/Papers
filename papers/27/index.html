Direct search for a ferromagnetic phase in a heavily overdoped nonsuperconducting copper oxide

The research question: Does a ferromagnetic phase replace superconductivity at extreme overdoping?

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

Millikelvin muons detect dilute frozen moments in nonsuperconducting x = 0.33 LSCO, without establishing long-range ferromagnetic order. Zero-field muons are useful because they do not require an applied magnetic field to polarize the probe. Earlier measurements above 2 K showed no static electronic moments for these high dopings. The new work extends measurements to 0.02 K. Its key comparison is LSCO x = 0.24, still superconducting, and x = 0.33, nonsuperconducting. The latter was annealed under high oxygen pressure to homogenize oxygen and showed no resistive superconductivity down to 0.1 K. Sample quality and temperature range are essential parts of the inference.

Terms to understand before starting
  • LSCO: layered La2-xSrxCuO4; nominal x is Sr per formula unit
  • Local probe: a distribution can matter even when the bulk average barely changes
Muon polarization
Ensemble average direction of implanted muon spins, inferred from positron asymmetry.
Local field distribution
Range of magnetic fields sampled at stopping sites; its width differs from its mean.
Transverse field
Applied field perpendicular to the initial muon spin, producing a precession signal.
Longitudinal field
Applied field along the initial muon spin, useful for testing weak quasistatic transverse fields.
Motional narrowing
Rapidly changing local fields average over the probe window and can reduce relaxation.
Curie response
Approximately inverse-temperature magnetic susceptibility associated with an effective moment contribution.

Picture 3 / 8

Dilute is different from uniform

Look for: A distributed set of weak local fields gives exponential relaxation rather than coherent precession.

Commensurate long-range ferromagnetic order would normally produce a well-defined local field and coherent oscillations. The observed extra relaxation is instead approximately exponential, consistent with a dilute distribution of static moments and a Lorentzian-like field distribution. Its quoted half-width is about 1.4 G at 0.02 K. The authors argue against lingering parent-compound antiferromagnetism by considering the disappearance and fast timescale of the relevant correlations at high doping. They do not convert the lack of oscillations into a claim that every form of ferromagnetism is absent.

The observation supports dilute static moments, not commensurate long-range ferromagnetism.

Picture 4 / 8

Combine three probes

Look for: Susceptibility and transport add constraints but do not remove the impurity ambiguity.

The x = 0.33 susceptibility has a Curie component and its χT product is approximately linear over a wide temperature interval, unlike the x = 0.24 comparison. Fits combining temperature-dependent susceptibility and magnetization between about 15 and 150 K give an effective moment of 2.86 Bohr magnetons and approximately 0.00312 moments per tetragonal unit cell. These are model-dependent effective parameters, not a count of individually imaged ions. A dipole-field simulation for that dilute density gives a local width near 1.6 G, close to the muon estimate.

Weak localized ferromagnetism is supported as an interpretation, with extrinsic origins still open.

Part 1

A search below earlier experimental limits

The new work extends measurements to 0.02 K. Its key comparison is LSCO x = 0.24, still superconducting, and x = 0.33, nonsuperconducting. Part 2

The local-field evidence for freezing

The x = 0.24 zero-field spectrum is temperature independent and dominated by nuclear fields. In x = 0.33 an extra electronic relaxation develops below roughly 0.9 K. A longitudinal field of 14 G strongly decouples this additional decay. Comparing the residual longitudinal rate with Gaussian and Lorentzian fluctuation models gives an upper fluctuation frequency near 9.5 × 10^4 Hz at 0.02 K. The moments are therefore effectively frozen on the relevant muon timescale. The spectrum includes a large nonrelaxing contribution from muons stopping in the silver holder, explicitly accounted for in the fit. Ignoring that background would corrupt both the local width and any magnetic volume interpretation.

Part 3

Why the result is not a uniform ferromagnet

Commensurate long-range ferromagnetic order would normally produce a well-defined local field and coherent oscillations. The observed extra relaxation is instead approximately exponential, consistent with a dilute distribution of static moments and a Lorentzian-like field distribution. Its quoted half-width is about 1.4 G at 0.02 K. The authors argue against lingering parent-compound antiferromagnetism by considering the disappearance and fast timescale of the relevant correlations at high doping. They do not convert the lack of oscillations into a claim that every form of ferromagnetism is absent. The specific conclusion is that a long-range uniform ferromagnetic phase of the proposed kind is not established.

Part 4

Bulk susceptibility checks the scale

The x = 0.33 susceptibility has a Curie component and its χT product is approximately linear over a wide temperature interval, unlike the x = 0.24 comparison. Fits combining temperature-dependent susceptibility and magnetization between about 15 and 150 K give an effective moment of 2.86 Bohr magnetons and approximately 0.00312 moments per tetragonal unit cell. These are model-dependent effective parameters, not a count of individually imaged ions. A dipole-field simulation for that dilute density gives a local width near 1.6 G, close to the muon estimate. The consistency suggests that the Curie moments and the moments freezing below 0.9 K belong to the same underlying population.

Part 5

Transport support and the remaining ambiguity

The x = 0.33 in-plane resistivity follows approximately T^(5/3) above 60 K and T² over roughly 4.5 to 50 K. Together with Curie behavior, the authors interpret this as support for weak itinerant ferromagnetism localized around nearby Cu sites at high doping. Transport exponents are indirect evidence and do not determine spin orientation or prove a unique scattering channel. The paper explicitly states that an extrinsic impurity cannot be completely ruled out and that a systematic doping study requires additional large crystals. The appropriate lesson combines a secure frozen dilute local-field observation with a cautious proposed ferromagnetic origin. The missing online supporting information is not part of the supplied four-page PDF.

Part 6

How to compare the evidence

The longitudinal-field control is essential to interpreting the small extra decay. A field applied along the initial muon spin can suppress relaxation from a sufficiently weak quasistatic transverse distribution, whereas rapidly fluctuating fields need not decouple in the same way. This is the role of the 14 G test, distinct from the strong transverse fields in source 26. The source then estimates a dilute-moment contribution using a spatial-field model and compares it with susceptibility. Treat that agreement as a consistency argument: moment concentration, moment size and spatial arrangement are not all independently imaged. The absence of a resolved precession also matters. Dilute frozen moments create a broad disordered distribution rather than the coherent local field expected for some uniform ordered states.

Part 7

Read polarization before naming the magnetic state

A muon is a tiny local magnetic compass, but the recorded signal combines many compasses. The measured asymmetry follows their average spin polarization as a function of time, usually in microseconds. A static field makes an individual spin precess; a collection of different static fields makes those spins gradually lose phase agreement. The resulting envelope can therefore decay even if each field is perfectly stationary. Conversely, rapidly fluctuating fields can average away and produce weak relaxation. These possibilities explain why the words relaxation, freezing and order cannot be substituted for one another. The fitting function describes a distribution and a time window before it identifies a microscopic arrangement.

The initial polarization direction and the applied field direction matter separately. A field transverse to the spin produces precession; a field parallel to the spin changes sensitivity to weak transverse components and can decouple a static distribution. The paper chooses a particular geometry to test a particular question. Reading the time traces requires keeping that geometry attached to the fitted rate. Also separate the mean precession frequency from the envelope: the former tracks an average field, while the latter tracks its spread or time dependence. A narrow mean shift can coexist with a substantial local distribution.

Part 8

Connect a local distribution to bulk response

A magnetometer adds contributions across the specimen, while the muon experiment detects the distribution of fields at stopping sites. Opposite contributions can cancel in the bulk sum and still leave a large field at some individual sites. A small average response therefore does not imply that every microscopic environment is nonmagnetic. Equally, a broad muon distribution is not a photograph of where the moments sit. Converting it into a cluster size, moment concentration or real-space pattern requires a model of stopping sites, couplings and spatial arrangement. The supplied article compares such models with several independent observations rather than treating one damping rate as a complete structural solution.

The most useful reading order is measurement, fit, model and conclusion. First identify what actually changes in the asymmetry trace under the stated control. Next identify which fit component captures the change. Then ask what physical distribution could produce that component, and which competing distributions the other data disfavor. This prevents a descriptive temperature fit from becoming an unearned claim about a unique ordered state. Temperature, Sr composition, field, holder background and accessible time range all belong to the result. Comparisons with another paper become meaningful only after matching those conditions; a positive induced response and a zero-field null can both be correct.

Part 9

Follow the freezing argument one control at a time

The decisive comparison is not simply a larger relaxation rate in one crystal. The x = 0.33 trace acquires an extra low-temperature decay, whereas the x = 0.24 comparison remains nuclear-like. Applying 14 G along the initial muon spin suppresses the extra decay strongly. That control shows that fields weak enough to be rearranged in the probe geometry are responsible; the authors then compare fluctuation models to estimate how slowly they change. Their upper rate bound at 0.02 K supports effective freezing on this probe window. It does not say that the moments are eternal, that they first appear chemically at 0.9 K, or that each Cu carries an identical ordered moment.

The very large holder contribution is especially instructive. Only 35% of the fitted asymmetry belongs to muons stopping in this x = 0.33 specimen; 65% comes from the silver support. The flat contribution is therefore not evidence that 65% of the sample has escaped magnetism. The sample term contains both the temperature-independent nuclear function and an additional electronic exponential. Reading the two terms physically is necessary before using the trace to discuss magnetic volume. The experiment’s claim concerns a dilute field-generating moment population, not a sample fraction obtained by copying the holder amplitude.

Part 10

Why susceptibility consistency is useful but not identification

In the susceptibility analysis, a temperature-independent contribution and a Curie contribution can produce an approximately straight χT plot: multiplying χ0 + C/T by T gives χ0T + C. This helps explain why the transformed plot is more revealing than the upturn alone. The simultaneous magnetization and susceptibility fit constrains a moment size and density, and their dipolar-field estimate is close to the width inferred by muons. Two probes therefore agree on a plausible scale for the moments. The agreement is stronger than an isolated low-temperature upturn but weaker than chemically identifying the responsible defect or electronically imaging a ferromagnetic island.

The transport fits add another layer. A straight segment when resistivity is plotted against T^(5/3) or T² identifies an approximate power law within its stated interval. It does not automatically supply the spin configuration or a unique collision process. The high-temperature T^(5/3) region and lower-temperature T² region coexist in the same nonsuperconducting specimen; neither is the freezing onset itself. The paper uses these observations to motivate localized weak itinerant ferromagnetism, then explicitly leaves an extrinsic origin open pending a systematic doping series. Keeping that final reservation preserves the distinction between the secure freezing observation and the proposed microscopic cause.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Why is 0.02 K important?

It accesses freezing below the earlier 2 K limit.

Does T^(5/3) resistivity prove ferromagnetism?

No. It is supporting transport evidence interpreted with the magnetic probes.

Has an extrinsic impurity been excluded absolutely?

No. The source explicitly calls for a more systematic doping study.

Why is the flat holder term not a nonmagnetic volume fraction?

Muons stopping outside the crystal create that signal; its fitted 65% amplitude belongs to silver rather than to a region of the specimen.

What does linear χT mean in the stated model?

For χ = χ0 + C/T it becomes χT = χ0T + C; the slope and intercept separate a constant susceptibility and a Curie contribution.

What remains unknown after the two local-field widths agree?

The microscopic chemical or intrinsic electronic origin, real-space arrangement and uniqueness of the moment model remain unresolved.
