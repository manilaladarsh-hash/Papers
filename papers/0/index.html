Superconductivity without phonons

Electronic response can create an effective pairing interaction whose attractive and repulsive regions are matched by the symmetry of the Cooper-pair state.

The research question: How a repulsive response can support pairing?

In two minutes

The whole guide at a glance

  1. A quasiparticle disturbs the surrounding spin or charge density and a second quasiparticle feels that response — an induced interaction that needs no phonons.
  2. The induced spin interaction is ∝ −g²(s·s′)χ, so singlet pairs (s·s′ = −3/4) and triplet pairs (+1/4) feel the same response with opposite sign and different strength.
  3. Near antiferromagnetism the interaction alternates in sign from site to site; a d-wave pair with nodes on the diagonals avoids the repulsive regions.
  4. In momentum space, strong scattering by Q = (π, π) joins Fermi-surface regions of opposite gap sign, so the repulsion helps pairing instead of hurting it.
  5. Geometry and energy scale both matter — cubic CeIn₃ versus layered CeRhIn₅ — and the mechanism is a framework that still needs material-specific tests.

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

One electron can disturb the charge or spin density around it, and another can interact with that delayed collective response. The effective interaction can contain favorable and unfavorable regions even when the microscopic electron repulsion is strong. Pairing becomes a matching problem: the spin state and spatial gap structure must use the favorable parts while respecting the Fermi surface and exchange symmetry. Antiferromagnetic fluctuations often favor sign-changing singlets in the models reviewed here, but band geometry, energy scales and strong-coupling corrections remain essential. The paper uses several material families to develop that logic. Historical assignments and qualitative design arguments are kept separate from present-day material identification.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Quasiparticle: An electron-like excitation together with its surrounding response cloud.
  • Susceptibility: A response function relating an applied perturbation to an induced density or magnetization.
  • Retardation: A time-delayed response, making the effective interaction frequency dependent.
  • Singlet / triplet: Two-spin states with total spin zero or one.

A descriptive explanation, step by step

Follow the full argument.

Read the full detailed argument12 parts · about 9 min

Part 1

1 · Start with dressed electrons and an induced response

A quasiparticle is an electron together with its response cloud, characterized by effective properties such as mass. The review asks what happens when one quasiparticle perturbs the charge or spin density and a second quasiparticle responds to that perturbation. The resulting interaction can differ greatly from the bare Coulomb or magnetic dipole interaction. Calling it electronic does not mean that all lattice effects or residual short-range repulsion have been removed.

The familiar phonon mechanism illustrates retardation: ionic response develops over time and can support an effective attraction despite short-time repulsion. The authors then construct an analogous charge-and-spin response description using susceptibilities and empirical coupling constants. This is a phenomenological framework for a metallic state with useful quasiparticles. It is most secure away from the immediate loss of that description near a strongly correlated insulating transition.

Part 2

2 · Understand the spin factor before saying magnetic attraction

The induced spin interaction is proportional to −gₘ²(s·s′)χₘ. For two spin-½ particles the singlet and triplet spin products have different signs and magnitudes. Near ferromagnetism the response is strongest at small momentum and varies slowly in real space; triplet pairing can benefit. The odd spatial parity required by exchange symmetry then often favors a p-wave candidate in simple models.

Near antiferromagnetism the response oscillates strongly in real space. The singlet interaction can be repulsive in one spatial region and attractive in another. A pair state that avoids the former while occupying the latter can lower its energy. On a crystal, label these states by point-group symmetry; angular-momentum language is an analogy to the continuum. Antiferromagnetic correlations do not mean that the two electrons must sit in a static Néel lattice while pairing.

Part 3

3 · Read Figure 3 as a matching problem

The square-lattice drawing places one quasiparticle at the origin and depicts the induced interaction at other sites. Its sign alternates spatially. The diagonal dashed lines mark nodes of a proposed d-wave pair state, reducing its overlap with repulsive regions in this commensurate example. Pair probability and signed pair amplitude are different: a probability plot is nonnegative, whereas the gap or amplitude can reverse sign.

The available low-energy states are restricted by the Fermi surface. One cannot choose an arbitrary localized waveform to exploit any attractive region; the permitted momentum components and the temporal response matter. The gap equation expresses this as an eigenvalue problem. This links the review’s real-space intuition with the Moriya paper’s momentum-space calculation. It explains why merely observing a large susceptibility or a negative patch of potential is insufficient to predict Tc.

Part 4

4 · Why dimension, band structure and energy scale change the result

The authors argue that quasi-two-dimensional antiferromagnetic systems can favor singlet pairing when crystal symmetry allows a good match between the interaction and the pair state. They compare cubic CeIn₃ with tetragonal CeRhIn₅, while noting the role of characteristic electronic energy scales. Their Figure 4 multiplies the CeIn₃ Tc curve by three for visibility; ignoring that factor would distort the comparison. Crystal anisotropy is part of a model-guided materials argument, not a guarantee of superconductivity.

Realistic band responses contain multiple momentum structures, which may reinforce or frustrate the proposed pairing state. The triplet channel also carries a smaller isotropic spin factor than the singlet channel. Thus proximity to ferromagnetism need not yield strong superconductivity. The review’s specific Sr₂RuO₄ p-wave assignment reflects a historical model proposal and should not be used as settled material identification. The general symmetry-matching reasoning can be understood without adopting that assignment.

Part 5

5 · Limits of the simple glue picture

The lowest-order induced interaction assumes small enough effective fields for a simple response expansion. When dimensionless coupling is of order one, neglected corrections can become important. The review describes numerical treatments of Gaussian fluctuating fields in which magnetic and density channels receive different corrections. Those results motivate further study; they do not establish an exact, generally applicable hierarchy of all pairing mechanisms.

The authors also contrast itinerant and localized approaches to the cuprates. A response of delocalized quasiparticles is conceptually natural on the more metallic side; exchange among strongly correlated localized degrees of freedom is a different starting point. Explaining their relation is a major problem, not something eliminated by the word glue. This review is useful for constructing hypotheses: specify the response function, coupling, Fermi surface, pair symmetry and controlled approximations, then test the resulting observables.

Part 6

6 · Screening does not mean a uniformly attractive force

The review first teaches electronic response using a test charge. A static screened interaction can remain repulsive everywhere in a simple example, while a moving disturbance produces a response delayed in space and time. The effective interaction is therefore a function of both separation and time, not a single negative constant replacing the Coulomb repulsion. The phonon example is useful because it shows how a delayed attractive part can coexist with an unfavorable short-time interaction.

The magnetic analogy works by letting one quasiparticle polarize the spin density and a second respond to the resulting effective field. The strength of that response is a susceptibility, and the couplings connecting electrons to it must also be specified. The source uses a linear-response construction before discussing extensions. This construction clarifies how a microscopic repulsion can produce favorable components in a collective pairing channel; it does not show that all electrons are attracted at all separations.

The pair state must respect fermionic exchange symmetry and use low-energy momenta near the Fermi surface. It cannot be drawn arbitrarily to sit only on every favorable part of an interaction. A real-space picture of attractive and repulsive sites is thus one piece of an eigenvalue problem involving the band structure and dynamics. That restriction explains why favorable-looking interactions sometimes fail to give a strong superconducting instability.

Part 7

7 · Distinguish the charge channel from the spin channel

In the density channel the product of the two quasiparticle charges has one sign for two electrons. In the magnetic channel the quantum expectation of s·s′ differs between singlet and triplet states. This makes the magnetic interaction's sign depend on the pair's spin state as well as on the susceptibility's spatial oscillation. The singlet and triplet values, −3/4 and +1/4 in dimensionless spin-one-half units, also differ in magnitude. The factor of three is a quantum-spin result, not a generic counting factor for every anisotropic magnet.

Near a ferromagnetic tendency, long-wavelength magnetic response can favor the triplet channel in the simple model. Exchange symmetry then constrains its spatial form to odd parity. Near an antiferromagnetic tendency, short-wavelength sign changes allow an even-parity singlet to avoid unfavorable overlaps using a nonuniform spatial amplitude. On a square lattice the d-wave example has diagonal nodes that match the interaction's structure particularly well.

The density response can also vary in space and support unconventional pairing. The review emphasizes that charge and spin contributions may cooperate or interfere, depending on the band and proposed pair state. A claim of magnetic mediation should therefore specify which response and spin channel are used; a large static magnetic susceptibility alone is not the complete interaction kernel. This is the common principle connecting the intuitive review to Moriya's quantitative pairing eigenvalue.

Part 8

8 · Read the materials comparisons as a conditional design argument

A large susceptibility does not by itself set a large Tc. The energy bandwidth and characteristic fluctuation frequency provide scales, while dimensionality and Fermi-surface geometry determine how well an allowed pair state can use the induced interaction. The review compares cubic CeIn₃ with the more anisotropic CeRhIn₅ because those features change together in a useful family. It argues that lower-dimensional response and tetragonal symmetry can support a better match for antiferromagnetically mediated singlets.

The pressure plot also supplies a practical caution: the CeIn₃ superconducting temperature is multiplied by three for display. Compare the underlying temperatures, not just the apparent vertical height of the curves. Magnetic ordering and superconductivity respond differently to anisotropy, so saying that lower dimensionality suppresses order is incomplete unless the type of order is named.

The review extends this reasoning to electronic energy scales in related f-electron systems and to compounds with two superconducting domes associated with different nearby instabilities. Those examples motivate using pressure and chemistry to explore channels; they do not establish that every dome has one known glue. The material comparisons are qualitative tests and model-guided suggestions. Their strength is in identifying which variables a serious calculation must retain, rather than prescribing a universal recipe for higher Tc.

Part 9

9 · Why realistic bands can frustrate a simple mechanism

A featureless continuum susceptibility has a relatively simple momentum dependence. A crystal band can contain nesting, several pockets or enhanced density of states near particular momenta. Its real-space response then superposes oscillations of different wavelengths. A pair function that matches one component can overlap poorly with another, so increasing one response peak can fail to increase the leading pairing eigenvalue.

The supplied review uses a historical Sr₂RuO₄ p-wave scenario to illustrate this problem. In that model, short-wavelength response and van-Hove-related weight can be poorly matched to the proposed odd-parity pair state. The calculation's lesson is about matching momentum weight and pair symmetry. The material assignment itself belongs to the review's 2007 context and is not presented here as a current determination of its order parameter.

This distinction also clarifies the scarcity of superconductivity near some ferromagnets. In the isotropic model the triplet spin factor is smaller, and unfavorable dynamical effects or competing response structures can matter. Strong spin anisotropy changes the comparison, motivating the discussion of uranium-based ferromagnetic superconductors. A magnetic instability can be continuous or strongly first order, with very different fluctuation spectra. Proximity on a phase diagram is therefore a starting clue; the character of the fluctuations still needs to be measured or calculated.

Part 10

10 · What the response approximation leaves to future theory

The simplest construction averages weak effective fields using response functions. Near strong coupling, the electrons can be altered so substantially that their assumed quasiparticles and Gaussian field distribution become questionable. The authors discuss nonperturbative calculations within specified fluctuating-field models because perturbative corrections can treat magnetic and density channels differently. That is evidence about those models, rather than an exact solution of all electronic superconductors.

For the cuprates, a metallic response-based treatment and a localized exchange-based treatment start from different useful degrees of freedom. They need not be mutually exclusive descriptions of every regime, but relating them quantitatively is hard. The review does not remove that difficulty by renaming fluctuations as glue. A productive reading identifies the initial degrees of freedom, the susceptibility supplied to the calculation, the vertex or coupling, the retained frequency range and the output pairing state. Each item can then be compared with a suitable probe. This makes the paper a framework for constructing and evaluating mechanisms without mistaking an appealing analogy for a controlled prediction.

Part 11

Material criteria are conditional on the interaction model

The review proposes a favorable combination: a relatively high electronic energy scale, a simple band structure, strong quasi-two-dimensional antiferromagnetic correlations and geometry that permits a sign-changing pair wavefunction to avoid strongly repulsive parts of the induced interaction. These conditions are connected. A large interaction by itself need not produce a high transition temperature if it also destroys the quasiparticles required by the calculation. A suitable sign pattern by itself does not supply attractive weight on the available Fermi-surface states.

The itinerant response construction is most natural for delocalized quasiparticles away from a metal–insulator transition. A localized spin model begins from different degrees of freedom and can also favor d-wave pairing. The source associates these approaches more naturally with overdoped and underdoped regimes respectively, while treating their relationship as unresolved. Its proposed criteria should therefore guide conditional comparisons among materials rather than certify one model across the entire copper-oxide phase diagram.

Part 12

Higher-order corrections are a physical uncertainty, not cosmetic detail

The lowest-order induced interaction assumes sufficiently small fluctuating molecular fields. The review warns that relevant dimensionless couplings in candidate materials may instead approach unity, making corrections physically consequential. Numerical averaging over Gaussian fields is discussed as one route beyond the small-amplitude construction. In preliminary calculations, the corrections enhance the magnetic channel and reduce the density channel over the parameter range studied. That difference could matter for selecting a robust pairing mechanism, but it is not a universal theorem at every coupling or filling.

The source also distinguishes failure of the approximate interaction from a completed solution of the resulting pairing problem. Demonstrating that a simple expression misses essential physics does not determine the new transition temperature. The revised treatment remains one of several possible extensions. This is why the review ends with material tuning and theoretical questions rather than a numerical prescription: the useful sign, spin and geometry arguments remain valuable, while a quantitative strong-coupling account requires more than extrapolating the original weak-field expression.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Does superconductivity without phonons mean phonons cannot influence the material?

No. It identifies an electronic pairing mechanism; lattice structure, phonons and other interactions can still affect its strength and spectra.

Why is pair probability insufficient to show d-wave sign reversal?

Probability is the squared modulus and removes the sign or phase of the amplitude.

Why can a stronger susceptibility fail to produce a higher Tc?

Its momentum structure can mismatch the gap, compete with coherence or ordering, or lie outside the approximations that make a simple pairing kernel reliable.

Why is the existence of an attractive region insufficient?

The allowed low-energy momenta lie near the Fermi surface, and the pair state also obeys exchange symmetry and dynamical constraints. Its eigenvalue depends on the full kernel.

Why must probability and pair amplitude be distinguished?

Probability is nonnegative; an amplitude or gap can have opposite signs in different regions. Pairing through repulsive momentum transfer relies on the signed quantity.

What error follows from reading the CeIn₃ curve without its label?

Fig. 4 plots three times its superconducting temperature for visibility. Comparing the displayed height directly with CeRhIn₅ would overstate the underlying Tc.
