Revisiting 63Cu NMR evidence for charge order in superconducting La1.885Sr0.115CuO4

Very short-delay Cu NMR recovers broad, rapidly relaxing spectral weight below the independently established charge-order onset in x = 0.115 LSCO.

The research question: Why did older Cu NQR measurements miss the true onset of charge-influenced domains?

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

Very short-delay Cu NMR recovers broad, rapidly relaxing spectral weight below the independently established charge-order onset in x = 0.115 LSCO. Charge ordering was independently found near 80 K by x-ray work on the same growth batch. Earlier Cu NQR studies had assigned approximately 50 K onset. The source identifies a concrete reason: broad, rapidly decaying echoes below charge onset were lost to experimental dead time or an inappropriate early-time Gaussian extrapolation. The new experiment uses an external field of 9 T along c and pulse separations down to 2 microseconds. The 180-degree pulse lasts about 5 microseconds, so the finite-pulse correction and approximately 2.5 microsecond receiver dead time matter when discussing actual access to fast signals.

Terms to understand before starting
  • LSCO: layered La2-xSrxCuO4; nominal x is Sr per formula unit
  • Local probe: a distribution can matter even when the bulk average barely changes
Wipeout
Loss of detectable nuclear intensity under the chosen echo and frequency conditions.
Echo delay τ
Separation between echo-sequence pulses; detection occurs after approximately 2τ with finite-pulse corrections.
Boltzmann correction
Correction for temperature-dependent equilibrium nuclear polarization.
Charge order
Spatially modulated electronic charge response established with an appropriate structural or electronic probe.
Spin freezing
Spin fluctuations slow enough to appear static within a specified probe window.
Spectral weight
Integrated detected nuclear response after the stated corrections; distinct from charge modulation amplitude.
Broad wings
Wide, often rapidly decaying nuclear spectral components around a narrower surviving peak.

Revise the old onset

Look for: Independent x-ray onset near 80 K agrees with fast-echo anomalies.

Wing nuclei relax longitudinally about five times faster than main-peak survivors. Longer-delay measurements therefore preferentially record the less affected regions, whose 1/(T1T) retains a Curie-Weiss form with θ about 130 ± 15 K. Short-delay measurements show the extra fast response. Above charge onset the echo is described by a relaxation-induced exponential times a Gaussian indirect-coupling term. Below onset the full-time fit fails because the fast component alters early decay; only an appropriately short interval can be analyzed consistently.

The old approximately 50 K wipeout onset reflected measurement selection.

Picture 5 / 8

A new shape needs a new component

Look for: At high temperature, changing the delay mainly scales the magnitude of a common nearly Gaussian spectrum.

At high temperature, changing the delay mainly scales the magnitude of a common nearly Gaussian spectrum. That behavior is compatible with a comparatively uniform decay over the accessible spectral region. At lower temperature, shortening the delay does something qualitatively different: it reveals broad wings around the main peak. A common multiplicative correction to a narrow spectrum cannot reproduce a new component with a different decay time.

The new shape is therefore the crucial evidence that the old measurement selected survivors rather than merely undercounting an otherwise identical line.

Picture 6 / 8

Separate population from order amplitude

Look for: The short-delay integrated signal is approximately conserved across charge onset in the stated temperature interval.

The short-delay integrated signal is approximately conserved across charge onset in the stated temperature interval. At the same time, the narrow normally behaving peak loses relative weight and the broad wings gain it. The natural interpretation is redistribution between differently responding nuclear environments. The inferred normal and wing fractions summarize that redistribution after extrapolation and correction. They are not the charge modulation amplitude, and a complement such as one minus the normal fraction does not turn them into a structural order parameter.

A source population is being assigned by its nuclear response, not directly imaged as a sharply bounded real-space domain.

Part 1

A controlled revisit of the earlier interpretation

Part 2

A nearly fixed mean can hide a changing distribution

For field along c, second-order quadrupolar shifts are very small and on-site and transferred uniform spin hyperfine fields nearly cancel. The central frequency therefore changes little even though magnetism changes locally. At 135 K the spectra at different delays have nearly identical Gaussian shape, with half width about 278 ± 18 kHz, differing mainly in overall decay. At 40 K, delays of 2 to 4 microseconds reveal broad wings near 100.5 and 104.5 MHz around the approximately 102.7 MHz main peak. The wings vanish for longer delays and can have T2 near 10.5 microseconds. A fit to only the narrow main peak systematically misses their weight. This is direct evidence of a distribution of local response and decay times.

PDF p. 4 · PDF p. 5

Part 3

What the intensity fractions mean

After Boltzmann correction, the integrated short-delay spectrum conserves total weight from 295 K to about 40 K across charge onset. The missing narrow-peak weight moves into the broad wings. The authors estimate the normally behaving fraction by extrapolating the longer-delay main component to zero delay, then define IWing = 1 − INormal. These fractions characterize spectral populations, interpreted as differently influenced regions, rather than the size of the electronic charge modulation. Below and near Tc, radio-frequency shielding and further spin slowing remove accessibility, so the lower-temperature fraction comparison needs caution. The main-peak fraction is still about one-third near 30 K, while other probes identify a smaller static magnetic fraction. The experiment does not establish simple macroscopic phase separation.

Part 4

Relaxation, linewidth and the echo model

Wing nuclei relax longitudinally about five times faster than main-peak survivors. Longer-delay measurements therefore preferentially record the less affected regions, whose 1/(T1T) retains a Curie-Weiss form with θ about 130 ± 15 K. Short-delay measurements show the extra fast response. Above charge onset the echo is described by a relaxation-induced exponential times a Gaussian indirect-coupling term. Below onset the full-time fit fails because the fast component alters early decay; only an appropriately short interval can be analyzed consistently. Field-dependent linewidth proportional to field favors a magnetic distribution over a dominant second-order quadrupolar spread, which would scale inversely with field. Increased low-frequency magnetism need not imply a large resolved NQR frequency modulation.

Part 5

What changes in the scientific interpretation

The revised onset agrees with charge order near 80 K; earlier recovery procedures discarded the most affected nuclei. At dopings below approximately 0.10, the paper warns that wipeout onset can instead reflect localization, so the old extrapolation of stripe onset across the entire underdoped range is unsafe. The spin system slows gradually: neutrons detect apparently static order near 30 K on a much faster time window, and muons see freezing at lower temperatures. Those differences are compatible with heterogeneous glassy dynamics. The source fits an effective low-energy correlation scale around 40 K in the charge-influenced response, far below the parent exchange scale, but it is not a microscopic exchange J or a universally valid asymptotic fit. Appendices verify recovery forms and compare field orientations. The secure inference is heterogeneous redistribution of Cu NMR response, with a quantitatively demonstrated visibility bias.

Part 6

How to compare the evidence

A useful thought experiment is to repeat the identical frequency sweep with a longer echo delay. The fast-decaying wing nuclei now contribute much less, so the spectrum appears narrower and its integrated intensity falls even if the shortest-delay experiment still finds the full corrected weight. This is a measurement-window effect, not the removal of Cu atoms. Extrapolating the surviving narrow component back toward zero delay defines INormal, while the remaining corrected weight defines IWing. These weights describe differently relaxing environments and require intensity normalization; they do not directly measure the sinusoidal charge-density amplitude. The source ties the new wings to independently established charge ordering in the same specimen, which is stronger evidence than applying a universal wipeout rule to every low-doping sample.

Part 7

See the experiment as a visibility filter

A spin echo is recorded only after the excitation pulses and the receiver recovery interval. Nuclei whose transverse signal decays before that time can be present physically but absent experimentally. A long pulse separation therefore filters out fast-decaying environments more strongly than a short one. A broad frequency distribution creates another filter: a narrow sweep or excitation bandwidth may miss nuclei outside the chosen window. The article's intensity is consequently an operational quantity measured under specified pulse and spectral conditions. Counting the visible area without accounting for those conditions can confuse redistribution of response with a change in the number of nuclei.

Temperature corrections are equally important. The equilibrium nuclear polarization changes with temperature, and the echo amplitude changes with decay between excitation and detection. A raw rising signal on cooling could reflect greater Boltzmann polarization, while a falling signal could combine that increase with a much stronger loss of visibility. The source corrects for those effects before discussing missing spectral weight. The correction is still limited by what is actually observed: an extrapolation based only on slow surviving echoes cannot reconstruct an unobserved, differently decaying population without additional measurements. This is why shortening the delay or expanding the frequency sweep can alter the scientific conclusion rather than merely improve the appearance of a spectrum.

Part 8

Charge order and spin freezing have different observables

A charge modulation changes the electronic density and potentially the local electric gradient. Slowing spin fluctuations alter the local magnetic response and nuclear relaxation. In correlated materials these changes can influence one another, but their measured onset temperatures need not coincide. Diffraction supplies a momentum-specific order signature under its time and spatial windows; nuclear intensity supplies accessibility under an echo protocol; a muon supplies a local magnetic time window. A comparison is strongest when each quantity is kept in its own units and interpreted through its own measurement, rather than labeling every anomaly the same order parameter.

Glassy slowing makes this distinction especially important. As a distribution of electronic fluctuation rates shifts to slower values, different instruments can declare the system effectively static at different temperatures. A nuclear experiment can even lose its echo when fluctuations enter an unfavorable relaxation window before recovering a broader low-temperature signal elsewhere. Such behavior is not a simple monotonic meter of the charge amplitude. Independent diffraction evidence can anchor a charge-order onset, while local nuclear measurements describe how much response becomes strongly affected and how heterogeneous that influence is. The source's historical interpretation, later methodological revision and unresolved spatial questions should therefore be read together.

Part 9

Why changing delay changes the spectral shape

At high temperature, changing the delay mainly scales the magnitude of a common nearly Gaussian spectrum. That behavior is compatible with a comparatively uniform decay over the accessible spectral region. At lower temperature, shortening the delay does something qualitatively different: it reveals broad wings around the main peak. A common multiplicative correction to a narrow spectrum cannot reproduce a new component with a different decay time. The new shape is therefore the crucial evidence that the old measurement selected survivors rather than merely undercounting an otherwise identical line.

This also explains why a fitted main-peak width and a normal-looking long-delay recovery can coexist with stronger magnetism elsewhere. The broad-wing nuclei decay more rapidly and relax longitudinally faster; the long-delay experiment filters them out before recovery is analyzed. The nearly unchanged average central frequency does not rescue a uniform account, because the c-axis uniform spin coupling nearly cancels while local field variations can still broaden the response. The paper's finite-pulse and dead-time corrections are thus connected directly to its physical conclusion. They determine access to the component that reveals the heterogeneous charge-influenced spin response.

Part 10

Read conserved weight and inferred fractions carefully

The short-delay integrated signal is approximately conserved across charge onset in the stated temperature interval. At the same time, the narrow normally behaving peak loses relative weight and the broad wings gain it. The natural interpretation is redistribution between differently responding nuclear environments. The inferred normal and wing fractions summarize that redistribution after extrapolation and correction. They are not the charge modulation amplitude, and a complement such as one minus the normal fraction does not turn them into a structural order parameter. A source population is being assigned by its nuclear response, not directly imaged as a sharply bounded real-space domain.

The low-temperature limit changes the conditions again. Superconducting radio-frequency shielding and further spin slowing compromise accessibility, so apparent conservation above that regime cannot simply be extrapolated through it. Independent x-ray evidence anchors the charge onset near 80 K, while the delayed historical wipeout onset near 50 K reflects missed fast signal. Different apparent spin-freezing temperatures in neutron and muon measurements then add time-window information rather than contradicting the charge onset. The paper also warns that low-doping wipeout can follow localization. These distinctions prevent a useful correction in one crystal from becoming an unjustified universal stripe boundary throughout LSCO.

Symbols and units: τ: pulse separation; M0: extrapolated echo; T2R: exponential relaxation contribution; T2G: Gaussian indirect-coupling time.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Why is nearly unchanged Knight shift compatible with broad wings?

The uniform c-axis spin hyperfine field nearly cancels, while a heterogeneous local field distribution can broaden the line.

Is IWing the amplitude of charge order?

No. It is complementary spectral weight assigned to the more strongly charge-influenced response.

Why can a long-delay T1 look normal?

The delay selects the slowly decaying, less strongly affected nuclei.

Why cannot one common decay factor recover the low-temperature full spectrum?

The broad wings have a different decay behavior and alter the shape when delay is shortened.

What does conserved short-delay weight establish in its stated interval?

Visible spectral weight is redistributed between narrow and broad response rather than simply vanishing under that protocol.

Why does the inferred fraction become harder to interpret near Tc?

Radio-frequency shielding and further slowing change accessibility, so the higher-temperature corrections and conservation are insufficient there.
