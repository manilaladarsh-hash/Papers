Filling Dependence of Electronic Properties on the Verge of Metal-Mott-Insulator Transitions in Sr1−xLaxTiO3

The supplied 1993 titanate study combines Hall, heat-capacity, susceptibility and resistivity evidence for increasing effective mass near a filling-controlled Mott boundary.

The research question: Does the metallic titanate approach its insulating endpoint by losing carriers or by making its carriers strongly renormalized?

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

Changing La content in a titanate changes how full its electronic band is. Near the d1 Mott-insulating parent, this paper finds that metallic carrier counting remains fairly simple while heat capacity, spin response and temperature-dependent resistance grow strongly. The authors interpret that combination as increasingly heavy, renormalized quasiparticles rather than a metal losing nearly all its carriers. A quadratic resistivity law and a near-constant Wilson ratio can therefore coexist with strong interactions. Careful oxygen and La chemistry are essential near the boundary, and a deficient sample labeled 1* is distinct from the true insulator. The result concerns the measured metallic regime; an infinite endpoint mass is an extrapolation, and the later NMR paper supplies a local fluctuation test.

A descriptive explanation, step by step

Follow the full argument.

Part 1

Translate the composition before comparing papers

Tokura and colleagues write Sr1−xLaxTiO3, making x the La concentration and nominal Ti 3d electron filling. At x = 0 the end member is SrTiO3 with nominal d0 Ti4+; at x = 1 it is LaTiO3 with d1 Ti3+. The latter is an antiferromagnetic Mott-Hubbard insulator when stoichiometry is controlled carefully. Replacing some La with Sr removes electrons and can produce a metal near x around 0.95. Source 45 describes the same family as La1−xSrxTiO3, reversing the meaning of x. Consequently a plot that rises as x approaches one here corresponds to approaching small Sr fraction there. This elementary translation prevents a false disagreement about which direction approaches the insulating state or which substitution introduces holes relative to LaTiO3.

Part 2

Why sample chemistry is part of the argument

Close to the insulating endpoint, small La deficiency or oxygen off-stoichiometry can change the electronic state. The authors therefore emphasize reducing growth conditions and composition checks. Their nearly stoichiometric x = 1 sample is insulating with antiferromagnetic ordering around 138 K, whereas x = 0.95 is metallic. A nominally x = 1 sample labeled 1* in a later plot is barely metallic, plausibly because of a small La deficiency. These are not interchangeable endpoints. The clean comparison asks how properties vary among metallic samples as filling increases, with a separately established insulating reference. Far toward low carrier density, localization effects complicate the simplest metallic picture. The paper's Fermi-liquid argument is therefore restricted to the investigated metallic regime, not every composition from zero to one.

Part 3

The Hall result separates count from renormalization

The metallic samples have negative Hall coefficients with weak temperature dependence in the reported range. The magnitude of the inverse Hall coefficient follows the filling reasonably well, consistent with about one extra electron per La substituted for Sr. Within the paper's carrier-count interpretation, the metal near x = 1 retains a large electron Fermi surface rather than a tiny Fermi surface counting only doped holes. This contrasts with familiar hole-doped cuprate behavior discussed by the authors. A Hall coefficient is nevertheless a transport observable and need not equal exactly −1/(ne) in every multiband or strongly anisotropic metal. The strength of this work is the agreement with the simple count in these samples, used together with other probes rather than elevated to a universal rule for correlated systems.

Part 4

Heat capacity and susceptibility reveal an enhanced scale

The low-temperature electronic heat-capacity coefficient γ increases strongly toward the insulating endpoint. The spin susceptibility, after subtracting core diamagnetism, shows a parallel filling dependence and is approximately Pauli-like in the metallic state. Their ratio gives a Wilson ratio near two over the reported range. In the authors' Fermi-liquid analysis, this approximately constant ratio argues against a progressively increasing extra Stoner factor and favors a growing quasiparticle mass. A noninteracting spherical-band reference would give a much gentler filling dependence, proportional to x to the one-third power. The distinction is not that interactions disappear because the curves look Pauli-like: a Fermi liquid can have very heavy quasiparticles. The enhancement is inferred from several coefficients whose common dependence is more informative than any one alone.

Part 5

Transport cross-checks the thermodynamic interpretation

The metallic resistivity is well described over substantial reported ranges by ρ = ρ0 + AT². Its coefficient A rises conspicuously as the insulating endpoint is approached. Plotting A against γ² produces an approximate proportionality similar to the empirical comparison made for heavy-fermion metals. This supports an interaction-driven increase of scattering and mass scales, although the relation is empirical and depends on the material and conventions. The conclusion proposes that the Mott approach is characterized by critical mass enhancement, rather than primarily by disappearance of carriers. No experiment measures an infinite mass in the insulating sample, and the source itself notes that the enhancement is less steep than some low-dimensional theoretical predictions. The comparison to cuprates is an argument about dimensionality and charge-gap character, not a demonstration that the same microscopic physics describes all oxides.

Part 6

A filling control changes charge count without replacing the Ti network

Replacing divalent Sr with trivalent La adds nominal electrons while preserving the basic perovskite Ti–O network. It is a useful way to change band filling rather than directly alloy the magnetic titanium sublattice. At the d1 endpoint, however, very small oxygen or La errors can remove enough electrons to turn the insulator into a metal. Growth in reducing conditions and chemical checks are consequently part of the physics argument. The stoichiometric insulating endpoint calibrates what the filling-controlled metallic samples are approaching.

The paper distinguishes that endpoint from a nominally x = 1 specimen marked 1*, whose small deficiency leaves it barely metallic. Comparing its metallic coefficient with the insulating endpoint as if they were identical would create a false contradiction. At the other end, very low electron density can expose impurity localization and also limit a simple Fermi-liquid description. The systematic result applies to the studied metallic region between those complications. Its composition notation counts La and electron filling; the later NMR notation counts complementary Sr and holes relative to LaTiO3.

Part 7

Resistivity separates an intercept from an interaction-sensitive slope

Plotting resistivity minus its residual part against temperature squared turns a quadratic law into a slope comparison. The residual term represents the extrapolated zero-temperature transport background, while A records the temperature-dependent contribution in the fitted range. Samples with comparable residual resistivity can still have very different A. The source uses that observation to argue that the growing coefficient reflects filling-dependent electronic renormalization rather than merely more static scattering from imperfect specimens. The broad reported quadratic ranges support a Fermi-liquid organization of these metallic data.

The argument does not identify electron–electron scattering solely because a plot looks straight. It gains force from the accompanying heat capacity and susceptibility and from the approximate A-versus-γ-squared relation. Each coefficient has its own units and extraction procedure: A is resistivity per kelvin squared, while γ is energy per mole per kelvin squared. Squaring γ changes its units, and the proportionality constant belongs to an empirical comparison. A large A is therefore compatible with a highly interacting metal, even when its temperature exponent resembles the conventional Fermi-liquid form.

Part 8

Hall count and thermodynamic weight need not scale alike

The negative Hall coefficient identifies an electron-like response under the source's interpretation. Its inverse magnitude roughly follows the electrons introduced by La substitution, with little temperature dependence in the compared measurements. Approaching the Mott edge does not simply drive that inferred carrier count to zero. The authors interpret this as a large electron Fermi surface consistent with the filling, in contrast to a picture that counts only the small number of holes removed from the d1 insulator. It remains a transport-based count within the examined system, rather than an exact identity for every correlated multiband metal.

Heat capacity asks a different question: how much low-energy electronic entropy is available per small temperature increase. The intercept of electronic C/T versus T squared yields γ after the low-temperature contributions are separated. Its much stronger enhancement than the simple x-to-the-one-third band reference points toward a renormalized DOS. Spin susceptibility rises in parallel, so their Wilson ratio stays near two rather than acquiring an additional divergent factor. Several probes can therefore indicate heavier electronic states while the Hall count remains relatively ordinary. There is no contradiction between retaining carriers and making them increasingly difficult to move coherently.

Part 9

A critical interpretation is an extrapolation from the metal

The study uses Fermi-liquid relations to associate enhanced γ and susceptibility with a growing effective mass and relates transport scattering to that renormalization. It does not measure an infinite quasiparticle mass inside the insulating endpoint, where the metallic reference no longer applies. The observed enhancement is explicitly less steep than some theoretical limiting predictions discussed by the authors. That difference is scientifically relevant rather than a reason to draw an invented divergence through the final data point.

The A-versus-γ-squared plot offers an empirical cross-check with heavy-fermion metals, whose absolute heat-capacity coefficients can be much larger. Similar scaling suggests shared renormalized-electron reasoning without proving identical microscopic orbitals or fluctuations. The comparison with cuprates is also conditional. Their parents have different charge-gap character and their motion is more two-dimensional, so the same filling control need not yield the same metallic state. The later titanium/lanthanum NMR experiment addresses the unresolved microscopic fluctuation question with different site weights. Together the two papers form a sequence of bulk evidence followed by a local test, while their complementary definitions of x must be translated before combining any plots.

What the evidence supports—and where it stops

  • Historical 1993 study of specific metallic samples, not a current universal Mott phase diagram.
  • Hall carrier counting and mass extraction use a Fermi-liquid interpretation; divergent mass at the exact insulating endpoint is an extrapolation.
  • Small stoichiometry changes are important near the boundary; x convention differs from source 45.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Is x the Sr fraction in this paper?

No. It is the La fraction in Sr1−xLaxTiO3, complementary to source 45's x.

Why distinguish the sample labeled 1*?

Its slight nonstoichiometry makes it barely metallic, unlike the nearly stoichiometric insulating endpoint.

What does an approximately constant Wilson ratio imply here?

The spin and heat-capacity enhancements track each other; the authors do not require a separately growing Stoner enhancement.

What does a larger A at the same T² exponent establish?

A stronger temperature-dependent transport contribution; together with other probes it supports renormalization without changing the quadratic exponent.

Why is a large Hall count compatible with enhanced γ?

Counting carriers and measuring their low-energy DOS weight are different questions; interactions can make retained carriers heavier.

Is the reported A/γ² relation exact for all metals?

No. It is an approximate empirical cross-check with its own units and material assumptions.
