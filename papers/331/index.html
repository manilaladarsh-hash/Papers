Magnetic properties and electronic conduction of superconducting La2−xSrxCuO4

The research question: How do sample purity, hole doping and Zn substitution change susceptibility scaling and its relation to electrical resistance?

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

The supplied 1994 LSCO study separates a scaled host response from Curie tails, finds stronger low-temperature suppression than the earlier Johnston curve, and compares its crossover with transport without treating their doping dependence as identical. They stress that even nominally nonmagnetic impurities on Cu sites can induce host moments and a Curie contribution. High Tc and the absence of a residual low-temperature T-linear heat-capacity term near optimal doping are cited as indicators of clean samples, rather than direct atom-by-atom purity maps. Their own normal-state SQUID measurements use 10 kOe up to 550 K; resistivity extends to roughly 820 K. Data reaching 1000 K in the susceptibility comparisons come from cited Yoshizaki measurements. Low-temperature diamagnetic transitions use a separate low-field protocol. In normal-state superconducting-sample comparisons they omit data below 50 K to avoid superconducting fluctuation effects.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Uniform susceptibility: static response at q=0
  • Curie response: C/T from approximately free local moments
  • Broad maximum: crossover feature, not necessarily a phase transition
  • Knight shift: local hyperfine-weighted uniform spin response
  • Transport crossover: deviation from a chosen high-temperature resistivity trend
Uniform susceptibility χ
Static response to a spatially uniform magnetic field.
Curie term C/T
Inverse-temperature contribution used to describe an effective moment response.
Van Vleck response
Orbital magnetic susceptibility contribution often approximately temperature independent.
Scaled host response
Temperature-dependent contribution compared after background and normalization choices.
Tmax
Observed or fitted temperature of the specified susceptibility maximum.
Scaling collapse
Agreement of normalized shapes after permitted offset, amplitude and horizontal rescaling.
Effective exchange
Magnetic scale inferred through a particular model and Hamiltonian convention.

A descriptive explanation, step by step

Follow the full argument.

Part 1

High purity changes what can be inferred

Nakano and colleagues measure ceramic LSCO susceptibility and resistivity over a wide Sr range and examine Zn substitution at x=.16 and.22. They stress that even nominally nonmagnetic impurities on Cu sites can induce host moments and a Curie contribution. High Tc and the absence of a residual low-temperature T-linear heat-capacity term near optimal doping are cited as indicators of clean samples, rather than direct atom-by-atom purity maps. Their own normal-state SQUID measurements use 10 kOe up to 550 K; resistivity extends to roughly 820 K. Data reaching 1000 K in the susceptibility comparisons come from cited Yoshizaki measurements. Low-temperature diamagnetic transitions use a separate low-field protocol. In normal-state superconducting-sample comparisons they omit data below 50 K to avoid superconducting fluctuation effects. This makes the eventual zero-temperature host limit an extrapolation, not a directly observed normal state.

PDF p. 1 · PDF p. 2 · PDF p. 3 · PDF p. 4 · PDF p. 5

Part 2

The lightly doped upturn has a separate origin

For small x, the measured susceptibility contains a broad upturn linked to antisymmetric exchange in the orthorhombic crystal. That contribution is distinct from the small, steep low-temperature Curie tail, which the authors regard as extrinsic in this dilute-Sr regime because its coefficient depends on preparation. The structural distortion enables a coupling between uniform response and staggered AF correlations. The upturn disappears near x=.05 although the orthorhombic distortion has decreased only modestly. The paper therefore interprets the reduction primarily through weakened two-dimensional AF correlations rather than loss of orthorhombicity alone. This is an inference within its exchange model. Disappearance of this particular contribution does not mean that every AF correlation at every momentum is zero throughout the superconducting compositions. Nor should the word ferromagnetic used for the parent upturn be confused with an independently established bulk ferromagnetic phase.

PDF p. 1 · PDF p. 2 · PDF p. 3

Part 3

Scaling survives, but the low-temperature shape changes

For samples with a broad maximum, subtracting a constant offset and rescaling by Tmax and the host maximum collapses the response onto a common curve. At x around.20 and above an added C/T term is needed; its coefficient increases with further Sr doping. This piece must be removed before identifying the host curve. The new normalized host response extrapolates below 20% of its maximum at low temperature, compared with roughly 40% in Johnston's earlier AF-like analysis. The authors attribute part of the earlier difference to impurity-induced moments and suggest an additional spin-singlet tendency to explain suppression beyond their Heisenberg comparison. That suggestion is distinct from the measured corrected curve. Oxygen Knight-shift data support the extracted temperature dependence using assumed hyperfine coupling and shift offsets; the microscopic check constrains the separation but is not an assumption-free absolute susceptibility calibration.

Part 4

Transport agrees over one range and separates over another

The resistivity is compared with a high-temperature linear extrapolation. Its downward deviation defines T*. For approximately.09<x<.16 this crossover agrees well with susceptibility Tmax. At smaller x, cited susceptibility data show a stronger fall below Tb without a visible maximum even up to 1000 K; the transport crossover then corresponds to Tb rather than an invented peak. These correlated scales suggest a relation between magnetic response and scattering. They do not identify one unique microscopic process. Near optimal doping the downward resistivity deviation becomes weak or unclear while susceptibility still decreases on cooling. More overdoped resistivity is curved even at high temperatures. The paper explicitly says the LSCO relation is more complicated than the YBCO analogy it discusses. A single boundary drawn through all these regimes would conceal its most useful qualification.

Part 5

Zn changes the balance, not just the total signal

For Zn-substituted x=.16 samples, increasing Zn lowers Tc, increases the fitted Curie coefficient and reduces both Tmax and the scaled host amplitude. The corresponding changes at x=.22 are weaker. An especially useful comparison holds Tmax approximately similar between a Zn-substituted sample and a differently Sr-doped unsubstituted sample: their amplitudes still differ substantially. Matching a crossover temperature therefore does not guarantee that the same amount of material contributes to the scaled magnetic response. The authors propose that regions near Zn switch toward Curie-like behavior while superconductivity is locally suppressed, using thermodynamic work as supporting context. This spatial picture remains inferred. It links naturally to the separate Y124 recovery analysis, but the bulk LSCO Curie coefficient and Y124's host/impurity NQR fit parameters are different observables and should not be assigned one numerical value.

Part 6

The total susceptibility is a sum of physically different responses

The measured static susceptibility can include a temperature-independent background, a broad temperature-dependent host response and a low-temperature Curie contribution. Those terms can overlap: a growing C/T tail can hide a falling host curve. A visible upturn in total χ therefore does not prove that the entire Cu spin system has acquired the same response. The article separates contributions using temperature range, composition and additional comparisons. The fitted separation is physically motivated but must retain its assumptions about core, orbital and impurity backgrounds.

A constant fitted term is particularly easy to overinterpret. Core diamagnetism, Van Vleck response and a carrier-associated contribution can all be temperature independent over a range. A curve collapse alone cannot determine their absolute decomposition unless a reference or constraint fixes the additive ambiguity. Likewise, a Curie constant summarizes an effective moment population under a model; it is not a spatial image or chemical identification. The source's stronger conclusions require comparisons with other observables and preparation controls. Reading those stages separately makes the susceptibility a rich electronic constraint without turning every fitted component into an independently measured microscopic subsystem.

Part 7

Scaling tests shape after allowing offset, height and temperature changes

To compare curves with different maxima, the analysis subtracts an offset, divides by a characteristic amplitude and rescales temperature by a characteristic temperature. Raw curves can therefore differ greatly while their normalized shapes agree. The nontrivial result is the remaining common shape after those allowed changes, not identical susceptibility or identical exchange at every composition. A fitted maximum outside the observed interval adds another assumption: the scaling model infers its location rather than the experiment directly measuring a peak there. Normalization should be explained before the word universal is used.

The physical interpretation then depends on the comparison model. A square-lattice spin calculation can relate a characteristic temperature and amplitude to effective magnetic scales, but applying it to doped carriers involves assumptions beyond a successful collapse. Low-temperature extrapolations also require care when superconductivity or a Curie tail hides the normal host response. Different supplied studies can agree on part of the normalized shape while disagreeing on its low-temperature limit or on the background removal. Keeping those disagreements visible is useful: it reveals which observations are robust and which deductions depend on purity, subtraction or model choice. A crossover temperature correlated with transport still does not by itself determine the common microscopic cause.

Part 8

A rising total and a falling host can coexist

The apparent contradiction disappears once the fitted components are kept separate. A C/T contribution increases on cooling while the scaled host response decreases. Their sum can rise, fall or conceal a broad maximum depending on their relative amplitudes. The source uses this decomposition both for overdoped Sr specimens and for Zn-substituted samples. A total upturn therefore does not justify assigning a rising susceptibility to every Cu environment. Conversely, subtracting a convenient Curie term does not by itself prove the resulting host curve: the temperature interval, background assumptions and Knight-shift comparison provide the additional constraints.

The revised low-temperature host limit is extrapolated because superconducting and fluctuation-contaminated data are excluded from the normal-state analysis. Its difference from the earlier Johnston curve is important evidence about purity and decomposition, not a direct T = 0 normal-state measurement. The proposed extra singlet tendency is a microscopic interpretation of the stronger reduction. The magnetic upturn in dilute Sr samples also contains an antisymmetric-exchange-related component distinct from the steep extrinsic Curie tail. Collapsing those contributions into one ferromagnetic label would ignore the article's deliberate separation of physical mechanisms.

Part 9

Matching a temperature scale does not match the participating response

The Zn comparison changes more than the total low-temperature tail. It lowers Tc, changes the characteristic host temperature and reduces the host amplitude while increasing the fitted Curie contribution, with stronger effects in one Sr composition than the other. Comparing specimens with similar Tmax but different amplitude shows that a common crossover scale does not guarantee the same amount of scaled host response. The authors interpret this as a redistribution of correlated response near Zn, linked with local superconducting suppression, while the spatial region picture remains inferred rather than imaged.

The transport comparison has a similar restricted logic. A high-temperature linear extrapolation defines the resistivity departure, and over part of the underdoped range its scale agrees with the susceptibility crossover. At lower doping a different magnetic scale replaces a nonexistent observed maximum; near optimal doping the resistivity departure becomes unclear while the host still falls. A single common line through all compositions would hide those distinctions. The source therefore supplies a useful correlation and useful exceptions. Its oxygen Knight-shift caption inconsistency also remains documented rather than being interpreted as a second independent dataset.

Essential equations, meaning and assumptions

χ(T) = χ0 + χF,max F(T/Tmax) + C/T

Symbols and units: χ0: fitted temperature-independent offset; χF,max: maximum of the scaled host component; Tmax: host maximum temperature; C: Curie coefficient; F(1)=1.

Physical meaning: Teaching form of the source's decomposition for overdoped or Zn-substituted samples. C/T is separated before the host is normalized; not every sample needs the same C.

u = T/Tmax; f(u) = χF(T)/χF,max

Symbols and units: u: reduced temperature; f: reduced host susceptibility after constant and applicable Curie terms have been removed.

Physical meaning: A curve collapse compares shape after background and amplitude changes. The source's extrapolated low-T f is below.20 rather than the earlier roughly.40.

Δρ(T) = ρ(T) − (aT + b); Δρ < 0 below the identified T*

Symbols and units: ρ: measured resistivity; aT+b: high-temperature linear comparison; T*: temperature where the downward deviation becomes evident.

Physical meaning: A teaching definition of the plotted transport comparison. Fit window and regime matter, and the deviation becomes unclear near optimal doping even while χ changes.

Symbols and units: Kspin: spin Knight shift; Ahf: hyperfine coupling; χspin: uniform spin response after offset assumptions.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Is the below 20% low-temperature normalized value a direct T=0 normal-state measurement?

No. It is an extrapolation of the fitted host component, with low-T superconducting/fluctuation regions excluded.

Does T* track Tmax equally at every doping?

No. At low doping the comparison uses Tb, and near optimal doping the resistivity deviation weakens while χ still falls.

Why can nominally spinless Zn produce C/T?

The substitution can induce magnetic moments in the correlated host environment.

Does disappearance of the dilute-Sr antisymmetric-exchange upturn prove all AF correlations vanish?

No. It constrains that contribution under the paper's model, not the entire momentum-dependent response.

Can total χ rise while the host χF falls?

Yes. The increasing C/T contribution can dominate the sum on cooling.

What does matching Tmax but differing amplitude show?

A shared crossover temperature does not guarantee the same participating host response or spatially affected fraction.

Why not draw one common magnetic/transport boundary at every x?

The source reports range-specific agreement and clear exceptions, including use of Tb and weak near-optimal resistivity departures.
