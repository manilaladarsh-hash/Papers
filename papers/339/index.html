YbRh2Si2: Spin Fluctuations in the Vicinity of a Quantum Critical Point at Low Magnetic Field

The supplied 2002 Si NMR study finds dominant uniform fluctuations at higher fields and an extra finite-q contribution at its lowest field, inferred from relaxation growing after the Knight shift saturates.

The research question: What spin-fluctuation character accompanies field-tuned non-Fermi-liquid behavior in YbRh2Si2?

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

Near a very low-field magnetic boundary, silicon NMR asks both how strongly the electrons respond uniformly and how strongly they fluctuate. In YbRh₂Si₂ the calibrated hyperfine coupling is negative, so a more negative shift means a larger positive spin response. At larger fields, both observables saturate and the source finds predominantly uniform ferromagnetic-like fluctuations in a heavy Fermi liquid. At the lowest measured field, the shift saturates while relaxation keeps growing, indicating an added finite-wavevector dynamic component. The authors associate that component with nearby antiferromagnetic tendencies and propose competition between them. They do not directly reach the critical field or map momentum-space peaks, and request an independent neutron test.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Knight shift: A calibrated nuclear-frequency response to the uniform electronic spin susceptibility.
  • Hyperfine coupling: The connection between an electronic magnetic response and the local nuclear field.
  • Heavy Fermi liquid: A low-temperature quasiparticle regime with strongly enhanced electronic scales.
  • Kondo scale: A characteristic crossover from local-moment behavior toward coherent heavy-electron dynamics.

A descriptive explanation, step by step

Follow the full argument.

Part 1

Why a clean low-field material is useful

The paper studies stoichiometric YbRh2Si2 near a very low-temperature magnetic boundary. Earlier bulk measurements cited in the source found ordering near 70 mK at zero field and restoration of Landau-Fermi-liquid behavior with increasing field. Unlike chemical substitution, field tuning need not add random dopant environments, making this a useful system for examining intrinsic critical fluctuations. The Si NMR measurements span roughly 1.5–25 kOe and use powdered single crystals that align with their ab planes parallel to the field because the susceptibility is strongly anisotropic. The relevant field direction is therefore essential. The source discusses an AF critical field near 0.5 kOe, but its own lowest NMR field is higher. It probes the vicinity and extrapolates the critical interpretation rather than measuring directly at that endpoint.

Part 2

Shift and relaxation contain different information

The Knight shift is proportional to the uniform static susceptibility, apart from orbital contributions. The K-versus-bulk-susceptibility plot gives a negative hyperfine coupling of about−727 Oe per Bohr magneton, so the increasingly negative shift reflects an increasing positive spin response. Relaxation divided by temperature samples low-frequency spin fluctuations over momentum space, weighted by the hyperfine coupling. The analysis subtracts the small relaxation coefficient of nonmagnetic LuRh2Si2 to isolate a Yb4f contribution. This reference subtraction and the anisotropic-to-isotropic shift estimate are assumptions that enter later ratios. A large rate alone does not identify AF fluctuations; it must be compared with how the uniform response and the probe weights behave. The same nucleus can consequently reveal a change of fluctuation character through a mismatch between two observables.

Part 3

The higher-field regime is not simply an AF QCP signature

For fields of2.5 kOe and above, the shift and relaxation coefficient both become approximately temperature independent below a field-dependent crossover temperature. The paper interprets that regime as a field-induced Landau Fermi liquid. The relaxation-to-shift-squared ratio is around one-tenth of its free-electron reference in the reported analysis. With the stated isotropic hyperfine assumption, such a small ratio indicates enhanced q≈0, ferromagnetic-like fluctuations rather than a dominant AF peak far from zero momentum. The observed approximately linear relation between 1/(T1T) and the spin shift is also compared with nearly ferromagnetic metals. Calling the entire experiment an 'AF fluctuation measurement' would therefore discard a central finding: strong uniform fluctuations persist over much of the field range even though the nearby zero-field ordered phase is antiferromagnetic.

Part 4

The low-field mismatch isolates an extra contribution

At the lowest measured field,1.5 kOe, the Knight shift levels off below about 200 mK while the 4f relaxation coefficient continues growing down to50 mK, approximately as T to the minus one-half over an extended range. If only the same uniform susceptibility were changing, these two trends would be difficult to reconcile. The source therefore infers an additional finite-q dynamic component, coexisting with a large nearly saturated uniform response. It associates that component with AF fluctuations that grow as the magnetic boundary is approached. NMR alone does not identify the exact wavevector or directly resolve two peaks in momentum space. The inference is still informative: the static uniform response cannot account for all the extra relaxation. This is an example of why comparing probes of response and dynamics can reveal information unavailable from either curve alone.

Part 5

What remains a proposed critical scenario

The final discussion suggests competition between ferromagnetic q≈0 and antiferromagnetic finite-q fluctuations could enhance non-Fermi-liquid behavior near the low-field endpoint. It connects that picture with a possible finite spatial correlation range and critical temporal fluctuations. Those are interpretations and theoretical comparisons, not directly measured correlation lengths from this experiment. The authors request high-resolution neutron spectroscopy to establish the precise q dependence. Their linewidth analysis also finds a nearly constant relative spread over a reported temperature interval, arguing against the strong Kondo-disorder distributions seen in some alloy systems; that does not rule out every possible defect. Read this historical 2002 paper as a carefully constrained fluctuation argument with an open momentum-space test, rather than a complete current account of YbRh2Si2 quantum criticality.

Part 6

A negative shift is a calibrated positive spin response

The Si Knight shift is compared with a separately measured bulk susceptibility, using temperature as the parameter along the comparison. The slope gives a negative hyperfine coupling. A growing positive electronic spin response can consequently move the nuclear shift farther negative. The source regards the orbital contribution as small, making the low-temperature shift especially useful for tracking the uniform response. Its powder grains align with their ab planes along the field because the magnetic anisotropy is large, so the measured direction is not a random isotropic average.

The linewidth adds a different check. Its relative spread is nearly constant across a stated temperature interval rather than expanding in the manner associated with broad Kondo-scale distributions in comparison alloy systems. Together with stoichiometry and low residual resistivity, that behavior argues against the particular disorder mechanism discussed by the authors. It does not prove a perfectly defect-free sample. The calibrated coupling sign, oriented geometry and linewidth check should therefore precede the low-field comparison. Without them, the increasingly negative shift might be misread as reduced susceptibility or its magnitude inserted into a Korringa ratio with the wrong directional convention.

Part 7

Local moments cross into a heavy-electron regime

At higher temperature the Yb-related relaxation coefficient varies roughly as inverse temperature, corresponding to a nearly constant 1/T1. The authors interpret this as local-moment dynamics. Below a characteristic scale near fifteen kelvin the raw relaxation rate decreases, consistent with emergence of a coherent heavy-electron band. That scale is compared with, but not set identical to, the characteristic temperature inferred from earlier heat measurements. Coherence here describes a change of electronic dynamics rather than static magnetic order.

To isolate the Yb contribution, the source subtracts the small relaxation coefficient of the nonmagnetic Lu analogue. The resulting quantity is labeled the 4f contribution in the figures. That subtraction is an analysis choice, not a new measurement of one exclusively isolated orbital. On further cooling at higher fields, both shift and relaxation coefficient become roughly temperature independent, marking a field-induced Landau-Fermi-liquid regime. Its crossover temperature decreases as field decreases. The independent changes of temperature and field are important: restoring low-temperature saturation with a field does not establish that the same field-free spectrum survives unchanged underneath it.

Part 8

A Korringa comparison constrains momentum only under assumptions

For a simple metal with isotropic coupling, relaxation and the squared spin shift have a reference ratio. An enhancement concentrated near uniform momentum can make that ratio smaller than the reference because the uniform shift grows especially strongly; enhancement away from zero momentum can add relaxation without the same shift increase. The source uses this logic in the low-temperature Fermi-liquid regime and finds a ratio near one-tenth of the reference at the larger measured fields. It also notes an approximately linear rate-to-shift relation similar to nearly ferromagnetic comparison metals. These observations motivate dominant q-near-zero fluctuations.

The measured shift is anisotropic, so the authors construct an isotropic component using susceptibility information and assume an isotropic hyperfine coupling. The reference-subtracted rate and that constructed spin shift enter the ratio together. It is therefore not a universal numerical test applicable to any material by copying the threshold. The paper explicitly says the comparison has its intended meaning in a well-defined low-temperature Fermi liquid. In the lowest-field non-saturating regime, the mismatch between observables supplies the central additional evidence rather than an unqualified extrapolation of that same simple-metal ratio.

Part 9

The lowest field adds dynamics beyond the saturated uniform channel

At 1.5 kOe the shift becomes nearly constant below about 200 mK, while the 4f relaxation coefficient continues to increase down to about 50 mK. The uniform static response has therefore stopped growing on the observed scale, but the weighted low-frequency dynamics have not. Within the source's coupling framework this points to a finite-wavevector contribution in addition to the large uniform component. The authors associate it with antiferromagnetic tendencies near the much lower magnetic boundary established in cited bulk measurements.

The experiment does not directly reach the proposed critical field near half a kilooersted. Its inference concerns the vicinity, and the field diagram includes prior bulk ordering information as well as NMR crossover points. The arbitrarily matched vertical scales of the field-dependent comparison must not be treated as equal physical units. The hatched region identifies where relaxation keeps developing after the shift has saturated; it is an inferred dynamical regime, not a diffraction image of two susceptibility peaks. A proposed competition between those components could help explain non-Fermi-liquid behavior, but its exact momentum structure and correlation length remain open. The source specifically requests high-resolution neutron measurements for that purpose.

Translate the mathematical statement

Essential equations, meaning and assumptions

Ks ∝ A(0) χ(q = 0,ω = 0)

Symbols and units: Ks: spin part of Si Knight shift; A(0): uniform hyperfine coupling, negative in this material; χ: static susceptibility.

Physical meaning: A negative shift need not mean a negative susceptibility. Saturation constrains the uniform static response.

PDF p. 2 · PDF p. 3

1/(T1T) ∝ Σq |A(q)|² χ″(q,ωN)/ωN

Symbols and units: T1: spin-lattice relaxation time; ωN: nuclear frequency; A(q): hyperfine amplitude; χ″: dynamic susceptibility. Conventional prefactors suppressed.

Physical meaning: Relaxation can grow because of finite-q dynamics even after the uniform static shift stops changing.

PDF p. 1 · PDF p. 3 · PDF p. 4

α = [1/(T1T Ks²)] / [1/(T1T Ks²)]free

Symbols and units: α: normalized relaxation-to-shift-squared ratio using isotropic spin shift and the source's reference assumptions.

Physical meaning: For the simple reference comparison, α below 1 favors q≈0 enhancement and α above 1 favors finite-q enhancement. The source finds about 0.1 in its higher-field Fermi-liquid regime; this is not a universal diagnosis without form-factor assumptions.

What the evidence supports—and where it stops

  • Historical 2002 source; not a current comprehensive QCP phase diagram.
  • The lowest measured NMR field lies above the discussed critical field; exact q dependence and correlation lengths are not directly measured.
  • Korringa interpretation uses reference subtraction, isotropic-shift estimation and hyperfine assumptions; proposed competition is distinct from observation.
