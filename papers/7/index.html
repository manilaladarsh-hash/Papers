Nakamura · supplied source 1989 · experiment

Studies of 63-65Cu-NQR and electrical resistivity for the wide range of x in La2-xSrxCuO4

Cu NQR and resistivity change substantially between ordinary superconducting doping and the very high-Sr localized compositions.

The research question: How do local spin response and bulk transport change across x up to 1?

In two minutes

The whole guide at a glance

  1. Cu NQR and four-probe resistivity follow La₂₋ₓSrₓCuO₄ powders from the insulator through superconductivity all the way to x = 1.
  2. Transport shows insulating, metallic/superconducting and, beyond x ≈ 0.8, variable-range-hopping regimes, with a resistivity minimum near x = 0.3–0.4.
  3. Complex low-x spectra simplify to two isotope peaks above x ≈ 0.4; recoveries are distributed, so the longest component is plotted.
  4. Superconductors show no Hebel–Slichter peak, and much of the nonsuperconducting metal obeys the Korringa law (T₁T ≈ constant).
  5. At x = 0.8–0.9 relaxation peaks and lost intensity suggest a renewed magnetic phase — a tentative assignment, not a measured order parameter.

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

Cu NQR and resistivity change substantially between ordinary superconducting doping and the very high-Sr localized compositions. Solid-state reacted powders are checked by x-ray diffraction for the K2NiF4-type structure, and resistivity uses four-probe contacts. The c-axis lattice parameter first grows and then falls above roughly x = 0.3 to 0.4. The authors report superconductivity over a moderate substitution interval, with bulk evidence from susceptibility and heat capacity in part of that range. Extending to x near 1 accesses a chemically and electronically different localized regime. Nominal Sr content across this range is not automatically identical to a calibrated mobile-hole density.

Terms to understand before starting
  • LSCO: layered La2-xSrxCuO4; nominal x is Sr per formula unit
  • Local probe: a distribution can matter even when the bulk average barely changes
NQR
Nuclear quadrupole resonance selected by the local electric-field gradient without an applied magnetic field.
Electric-field gradient
Spatial variation of the electric field at a nucleus; sensitive to structure and electronic charge.
Hyperfine form factor
Momentum-dependent weighting of electronic spin fluctuations at a selected nuclear site.
T1
Longitudinal spin-lattice recovery time of nuclear populations.
T2
Timescale governing loss of transverse echo coherence; it limits signal accessibility.
A/B environment
Two spectrally distinct local Cu quadrupole environments, each also containing isotope contributions.
Knight shift
Field-induced resonance displacement with spin and orbital contributions.

Part 2

Transport distinguishes multiple regimes

Low-x samples are insulating, intermediate compositions become metallic and superconducting, and compositions beyond roughly x = 0.8 show semiconducting resistivity. The room-temperature resistivity has a broad minimum near x = 0.3 to 0.4 before growing strongly. For high-x samples the logarithm of resistivity plotted against T^(-1/4) is approximately linear, interpreted as variable-range hopping. That is evidence for a transport regime with localized electronic states near the Fermi level under the source assumptions. Grain boundaries, sample chemistry and disorder matter for ceramic transport. A fitting exponent is not an independent determination of the microscopic density of states or localization mechanism.

Part 3

Spectra and distributed recoveries

Cu echoes are observed readily above 30 MHz for sufficiently doped samples; failure at lower doping is attributed to very short transverse relaxation. Smaller-x spectra have extra local-environment peaks in addition to the two isotope resonances. Above x about 0.4 the simpler two-peak spectrum remains. Magnetization recoveries are nearly single exponential around x = 0.4 but develop multiple components at smaller and larger substitutions. The paper presents the longest-time recovery component for practical comparison, explicitly acknowledging a distribution. A rate plotted for that component therefore cannot be equated to an unbiased whole-sample average or to an exhaustive microscopic map.

PDF p. 3 · PDF p. 4

Part 4

Superconductivity and metallic relaxation

In superconducting samples the Cu spin-lattice rate falls below Tc without the standard Hebel-Slichter enhancement and with a power-like trend. In much of the nonsuperconducting metallic region T1T is approximately constant, the Korringa relation. Both1/T1T and transverse relaxation change sharply with increasing Sr toward x = 0.3 to 0.4. Enhanced rates at lower substitution are interpreted as electronic spin correlations and indirect nuclear coupling rather than ordinary nuclear dipole effects alone. The source also revisits its earlier magnetic explanation for split lines: absence of matching relaxation anomalies and NMR evidence makes that long-range-order assignment unlikely. This self-correction is part of the paper, not a conclusion that every local line is magnetically ordered.

Part 5

The high-x anomaly and its limits

At x = 0.8 and 0.9 the rate develops low-temperature peaks and temperature-corrected intensity falls; even higher-x signals become difficult to see in the usual frequency window. The authors suggest a renewed magnetic phase, possibly antiferromagnetic, in the localized regime. That assignment remains tentative: intense relaxation, changing frequencies and inaccessible echoes can all reduce detected intensity. The reported behavior is not a direct measurement of an order-parameter amplitude. For comparison with later literature, retain the historical preparation, the very broad chemical range and the selected recovery component. Modern near-one-eighth stripe questions and heavily overdoped x = 0.33 muon searches address different regions and should not be folded into one universal picture.

Part 6

How to compare the evidence

Two selection effects should stay visible when comparing this early survey with later LSCO work. First, the reported relaxation may be the longest recovery component, because the whole recovery is not always a single exponential. Its doping trend is therefore not necessarily the trend of a spatially averaged rate. Second, a nucleus whose transverse decay becomes too rapid may fail to produce a detectable echo even though it remains in the sample. Combining intensity loss with a relaxation anomaly motivates a magnetic interpretation, but does not determine a unique ordered structure. Finally, compositions approaching x = 1 are outside the chemical range used in the ordinary cuprate superconducting dome. Their localized behavior must remain attached to these particular ceramic preparations and nominal compositions.

Part 7

Electric frequency and magnetic relaxation answer different questions

NQR uses the electric-field gradient at a quadrupolar nucleus to select a resonance without an external field. The gradient describes how the local electric potential changes around that nucleus; electronic occupations, neighboring ions and structural distortions can all affect it. A shift in NQR frequency therefore identifies a changed local electric environment before it establishes a changed hole concentration. Once a frequency slice is selected, the recovery of nuclear magnetization probes the fluctuations that exchange energy with those nuclei. This second measurement can be predominantly magnetic even though the first selection was electric. The distinction explains why the article examines both spectral shape and recovery instead of assigning a magnetic phase to every peak.

Magnetic relaxation is filtered. The electronic spin fluctuations must produce a field at the nucleus, so hyperfine couplings and the geometry of neighboring spins weight the momentum-dependent response. Different nuclei, orientations or local environments can weight the same electronic system differently. A large Cu rate does not automatically mean a similarly large uniform susceptibility, because antiferromagnetic fluctuations can dominate its weighted sum. Conversely, two unequal rates need not mean two unequal carrier densities if their couplings differ. The scientifically informative step is to combine a charge-sensitive environment label with its measured spin response, keeping the selection and the inferred electronic cause separate.

Part 8

Nuclear recovery and echo survival are separate clocks

Spin-lattice recovery describes how the populations of nuclear levels return toward equilibrium after excitation. Its timescale is T1. Echo decay describes how the transverse spin signal loses coherence before it can be detected; its characteristic times involve T2 and sometimes separate Gaussian and exponential components. A nucleus can have a measurable recovery only if its echo survives the pulse sequence and receiver dead time. This means that the published rate may describe a selected subset of nuclei rather than every Cu in the material. Rapid fluctuations, slow glassy fields, broad frequency distributions and overlap can affect those two measurements differently.

A recovery with several components also needs assignment before interpretation. Multiple nuclear levels, two Cu isotopes, overlapping A and B resonances, or different electronic environments can all produce more than one time dependence. A fitted exponential coefficient is not automatically the volume fraction of an electronic phase. When comparing different temperatures, check whether the spectrometer follows the moving resonance and whether the excitation bandwidth samples the same relative part of the line. When comparing different papers, check the pulse separation, frequency choice and fitted recovery convention. These procedural details explain why an apparent disagreement between rates can expose a selection effect rather than a contradiction about the whole spin system.

Part 9

The wide chemical survey is not the ordinary superconducting dome

The source follows nominal Sr substitution far beyond the narrow range normally used to discuss optimal and overdoped superconductivity. That breadth makes it a survey of changing electronic and chemical regimes, not simply a better resolved version of the familiar dome. Metallic transport at intermediate substitution and semiconducting behavior near the high-x end are qualitatively different contexts for the nuclear data. The plotted hopping transformation in the high-x ceramics is a way to test an assumed localized conduction law over an interval, rather than a direct measurement of a unique microscopic carrier state. Grain boundaries and preparation remain relevant to a ceramic transport comparison.

This distinction is also important for comparisons with later x = 0.33 experiments. A low-temperature magnetic search in a carefully annealed nonsuperconducting crystal is not equivalent to the extremely high nominal substitutions in this early ceramic series. The same word overdoped can conceal different preparation and electronic regimes. Preserve the actual nominal x, oxygen treatment and transport character attached to each result. The early survey's value is that it shows several regimes and raises questions about them; it does not establish one universal trajectory for every mobile carrier or a precise local-hole calibration over its whole range.

Part 10

Understand the deliberate choice of the longest recovery component

When the magnetization recovery is distributed, the paper uses its longest-time component for a practical comparison across compositions. That component supplies an operationally consistent observable, but its physical meaning differs from a volume-averaged rate. Fast components can contribute strongly at early times while making little difference to the later tail selected for the fit. An apparently gentle doping trend in the selected component can therefore coexist with strongly heterogeneous faster response. Reading the recovery convention is necessary before comparing its numbers with a later single-exponential or frequency-resolved analysis.

The paper's reconsideration of the split-line assignment is equally instructive. Extra spectral contributions were once interpreted magnetically, but the lack of corresponding anomalies and complementary NMR information make long-range magnetic order an unlikely explanation for those particular lines. A resonance can distinguish an electric environment without proving a magnetic phase. At the high-x end, intensity loss and rate anomalies motivate a renewed magnetic-state proposal, but the structure remains tentative. Frequency shifts and short echoes can also hide signal. The article consequently teaches both how to identify a useful anomaly and how to keep its visibility, recovery selection and microscopic interpretation separate.

Why is the plotted rate not always a mean?

What was reconsidered about split lines?

The early long-range magnetic-order explanation became unlikely without corresponding anomalies.

Is high-x magnetic order fully established?

It is proposed from anomalies, with the type still tentative.

Why is the longest-component rate different from an average?

The late recovery tail emphasizes slow components and can underrepresent faster populations.

Does an extra NQR line establish long-range magnetic order?

No. Electric environments and isotope structure also produce lines; the paper itself revises a magnetic assignment.

Those ceramic compositions and preparation conditions extend beyond the usual superconducting regime and have distinct localized behavior.
