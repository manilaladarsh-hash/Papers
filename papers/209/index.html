Anomalous Criticality in the Electrical Resistivity of La2-xSrxCuO4

High fields expose a low-temperature T-linear resistivity term over a broad overdoped range; the source’s scattering and pseudogap explanation is model dependent.

The research question: Does normal-state resistivity beneath the superconducting dome follow a conventional single-point quantum-critical pattern?

In two minutes

The whole guide at a glance

  1. High persistent and pulsed fields suppress superconductivity in LSCO crystals, exposing the normal-state resistivity below the zero-field Tc.
  2. A T-linear term persists at low temperature over a broad range, p ≈ 0.18–0.29, while p = 0.33 becomes more quadratic.
  3. Conventional quantum-critical metals confine such anomalies near one tuning point; LSCO does not, and its T² coefficient does not diverge.
  4. The data are fitted as ρ = α₀ + α₁T + α₂T²; α₁ falls with overdoping roughly as superconductivity weakens, while α₂ stays steady.
  5. The authors' anisotropic-scattering and pseudogap account is a model built on these coefficients, not something resistivity measures directly.

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

High fields expose a low-temperature T-linear resistivity term over a broad overdoped range; the source’s scattering and pseudogap explanation is model dependent. Fields perpendicular to the CuO2 layers suppress superconductivity, allowing normal-state measurements below the zero-field Tc. Detailed new field sweeps include p = 0.18, 0.21 and 0.23, supplemented by other dopings extending toward p = 0.33. For each temperature, a normal high-field form ρ(H) = ρ(0) + AH² is fitted over varying field cutoffs. A stable plateau in the extrapolated ρ(0) identifies a usable normal-state fit range; fitting too close to superconductivity or over too narrow a field interval is unreliable. Direct 48 T data display similar temperature dependence to the extrapolated curve, supporting the procedure without making the extrapolation assumption disappear.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Resistivity: charge transport weights electronic velocities and scattering
  • High magnetic field can expose a normal state hidden by superconductivity
  • A sum of linear and quadratic terms can give a changing effective exponent
Resistivity ρ
Electric-field-to-current-density ratio in the stated geometry; commonly reported in μΩ cm.
Hall coefficient RH
Transverse transport response per applied magnetic field under a defined sign convention.
Residual resistivity
Temperature-independent term used in a chosen low-temperature transport fit.
Magnetoresistance
Change of resistivity under an applied magnetic field.
Effective exponent
Local logarithmic slope of a fitted or measured temperature-dependent response.
Normal-state continuation
Inferred nonsuperconducting response where zero-field superconductivity prevents direct access.
Nominal doping
Chemical substitution label, which may differ from inferred mobile or effective carrier content.

Part 3

Why the result is anomalous in the QCP comparison

In the conventional metallic examples discussed by the source, anomalous low-temperature resistivity is confined near a critical tuning point, with a crossover to T² away from it and a divergent quadratic coefficient on approach. LSCO instead retains a linear term over a broad doping interval. The quadratic coefficient does not diverge toward the special composition; its detailed value depends on including the saturation term. Both fitting procedures identify a kink and a maximum linear coefficient near pc = 0.185 ± 0.005, about 1 microohm cm per kelvin. The robustness of that trend is stronger evidence than assigning every color in an interpolated exponent diagram to a sharply bounded phase. A second tuning parameter or an extended anomalous phase are discussed as possibilities.

Part 4

From a coefficient to a scattering-rate hypothesis

On the overdoped side, α1 decreases with doping and roughly tracks decreasing superconducting strength, while the saturation-model α2 remains comparatively steady. The paper considers both microscopic coexistence of differing regions and two scattering channels on a common Fermi surface. Its favored momentum-dependent picture draws on angle-dependent magnetoresistance in Tl2201: a nearly isotropic T² term plus a T-linear term largest near antinodes and weak near nodes. Using a two-dimensional transport estimate with d = 0.64 nm, antinodal vF about 8 × 10⁴ m/s and kF about 7 nm⁻¹, the measured slope gives a momentum-average ℏ/τ near πkBT and a modeled antinodal value near 2πkBT. These numerical rates depend on geometry, velocities and the transferred angular form, and are not a direct LSCO lifetime measurement.

Part 5

The proposed pseudogap account and its boundaries

The source suggests that the interaction associated with linear scattering strengthens toward lower doping and can both favor pairing and destroy antinodal quasiparticle coherence. Loss of strongly scattering states could then reduce α1 and superconducting condensation energy below pc, with a pseudogap stabilizing the remaining states. This is a proposed causal narrative, not uniquely selected by the resistivity data. The directly established findings are an extended low-T linear contribution, coefficient trends and a kink under the chosen analysis. The later supplied ARPES paper offers a band-topology account of much of the thermodynamic peak near a nearby composition, underscoring that transport and calorimetry need distinct assumptions. The supplied fifth PDF page contains this article’s final references and the beginning of an unrelated copper-mechanics report; that fragment is outside the LSCO lesson. The online methods and supplementary figures are referenced but absent.

Part 6

How to compare the evidence

The two resistivity terms illustrate why an effective exponent can move continuously without marking separate phases. Their relative contribution changes with temperature even at one doping, so a single fitted exponent can summarize a mixture rather than identify a unique scattering law. The source therefore examines linear and quadratic coefficients separately and checks how they evolve with doping and fit range. Its extended low-temperature linear contribution differs from a textbook fan collapsing onto one critical point. Turning a transport coefficient into a microscopic lifetime introduces additional assumptions about Fermi velocity, surface geometry and angular scattering; the anisotropy motivation partly comes from another cuprate. Keep the measured coefficient trend, the inferred antinodal decoherence and the proposed connection to pairing as three successive levels of inference.

Part 7

A fitted transport law is an observation before it is a mechanism

Electrical resistivity measures the response of charge motion to an applied current in the stated geometry. The measured value combines electronic structure, available states, scattering and sometimes sample connectivity. A useful temperature fit can summarize the data without uniquely separating those ingredients. A linear term, a quadratic term, a Hall sign or a hopping-like transformation each constrains a transport description, but none alone specifies a microscopic spin pattern or pairing interaction. The supplied paper therefore compares transport with magnetic or superconducting information instead of using one exponent as a complete electronic phase label.

Changing the plotted variables can help test a law but can also obscure mixtures. A straight segment against T² tests an approximate quadratic dependence over that interval. A local logarithmic exponent summarizes a derivative and may evolve smoothly when two additive contributions coexist. The fit range and residual offset matter because subtracting a different constant changes that derivative, especially at low temperature. Compare the source's alternative fits and controls before treating a coefficient as universal. A microscopic lifetime requires additional assumptions about Fermi-surface geometry and velocities, while a Hall-derived count requires a sufficiently simple transport model. The direct coefficient or sign trend should remain distinct from those stronger interpretations.

Part 8

Keep chemical tuning and access to the normal state separate

Nominal substitution identifies a preparation parameter, not automatically the number of mobile carriers in every electronic regime. Oxygen stoichiometry can compensate nominal holes and also change disorder. A magnetic field can suppress superconductivity and add magnetoresistance while leaving the chemical composition fixed. These controls alter different combinations of physical variables. The paper's inference is meaningful only when the measured state, field geometry, preparation and composition convention are preserved alongside the transport curve. Translating chemical notation should precede comparison with a modern doping diagram.

Access beneath the zero-field superconducting transition requires particular care. A high-field resistivity is directly measured in that field, whereas an inferred normal-state zero-field continuation depends on a model of magnetoresistance. Likewise, a missing superconducting transition means none was detected under the stated conditions and temperature floor, not a proof about every specimen at that nominal composition. The source uses complementary response and preparation checks to constrain these distinctions. This is why comparing samples, fitting protocols and limits is part of learning the physics rather than ancillary experimental detail. It prevents an approximate trend from becoming a universal boundary or a uniquely identified collision mechanism.

Part 9

The field-cutoff plateau tests an extrapolation assumption

Below the zero-field transition, the normal zero-field resistivity cannot be directly read from an ordinary zero-field measurement because superconductivity intervenes. The source measures high-field transport, fits a normal magnetoresistance form and extrapolates its intercept. Changing the lower field cutoff then checks whether the inferred intercept has stabilized in a genuinely normal fitting interval. A plateau supports the chosen procedure; a moving intercept signals that superconducting effects, insufficient field range or an inadequate fit may still matter. This is more informative than taking one convenient high-field fit without a stability test.

The direct high-field curve supplies a second comparison by showing a similar temperature trend to the extrapolated result. It strengthens the observed extended linear contribution while leaving the extrapolation's model status intact. The inferred curve is therefore a normal-state zero-field continuation, not a directly measured zero-field resistivity beneath Tc. The supplied article's main pages support these controls; its absent online methods and supplementary figures cannot be cited as separately reviewed data. Keeping the measured and inferred curves distinct is central to understanding what has been established about low-temperature transport.

Part 10

A moving exponent can be produced by two fixed powers

For a positive temperature-dependent response α1T + α2T², the logarithmic derivative is (α1 + 2α2T)/(α1 + α2T). It varies continuously between linear-dominated and quadratic-dominated behavior as the relative terms change, even when the same additive law remains valid. An effective-exponent color map can therefore show smooth changes without marking separate phases or proving a single power-law mechanism at every point. The source examines the coefficients independently and tests a saturation form to assess fit-range sensitivity. Its robust linear trend and kink deserve more weight than a phase interpretation assigned to each interpolated color.

The broad low-temperature linear interval also differs from the conventional comparison in which anomalous transport narrows onto one critical composition and the quadratic coefficient grows strongly. The paper then relates the linear term to superconductivity and proposes anisotropic antinodal scattering, partly using motivation from another cuprate. Converting a slope to a momentum-resolved lifetime requires the stated velocity, geometry and angular assumptions; it is not an independent LSCO lifetime measurement. The measured coefficient evolution, inferred loss of antinodal coherence and proposed pairing connection are successive claims of increasing interpretation, rather than one experimentally identical result.

Symbols and units: ρab: in-plane resistivity; α0: residual term; α1 and α2: fitted linear and quadratic coefficients.

Physical meaning: Coexisting fitted terms do not by themselves prove two independent microscopic fluids or scattering mechanisms.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Is the inferred ρ(0) directly measured at zero field below Tc?

No. It is the normal-state extrapolation from high-field magnetoresistance.

Does α1 directly measure a momentum-resolved lifetime?

No. Relating a resistivity slope to lifetime requires Fermi-surface and angular-scattering assumptions.

What disagrees with a simple conventional QCP comparison?

The low-temperature linear term spans a broad doping interval and the quadratic coefficient does not show the expected divergence.

Why vary the lower field cutoff?

A stable inferred intercept tests whether the fit uses a sufficient normal-state field interval instead of superconducting or unstable data.

Can an exponent between one and two arise without a new single power law?

Yes. The logarithmic slope of α1T + α2T² changes continuously with the relative contributions.

Which inference partly imports information from another cuprate?

The proposed angular scattering structure motivating antinodal lifetime estimates uses complementary Tl2201 evidence; it is not directly mapped in LSCO here.
