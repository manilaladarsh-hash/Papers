6 · Ag₃LiIr₂O₆: disorder can obscure order

Sample comparison and local distributions reveal why a weak bulk signature is inconclusive.

The research question: How do the cleaner and more disordered specimens change the magnetic inference?

In two minutes

The whole guide at a glance

  1. Ag₃LiIr₂O₆ is made by topochemical exchange; sample A is cleaner, while sample B has Ag inclusions and stacking faults.
  2. Lithium lines broaden below about 14 K, and sample A shows a sharp 1/T₁ maximum near 9 ± 1 K, consistent with μSR evidence for order.
  3. ILT in sample A treats roughly 60 % at 4.2 K as static on the NMR time scale, with the rest still fluctuating.
  4. Sample B's extra lithium component (about 17 % of the detected line) has a small shift and slow recovery: suppressed magnetism near inclusions.
  5. That 17 % is not an Ag percentage — one inclusion can affect several lithium sites and pulse selection shapes intensity — so disorder can obscure order.

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

Sample B’s additional lithium component accounts for about 17% of the detected line. That number is not a measured Ag substitution percentage: one inclusion can influence several nearby lithium environments, and pulse selection affects intensity. The interpretation gains support because the main B line resembles the cleaner A response while the side component shows suppressed local magnetism. Keeping the components separate explains why a bulk measurement can mix relatively intact regions with environments altered by disorder.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Quantum spin liquid candidate: a material tested against a quantum-disordered model; a null ordering signal is insufficient identification.
  • K: local resonance shift, sensitive to a coupling-weighted static susceptibility.
  • 1/T₁: recovery rate that samples fluctuations in the probe frequency window.
  • ILT: constrained, regularized inference of a relaxation-rate distribution from recovery data.
  • log-rate density: probability is the area in d log₁₀(1/T₁), not peak height.
Quantum spin-liquid candidate
A material investigated for a nonconventional disordered quantum magnetic state; candidate evidence is not a unique phase identification.
Recovery kernel
The nuclear-state and preparation-dependent function mapping a relaxation rate to measured magnetization.
ILT
Inverse-Laplace-transform inference of rate-distribution weights from a measured recovery under a specified kernel and regularization.
Log-rate density
A distribution whose weight is integrated using dlog₁₀R rather than dR; peak height and integrated fraction differ.
Tikhonov regularization
Here, the positive term α ‖P‖² used to stabilize the nonnegative weight inference, with analysis-dependent broadening.
Geometric mean rate
The exponential of a normalized weighted mean of log rates; different from an arithmetic mean.
Echo visibility
The detected fraction remaining after transverse decay, pulse selection and timing.
Frequency–rate map
A joint representation connecting selected resonance frequency, distribution of rates and retained equilibrium intensity.
Affected neighborhood
The spins contributing to a nuclear response near a defect, without assuming an imaged sharp real-space boundary.

A descriptive explanation, step by step

Follow the full argument.

Part 1

Use the specimen history as part of the evidence

Topochemical exchange produces a metastable iridate with honeycomb layers, and synthesis history changes disorder. Sample A is cleaner with respect to unwanted in-plane Ag inclusions; sample B has those inclusions as well as stacking faults. Neither should be relabeled an exact realization of the ideal Kitaev model. The local lithium resonance of B has an additional side component with about 17% relative signal, while A has one principal line. Because a single defect can influence nearby nuclei, that 17% is not a chemical Ag concentration. The main peak in B has a shift close to A’s, supporting the interpretation that it samples relatively cleaner regions.

Part 2

Separate line broadening from recovery-rate peaks

Line broadening starts below roughly 14 K, consistent with the onset of slow or static magnetic fields. Sample A also has a sharp 1/T₁ maximum near 9 ± 1 K, which the author connects with critical slowing and supporting μSR ordering evidence. The broad maximum near 40 K should not automatically be called a gap. Lithium occupies a high-symmetry site among six Ir neighbors, and cancellation of hyperfine fluctuations could suppress its response even without a spin-excitation gap. The source explicitly retains that alternative. A line width and a recovery rate therefore need their separate static/dynamic windows before being combined into a phase interpretation.

Part 3

What the inferred fractions mean

The low-temperature ILT analysis of A separates components whose rates vary differently, and the thesis interprets roughly 60% at 4.2 K as static on the NMR window while some fraction remains fluctuating. In B, the side peak’s small shift and slow recovery support suppressed local magnetism near inclusions, with the bulk upturn attributed to additional weakly coupled spins. The thesis proposes singlet-like regions and orphan spins as a disorder scenario. These are material interpretations constrained by several measurements; ILT alone does not image the spin configuration. The caution for QSL identification is concrete: more disorder can wash out an ordering signature that is clear in a cleaner specimen.

Part 4

Separate a chemical defect fraction from its affected signal

Sample B’s additional lithium component accounts for about 17% of the detected line. That number is not a measured Ag substitution percentage: one inclusion can influence several nearby lithium environments, and pulse selection affects intensity. The interpretation gains support because the main B line resembles the cleaner A response while the side component shows suppressed local magnetism. Keeping the components separate explains why a bulk measurement can mix relatively intact regions with environments altered by disorder.

The ordering argument also combines distinct time windows. Static or slowly varying local fields broaden a line; dynamic fluctuations can produce a recovery-rate maximum when their slowing enters the nuclear window. The thesis compares those signatures with supporting magnetic measurements and infers a low-temperature mixture of static and fluctuating responses in A. A broad maximum at a higher temperature does not require a spin gap because the lithium geometry can cancel some fluctuating contributions. The numerical rate distribution alone cannot distinguish every microscopic spin configuration. Read the proposed singlet-like regions and orphan spins as a disorder interpretation constrained by the sample comparison. The practical lesson is that adding disorder can weaken a visible ordering signature without producing the desired ideal spin-liquid state. Local environmental accounting makes that ambiguity testable.

Does the 17% side line mean 17% Ag impurity?

No. A defect can affect several lithium nuclei. The relative observed nuclear signal is a different quantity from chemical defect concentration.

Why is the 40 K suppression not uniquely a gap?

The lithium coupling geometry may filter relevant antiferromagnetic fluctuations. The source retains a cancellation scenario.
