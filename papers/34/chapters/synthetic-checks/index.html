The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

With slow weight 0.6, slow rate 30 s⁻¹ and fast rate 1000 s⁻¹, the normalized saturation-style model is fully known. At 1 ms, its magnetization is about 0.271: the fast fraction has already recovered substantially while the slow fraction has recovered little. At very long delay both fractions approach equilibrium. At very short delay neither is recovered. If the rates are made equal, the weighted sum becomes one exponential regardless of the chosen weight. If one weight goes to zero, the absent population cannot be reconstructed from the recovery. These limits explain what information the curve can contain before any inverse algorithm is applied.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Quantum spin liquid candidate: a material tested against a quantum-disordered model; a null ordering signal is insufficient identification.
  • K: local resonance shift, sensitive to a coupling-weighted static susceptibility.
  • 1/T₁: recovery rate that samples fluctuations in the probe frequency window.
  • ILT: constrained, regularized inference of a relaxation-rate distribution from recovery data.
  • log-rate density: probability is the area in d log₁₀(1/T₁), not peak height.
Quantum spin-liquid candidate
A material investigated for a nonconventional disordered quantum magnetic state; candidate evidence is not a unique phase identification.
Recovery kernel
The nuclear-state and preparation-dependent function mapping a relaxation rate to measured magnetization.
ILT
Inverse-Laplace-transform inference of rate-distribution weights from a measured recovery under a specified kernel and regularization.
Log-rate density
A distribution whose weight is integrated using dlog₁₀R rather than dR; peak height and integrated fraction differ.
Tikhonov regularization
Here, the positive term α ‖P‖² used to stabilize the nonnegative weight inference, with analysis-dependent broadening.
Geometric mean rate
The exponential of a normalized weighted mean of log rates; different from an arithmetic mean.
Echo visibility
The detected fraction remaining after transverse decay, pulse selection and timing.
Frequency–rate map
A joint representation connecting selected resonance frequency, distribution of rates and retained equilibrium intensity.
Affected neighborhood
The spins contributing to a nuclear response near a defect, without assuming an imaged sharp real-space boundary.

An average changes with its definition

Look for: Log weights and linear rates produce different means.

Averages depend on their measure. The log-rate center takes a weighted average of logarithms and then exponentiates; the arithmetic mean averages the rates directly. Their large difference here is a feature of broad rate separation, and shows why an undefined phrase such as averageT₁ can obscure the physical comparison. For the shown toy mixture the arithmetic mean is 418 s⁻¹.

Always state the averaging convention.

A descriptive explanation, step by step

Follow the full argument.

Read the full detailed argument4 parts · about 2 min

Part 1

The two populations and their summed curve

The synthetic example has weight 0.6 at 30 s⁻¹ and 0.4 at 1000 s⁻¹, under unit equilibrium and saturation-style preparation. Forty recovery times across a broad delay range and 0.1% Gaussian noise are chosen. The forward curve is fully specified, so the inferred rates and areas can be compared with known truth. Under this setup, the thesis recovers peaks near 30.1 and 997 s⁻¹ and the expected fractions. A stretched fit compresses both into one characteristic rate and misses a shoulder. The example demonstrates what can be lost by a restrictive functional form, rather than presenting those two rates as observations in an actual sample.

Part 2

Geometric means and resolution

For normalized log-rate weights, the thesis’s center is the exponential of the weighted log rate. It is a geometric mean, which gives about 122 s⁻¹ for the chosen two-rate model. The arithmetic mean would instead be 418 s⁻¹, so the averaging convention matters. The source links larger regularization to wider inferred peaks and lower practical resolution, and discusses how increased SNR helps distinguish components. Its rough half-decade separation example is tied to particular sampling and noise. Resolution is not a universal property of ILT that can be quoted independently of the acquisition, kernel, represented rate grid and regularization.

Part 3

Visibility and incomplete preparation

At short measured times, incomplete inversion can arise from an imperfect pulse or recovery components faster than the shortest delay. The source discusses fitting a preparation factor and also representing extra very-fast bins, later excluding them from the physical analysis. Those auxiliary weights can account for a missing initial amplitude without demonstrating an actual separately resolved fast population. The guide’s interactive explorer computes only the exact forward two-population curve. It does not run the author’s inverse program or claim to recover distributions from arbitrary user data. Compare limiting cases—zero weight, equal rates and long delay—to understand the signal before interpreting an inversion.

Part 4

Use limiting cases to understand the two-rate curve

With slow weight 0.6, slow rate 30 s⁻¹ and fast rate 1000 s⁻¹, the normalized saturation-style model is fully known. At 1 ms, its magnetization is about 0.271: the fast fraction has already recovered substantially while the slow fraction has recovered little. At very long delay both fractions approach equilibrium. At very short delay neither is recovered. If the rates are made equal, the weighted sum becomes one exponential regardless of the chosen weight. If one weight goes to zero, the absent population cannot be reconstructed from the recovery. These limits explain what information the curve can contain before any inverse algorithm is applied.

The arithmetic rate mean is 418 s⁻¹, whereas the weighted geometric rate is about 122 s⁻¹. Both are correctly computed summaries of the same example; they answer different averaging questions. Neither is automatically the characteristic rate obtained by fitting a stretched exponential. To test the inversion, the source adds specified noise and sampling, then compares reconstructed peaks and areas with the known truth. Success at those settings demonstrates capability under controlled conditions. It does not establish the same resolution for a different acquisition or material. The browser explorer deliberately exposes the exact forward problem: moving the weights and rates changes the curve transparently, without pretending to fit unknown data or run the author’s ILT program.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

What happens when both rates become equal?

Their weighted sum reduces to a single-rate recovery regardless of the population weight.

Why do fastest auxiliary bins need caution?

They may absorb imperfect preparation or a missing short-time amplitude, rather than resolve a distinct physical environment within the measurement window.
