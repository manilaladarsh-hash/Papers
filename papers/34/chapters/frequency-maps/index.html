5 · Keep frequency and relaxation together

A joint distribution retains correlations; its marginals and selected slices answer different questions.

The research question: What can a two-dimensional map reveal that two averages hide?

In two minutes

The whole guide at a glance

  1. At each frequency a recovery is inverted into a rate distribution; keeping each slice's equilibrium amplitude builds a joint map C(f, R).
  2. Integrating over log rate gives back the line shape; integrating only part of the rate range gives a rate-resolved line.
  3. Two environments at nearly the same frequency with different rates merge in a spectrum but separate in the map.
  4. A recovery at one frequency can miss part of another population entirely.
  5. Maps cost much more acquisition time; drift, visibility, regularization and coverage all shape them, and normalizing slices erases relative intensity.

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

Suppose two environments occupy nearly the same frequency but have different relaxation rates. Their line-shape projection overlaps, so a conventional spectrum alone can merge them. A joint map can separate the rates at that frequency if the recovery contains enough information. Conversely, measuring a recovery at only one frequency can omit an environment elsewhere in the line. The source’s controlled three-component illustration demonstrates why the two coordinates are complementary, rather than why any two-dimensional image is automatically reliable.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Quantum spin liquid candidate: a material tested against a quantum-disordered model; a null ordering signal is insufficient identification.
  • K: local resonance shift, sensitive to a coupling-weighted static susceptibility.
  • 1/T₁: recovery rate that samples fluctuations in the probe frequency window.
  • ILT: constrained, regularized inference of a relaxation-rate distribution from recovery data.
  • log-rate density: probability is the area in d log₁₀(1/T₁), not peak height.
Quantum spin-liquid candidate
A material investigated for a nonconventional disordered quantum magnetic state; candidate evidence is not a unique phase identification.
Recovery kernel
The nuclear-state and preparation-dependent function mapping a relaxation rate to measured magnetization.
ILT
Inverse-Laplace-transform inference of rate-distribution weights from a measured recovery under a specified kernel and regularization.
Log-rate density
A distribution whose weight is integrated using dlog₁₀R rather than dR; peak height and integrated fraction differ.
Tikhonov regularization
Here, the positive term α ‖P‖² used to stabilize the nonnegative weight inference, with analysis-dependent broadening.
Geometric mean rate
The exponential of a normalized weighted mean of log rates; different from an arithmetic mean.
Echo visibility
The detected fraction remaining after transverse decay, pulse selection and timing.
Frequency–rate map
A joint representation connecting selected resonance frequency, distribution of rates and retained equilibrium intensity.
Affected neighborhood
The spins contributing to a nuclear response near a defect, without assuming an imaged sharp real-space boundary.

A descriptive explanation, step by step

Follow the full argument.

Read the full detailed argument4 parts · about 2 min

Part 1

Build the map with intensity intact

At each frequency, acquire a recovery and infer the rate distribution for the nuclei selected there. Retain the equilibrium amplitude associated with that frequency, then combine the slices into C(f,R). Integrating in log rate gives the frequency line shape; integrating only a restricted rate interval gives a rate-resolved partial line. A map thus keeps information about static local fields and dynamics together. A single-frequency distribution describes a slice, not the full sample, and individually unit-normalizing every slice would remove the line-intensity information needed for these projections.

Part 2

Why the synthetic three-component map is useful

The source constructs three overlapping two-dimensional Gaussian components. Two share nearly the same frequency yet have different rates; the third sits at another frequency. The ordinary line merges the frequency-overlapping pair, while one selected recovery misses parts of the third population. The joint map identifies all three under the specified synthetic conditions. The illustration here uses conceptual ellipses and projection arrows without copying fitted experimental coordinates or fabricating pixels. Inspect the original figures to distinguish the known synthetic model from the later material maps and their different uncertainties.

Part 3

The experiment costs more than the graphic suggests

A joint map needs many recoveries over frequencies, each with sufficient SNR for stable inference. The thesis describes a large acquisition-time cost compared with conventional one-dimensional work. Drift, different sensitivity or visibility across the line, regularization and finite frequency coverage can affect the combined result. The advantage is not that two dimensions automatically guarantee truth. It is that local frequency and rate can separate environments whose one-dimensional projections overlap. The Zn-barlowite fluorine study uses this approach to compare its narrow main signal with a broad, faster component.

Part 4

Recover a projection without discarding its intensity

Suppose two environments occupy nearly the same frequency but have different relaxation rates. Their line-shape projection overlaps, so a conventional spectrum alone can merge them. A joint map can separate the rates at that frequency if the recovery contains enough information. Conversely, measuring a recovery at only one frequency can omit an environment elsewhere in the line. The source’s controlled three-component illustration demonstrates why the two coordinates are complementary, rather than why any two-dimensional image is automatically reliable.

To build the map, retain the equilibrium signal amplitude of each frequency slice. Integrating all its weights over log rate then reproduces that slice’s spectral intensity. Summing only a selected rate interval instead yields a partial line associated with that dynamic range. Unit-normalizing each slice independently would keep its internal fractions but discard the relative heights needed to reconstruct the actual line. Acquisition drift, incomplete frequency coverage and changing SNR can distort those projections. Fixed regularization helps compare slices but cannot make their resolution identical. Read a joint feature through its measured recoveries, source intensities and reconstruction checks before attaching a domain label. A map organizes static/dynamic correlation; it does not by itself image a real-space boundary.

Essential equations, meaning and assumptions

M₀(f) = ∫ C(f,R) d log₁₀R

Symbols and units: f: resonance frequency; R: relaxation rate; C: intensity-preserving joint density.

Physical meaning: The full integral gives the line; a restricted integral gives a rate-resolved partial line. Use the same visibility and normalization conventions.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Why retain amplitude between frequencies?

The relative equilibrium signals encode the line shape. Removing them by separate normalization destroys that population weighting.

Does one P(R) represent the whole broad line?

No. It represents the excited frequency band unless full-line coverage and consistent weighting are established.

What is the strongest supported conclusion of this unit?

A joint distribution retains correlations; its marginals and selected slices answer different questions. Synthetic maps and measured material maps have different evidence status.
