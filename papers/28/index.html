From quantum matter to high-temperature superconductivity in copper oxides

A cuprate is a doped correlated insulator whose spin, charge and superconducting degrees of freedom cannot generally be treated independently.

The research question: From a constrained electron to a coherent pair?

In two minutes

The whole guide at a glance

  1. A cuprate is a doped Mott insulator: strong Cu–O repulsion forbids double occupancy, and doping adds mobile charge without erasing correlations.
  2. Long-range antiferromagnetism disappears quickly with doping, but finite-range dynamic spin correlations survive.
  3. The superconducting gap has d_x²−y² symmetry — nodes on the diagonals and a sign change under 90° rotation — which constrains, but does not fix, the mechanism.
  4. Pairing (the gap) and phase coherence (superfluid stiffness) are separate scales; a small stiffness can limit Tc on the underdoped side.
  5. Charge order, nematicity and pair-density waves break different symmetries, and ARPES, tunnelling and neutrons each see a different window.

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

The review begins with an apparent paradox: the parent copper oxide resists charge motion, yet doping can produce unusually strong superconductivity. Local repulsion and spin exchange remain important after the material becomes conducting. A sign-changing d-wave pair state can use repulsive interactions, but forming pairs and making their phases coherent are separate requirements. Between the parent insulator and a more conventional overdoped metal, spectroscopy, transport and scattering reveal a pseudogap, spatial orders and a strange metal. The article explains how these observations constrain each other while recording contradictions between probes and unresolved model problems. Read it as a carefully organized 2015 map of the research problem, with the materials papers supplying sample-specific detail.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Mott insulator: A charge-insulating state produced by strong interaction constraints rather than merely a filled one-electron band.
  • Superexchange: An effective spin interaction generated by virtual charge motion.
  • Antinode: A momentum region where the d-wave superconducting gap magnitude is large.
  • Phase stiffness: The energy cost of a spatial twist of the coherent superconducting phase.

A descriptive explanation, step by step

Follow the full argument.

Part 1

1 · Build the physical system before reading its phase diagram

The active electronic layers contain copper and oxygen orbitals. The review emphasizes Cu 3dₓ²₋ᵧ² hybridization with planar O 2p orbitals; the insulating spacer layers also control charge transfer. An odd electron count per unit cell would suggest a partially filled band in a simple one-electron picture. Strong local repulsion instead makes moving charge expensive, while the remaining spins can still fluctuate and order. This is the useful Mott-insulator starting point. A one-band Hubbard description is a reduced model of this Cu–O chemistry, not a complete orbital inventory.

Distinguish the hopping scale t, on-site repulsion U and antiferromagnetic exchange J. Virtual hopping generates J ≈ 4t²/U in the elementary strong-coupling reduction. That expression explains a mechanism and a scale hierarchy; it is not an accurate universal formula for every multiorbital cuprate parameter. Doping introduces mobile charge without automatically removing the strong correlations. Long-range antiferromagnetism can disappear while substantial short-range dynamical spin correlations remain.

Part 2

2 · Read Figure 2 without turning every line into a transition

The horizontal axis is holes per CuO₂ plane; the vertical axis is temperature. Follow a fixed-doping vertical trajectory to ask which changes appear on cooling. The superconducting dome identifies where coherent d-wave superconductivity develops. Underdoped and overdoped mean below and above the doping of maximum Tc for the material under discussion. They do not name two universal compositions shared by every family.

The dashed onset curves describe precursor correlations or fluctuations; T* marks a pseudogap crossover in this schematic. They should not all be interpreted as thermodynamic singularities. Spin order, charge order, superconductivity and the pseudogap overlap rather than occupying mutually exclusive boxes. The diagram synthesizes several materials and measurements. Its arrows and line positions are a historical organizing picture, not a calibrated phase boundary for a specific LBCO or LSCO sample.

Part 3

3 · Follow the argument for d-wave pairing

A repulsive interaction can favor pairing when its momentum dependence is strong and the gap changes sign between strongly coupled regions. Antiferromagnetic scattering near Q = (π/a, π/a) connects antinodal regions with opposite signs of the dₓ²₋ᵧ² gap. The negative sign in the gap equation allows such repulsive scattering to support a positive pairing eigenvalue. This is why a featureless on-site attraction is not required. The diagonal nodes are zeros of the gap, not missing lattice sites.

The review compares weak-coupling and t–J perspectives, while stressing the lack of a controlled general solution at the intermediate coupling relevant to cuprates. Numerical support for d-wave tendencies and close competing energies is evidence about models. Using measured spin spectra as pairing glue can be productive, but the same electrons form both the pairs and the fluctuations. Phonons, nonlocal Coulomb interactions and disorder can still influence Tc; electronic pairing does not imply that the lattice is irrelevant.

Part 4

4 · Separate a spectral gap from phase coherence

A superconducting condensate has a complex amplitude and a coherent phase. Superfluid stiffness measures the energy cost of twisting that phase and is related to magnetic screening. In underdoped materials the stiffness is small, so a large pairing-related energy scale does not guarantee a comparably high ordering temperature. This makes precursor pairing plausible, but the review does not equate all of T* with superconducting fluctuations.

The pseudogap suppresses low-energy spectral weight most strongly near the antinodes. ARPES and tunnelling reveal a nodal–antinodal distinction and apparent Fermi arcs. An arc in a spectral intensity map is not automatically an open, conventional quasiparticle Fermi surface; broadening, coherence factors and reconstruction complicate the interpretation. The review explicitly describes tensions between probes. Charge order can compete with uniform superconductivity, while proposed pair-density-wave order provides a route to spatially modulated pairing and frustrated interlayer phase coupling.

Part 5

5 · What the strange metal discussion establishes

Extended temperature-linear resistivity and incoherent spectral response challenge a simple weakly scattered quasiparticle description. Quantum critical scaling is one proposed explanation. The review asks which order parameter would be critical, whether its correlation length grows as required, and whether a single critical fan can cover the full observed range. A straight resistivity curve alone answers none of those questions.

Holographic descriptions are introduced as ways to investigate strongly interacting systems and generate useful questions. Their mathematical control applies to particular theories, not directly to a solved microscopic cuprate Hamiltonian. Keep three levels separate when taking notes: observations such as spectral suppression; phenomenology such as a scaling law; and a microscopic explanation. The review is strongest as a map of these relationships and unresolved problems, rather than as a final solution to high-temperature superconductivity.

Part 6

6 · Why the same Cu–O plane gives several different materials

The common plane does not make every cuprate interchangeable. Spacer layers set the charge transfer, the number of planes in a structural block can change, and the surrounding lattice influences disorder and interlayer coupling. The review deliberately emphasizes shared electronic physics while also showing why translating its schematic into a sample-specific temperature is unsafe. First identify the chemical family, then determine how its carrier density was calibrated, and then compare measurements made under similar fields and temperatures. A nominal substitution fraction is a preparation variable; holes per plane is the electronic variable used on the review's phase diagram.

This distinction is particularly useful when connecting the review to the materials guides. A low-temperature tetragonal deformation in a La-based compound can pin stripe orientation. YBCO contains an orthorhombic background and has charge-order wavevectors whose doping dependence differs from the La-based family. In the cited La-based stripe examples, charge modulation accompanies incommensurate spin order. In the YBCO examples discussed here, charge order need not have coincident static magnetic order. A common label such as charge order therefore describes a symmetry feature without identifying an identical microscopic state in all compounds.

Nor does losing static antiferromagnetism imply losing its interaction scale. Neutron and resonant inelastic X-ray measurements find substantial dynamical magnetic response after long-range order is suppressed. The relevant comparison is the spectral weight at a stated momentum and energy, not a yes-or-no magnetism label. This provides a bridge from the parent insulator to spin-fluctuation pairing, while leaving the quantitative pairing mechanism open.

Part 7

7 · Follow a single electron through ARPES and tunnelling

ARPES measures an occupied-electron removal spectrum resolved in momentum and energy. A narrow peak indicates a relatively coherent excitation; a broad spectrum indicates that the simple long-lived quasiparticle picture is less useful there. In the review, nodal regions near the zone diagonals retain more coherence than antinodal regions near the zone faces. The pseudogap is a loss of low-energy weight most pronounced in the latter regions. These statements describe a distribution around momentum space, not a single scalar gap assigned equally to every electron.

Below Tc, antinodal spectra can develop a Bogoliubov-like peak and backbending dispersion. That connects the superconducting state to familiar particle–hole mixing, even though the normal state above it was highly incoherent. The review emphasizes a puzzling relation between the emerging peak's weight and superfluid density. The coherence of the whole condensate and the visibility of a single-electron excitation appear connected, but the article does not derive a universally applicable equation equating them.

Scanning tunnelling spectroscopy measures a local spectral response at a chosen bias. A Fourier transform can reveal modulations arising from quasiparticle interference, charge order, or both. Dispersing peaks that move with bias and nondispersing modulations play different roles in the discussion. Crucially, the review records tensions between ARPES and tunnelling interpretations of antinodal coherence. Reading both probes as direct photographs of one identical object would erase this central unresolved issue. A successful explanation must account for their different measurement kernels, resolution, sample contexts, and the observed energy dependence.

Part 8

8 · What makes the orders intertwined

An order parameter records a particular collective pattern: superconductivity has a coherent complex pair amplitude, a charge-density wave has a spatial density modulation, and a nematic has unequal responses along otherwise equivalent axes. They can influence each other through the same electrons and through symmetry-allowed couplings. Calling them intertwined captures those relationships; it does not assert that every order starts at exactly T* or shares one measured amplitude.

The X-ray examples are a useful concrete test of competition. Charge-order intensity increases on cooling toward Tc and then decreases inside the superconducting state. This is evidence that developing superconductivity suppresses that charge-order signal. It does not alone prove microscopic phase separation, nor does it show that every charge fluctuation harms pairing. The review also discusses pair-density-wave proposals, in which the superconducting pair amplitude itself varies in space and reverses sign between neighboring stripes. If stripe directions alternate between planes, simple interlayer Josephson locking can be frustrated. The important prediction is a separation between strong in-plane pairing and weak three-dimensional coherence.

Current-order proposals are different again. They can preserve translations while producing an intra-unit-cell magnetic pattern. The review reports neutron evidence motivating such proposals, but also stresses the absence of expected signatures in local magnetic probes. That mismatch is scientifically important, not an inconvenient detail to omit. The supplied article treats pair-density-wave and orbital-current interpretations as developing proposals with evidential qualifications. Its publication date is part of the interpretation: these are the arguments assembled in 2015.

Part 9

9 · What a controlled calculation would have to explain

A useful microscopic model should reproduce more than the existence of a d-wave eigenfunction. It should explain the energy scales, doping trends, spectral coherence, competing patterns, and why ordering temperatures differ across materials. The review lists several numerical approaches because no one method removes every difficulty. Exact diagonalization treats a small finite Hilbert space accurately but limits spatial scales. Monte Carlo encounters the fermionic sign problem. Cluster methods approximate spatial correlations beyond a single site; DMRG is especially useful on chains and strips but does not turn a narrow strip into an unconstrained two-dimensional solution.

Agreement between approaches on a pairing symmetry is valuable because it survives changes in approximation. Near-degeneracy of superconducting and stripe states is equally valuable: small changes in a model or lattice environment can select very different outcomes when their energies are close. This explains why the review warns against increasing U indefinitely and treating the result as ever stronger superconductivity. The physically relevant intermediate regime lacks a generally controlled small parameter.

The overdoped discussion illustrates a second discipline: separate momentum ranges. Quantum oscillations and sharp ARPES peaks support a more conventional Fermi-surface description. Suppressed neutron weight near the antiferromagnetic vector can suggest weaker pairing-relevant fluctuations, yet the resonant X-ray response at smaller momenta shows that correlations have not simply vanished everywhere. A theory claiming that overdoping turns off all magnetism would miss that distinction. The review's conclusion is a set of constraints and open questions, not a solved universal phase diagram.

Part 10

10 · Read strange-metal claims as a chain of reasoning

The initial observation is that electrical resistivity remains approximately linear over an unusually wide temperature range and that electron spectra can be very broad. Applying a quasiparticle mean-free-path interpretation to sufficiently poor conductivity then becomes inconsistent with the assumptions that made that interpretation possible. This is a failure of that description, rather than a literal measurement that a well-defined electron travels an impossible distance. The review uses it to motivate collective approaches to transport.

Marginal-Fermi-liquid phenomenology organizes damping in terms of frequency and temperature, but a phenomenology is not yet the underlying Hamiltonian. Quantum criticality offers another framework: scale-invariant fluctuations could connect temperature and frequency through a common scaling variable. Its application to cuprates must identify the critical order, establish the expected correlation behavior, explain how superconductivity hides the low-temperature point, and justify the temperature range. The review asks those questions explicitly. Its discussion of holography seeks new theoretical tools for strong interactions, while acknowledging that the dualities proven mathematically are not derivations for the microscopic cuprate material. That limit belongs next to the attractive analogy.

Part 11

What a new theoretical language can and cannot explain

The review distinguishes a phenomenological description from a microscopic solution. Marginal-Fermi-liquid language organizes an unusual damping spectrum and its energy/temperature dependence, but the reported photoemission agreement is strongest near the nodes; it does not make the incoherent antinodes ordinary quasiparticles. Quantum-critical scaling supplies another organizing possibility, with a temperature-dependent relaxation scale and a fan near a zero-temperature transition. The paper asks which growing correlation length and which order parameter would support that interpretation, particularly when superconductivity conceals the proposed transition. A broad strange-metal region at high temperature is an additional constraint rather than automatic confirmation of a single critical point.

Holographic duality is discussed as a way to reason about certain strongly interacting field theories through a different mathematical description. In those examples scale-invariant metallic phases can emerge without tuning to one isolated point, then develop competing low-temperature states. The authors explicitly state that the theories for which the correspondence is established do not resemble the microscopic electrons of copper oxides. Similar qualitative responses consequently offer ideas and experimental questions, not a derivation of the cuprate Hamiltonian or proof that gravity is the material mechanism. The distinction is useful throughout the review: organize observations, test a mechanism's conditions, and retain what the calculation has not explained.

Essential equations, meaning and assumptions

Δ(k) = (Δ₀/2)[cos(kₓa) − cos(kᵧa)]

Symbols and units: The gap reverses sign under a 90° rotation and vanishes on the zone diagonals. a is the in-plane lattice spacing; Δ₀ sets the amplitude. The formula describes superconducting symmetry, not a universal pseudogap fit.

Physical meaning: The two cosine terms interchange under a ninety-degree rotation, reversing Δ. Nodes occur when they are equal. This gives symmetry and energy units, not a microscopic pairing mechanism.

What the evidence supports—and where it stops

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Why can spin correlations survive after the parent antiferromagnetic order disappears?

Long-range order requires spatially coherent static alignment. Exchange and local correlations can persist as finite-range, time-dependent fluctuations after that alignment is lost.

Does a d-wave gap identify the unique microscopic pairing mechanism?

No. It restricts the symmetry and interaction structure; multiple model descriptions can favor that symmetry.

What additional evidence would strengthen a quantum-critical explanation of linear resistivity?

A identified tuning parameter and candidate order, consistent length and energy scales, scaling of independent probes, and a justified range in which competing explanations are controlled.

Why does an apparently open Fermi arc require care?

The image displays spectral weight. A conventional Fermi surface is a connected boundary, and lifetime broadening, reconstruction and measurement sensitivity can hide parts of that boundary.

What is the direct evidence for competition in the X-ray examples?

The charge-order signal grows toward Tc and is suppressed below it. This constrains how superconductivity changes the observed order, without proving every proposed microscopic arrangement.

Why does increasing U indefinitely not solve the pairing problem?

The relevant intermediate-coupling models have no general controlled solution, and the review notes that very large U can favor other states. Exchange scales and charge motion must both be considered.
