Quenched disorder and vestigial nematicity in the pseudogap regime of the cuprates

Disorder can destroy long-range stripe position while leaving its discrete choice of direction intact. This remaining symmetry breaking is vestigial nematic order.

The research question: What remains of an incommensurate stripe CDW when weak quenched disorder disrupts its long-range order?

In two minutes

The whole guide at a glance

  1. An incommensurate stripe charge-density wave is described by two complex fields, ψx and ψy: the amplitude says how strong, the phase says where the maxima sit.
  2. Weak quenched disorder pins the stripes differently in each region, so global phase coherence — long-range stripe order — is lost for arbitrarily weak disorder in d ≤ 4.
  3. The composite |ψx|² − |ψy|² needs no common phase, so it can still choose x over y: a vestigial nematic with ⟨ψx⟩ = ⟨ψy⟩ = 0.
  4. In layered three-dimensional systems this nematic survives below a nonzero critical disorder strength, which tends to zero in strict 2D.
  5. Pinned (σ²G²) and thermal (TG) scattering both give peaks without coherent order; strain and oxygen-distribution tests are proposed to tell them apart.

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

A stripe needs both an axis and a common position for its density maxima. Frozen disorder can destroy the common position while leaving one axis statistically preferred. This paper constructs that remnant as the difference between the strengths of two possible stripe directions. A weakly coupled stack of layers can retain this discrete nematic order at finite disorder, although an isolated two-dimensional random-field layer cannot. Scattering then contains both fluctuating and pinned contributions, so peaks alone do not decide which order survives. The authors propose strain and oxygen quadrupolar tests of the remaining orientation. Their model explains a possible relationship between disorder, stripes and nematicity; it does not establish a universal cause of the cuprate pseudogap.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Complex amplitude: A field with both a magnitude and a phase that locates a density modulation.
  • Quenched disorder: A spatial random potential held fixed while the electronic variables equilibrate.
  • Vestigial order: A simpler broken symmetry remaining after a more elaborate parent order loses coherence.
  • Incommensurate CDW: A charge modulation whose sliding phase is not restricted to a finite set of lattice positions.

Part 1

Research question and system

What remains of an incommensurate stripe CDW when weak quenched disorder disrupts its long-range order? A layered tetragonal model favoring a unidirectional incommensurate CDW, with weak interplane coupling and Gaussian random fields. Cuprate experiments motivate the model; the LTT structure of LBCO explicitly favors one orientation per plane. Landau–Ginzburg–Wilson theory, replicas, a large-component self-consistent Gaussian/saddle-point approximation, and a correspondence with random-field Ising nematic order.

Part 2

Understand the composite order before the disorder diagram

The stripe fields ψx and ψy describe translational order at finite wavevector. Their mean amplitudes can vanish when random pinning destroys global phase coherence. The composite difference of their squared amplitudes can still choose x over y, because it does not require a common translational phase. This is vestigial nematicity: the discrete orientational remnant of a more complicated density-wave order, not necessarily a directly distorted quasiparticle Fermi surface.

Thermal and pinned contributions enter the model structure factor differently, through TG and σ²G². Finite-width peaks can thus survive without clean long-range order. The difference between continuous incommensurate phase symmetry and discrete orientational symmetry explains their distinct disorder sensitivity. Layer coupling matters: the critical nematic disorder vanishes toward strict two dimensions, and the authors identify a misleading 2D ordered saddle-point solution as an approximation artifact. The proposed oxygen quadrupolar tests concern spatial variations of local charge environments and are not measurements reported in this theoretical paper. Part 3

Results and interpretation

For the incommensurate random-field CDW considered, arbitrarily weak disorder removes conventional long-range stripe order in d ≤ 4. Its discrete nematic component can still order in a layered three-dimensional system below a nonzero critical disorder strength.

A vestigial state can have ⟨ψx⟩ = ⟨ψy⟩ = 0 but unequal average squared amplitudes. The paper's model combines thermal scattering TG with a disorder-pinning term σ²G²; finite-width scattering peaks need not uniquely reveal the clean-limit stripe or checkerboard pattern.

The nematic critical disorder tends to zero as interplane coupling vanishes. The authors identify the saddle point's ordered solution in strict 2D with disorder as an artifact. They propose strain-response tests and comparisons of in-plane oxygen NMR quadrupolar broadening.

Authors' proposal: look for the more robust vestigial symmetry to infer tendencies of an ideal clean system. They argue this may help interpret pseudogap CDW and nematic observations; it is not a proof that every cuprate pseudogap is caused by one nematic mechanism.

Part 4

What the two density waves contain

Write the slowly varying density modulation as the sum of a component near a wavevector along x and one near a wavevector along y. Each component is complex: its magnitude describes the strength of the modulation, while its phase says where a maximum sits relative to the lattice. Moving the pattern changes that phase. Rotating the pattern exchanges the two components. These are different operations, so the loss of one kind of order need not remove the other. A checkerboard would use both directions coherently; the model considered here instead has interactions that favor choosing one direction. That preference is an assumption of the Hamiltonian, not an inference from every observed scattering peak.

The nematic variable is built from the difference of the two local squared magnitudes, averaged over the system. It measures an intensity imbalance without demanding a common phase. A phase-disordered collection of locally stronger x stripes can consequently have no coherent density-wave expectation value and still distinguish x from y. This is why the adjective vestigial describes a precise symmetry relation, rather than merely a weaker version of a scattering peak.

Part 5

Why a frozen random field is especially disruptive

The disorder is quenched: its spatial configuration is fixed while the electronic degrees of freedom equilibrate. A local random potential couples directly to density modulation and rewards placing a stripe maximum at one location rather than another. Different regions receive incompatible instructions. An incommensurate stripe can slide continuously, and that continuous phase is unusually susceptible to this competition. The source uses the random-field dimensional argument to rule out conventional long-range incommensurate CDW order at arbitrarily small disorder in four or fewer spatial dimensions. This statement concerns the specified continuous translational order. A commensurate pattern locked to a discrete set of lattice positions has a different symmetry and cannot be assigned the same conclusion automatically.

Choosing x rather than y leaves a discrete, Ising-like alternative. In a genuinely three-dimensional layered system, a weak enough effective random field can leave this orientational order intact. Interplane coupling is therefore consequential even when most microscopic electronic motion is planar. Reducing that coupling toward zero makes the critical disorder for nematic order vanish. The source explicitly rejects a spurious ordered solution of its approximate saddle point in strict two dimensions.

Part 6

Read scattering as two contributions to the same peak

The model scattering function contains a thermal contribution proportional to T times a response propagator G, and a frozen-disorder contribution proportional to the disorder variance times G squared. Both are strongest where the inverse response is small. The squared propagator can make the pinned contribution look particularly sharp even without a coherent macroscopic density wave. A finite correlation length therefore produces a broadened peak, but the visual presence of a peak alone does not establish long-range order. Nor does an equal pair of x and y peaks in a field of view establish a checkerboard: domains of two stripe orientations can also contribute to that average.

A nematic imbalance shifts the effective inverse responses of the two channels in opposite directions. Their widths and intensities then differ. Temperature, pinning strength and the self-consistently determined response scale all matter; the source is not simply reducing a fixed peak amplitude as disorder increases. The large-component calculation supplies a controlled organizational scheme for these coupled quantities, while the exact positions of its phase boundaries remain model and approximation dependent. Scattering is an observable consequence of the theory, not a direct photographic measurement of its fluctuating complex fields.

Part 7

The phase diagram and what a symmetry-breaking field changes

Figure 1 holds the microscopic model parameters fixed and varies temperature and mean-squared disorder. In the clean weakly layered example, translational stripe order and orientational order disappear at distinct temperatures. Introducing disorder destroys the conventional incommensurate stripe order, leaving a finite range in which the nematic remnant survives. At sufficiently large disorder that remnant is lost too. The axes are not a universal temperature-versus-doping phase diagram for the cuprates. Changing layer coupling or the interactions changes the topology and transition character, and the paper discusses a stronger-coupling case in which the clean transitions merge.

Orthorhombic strain acts as a uniform field conjugate to nematic orientation. It explicitly favors one direction, just as a magnetic field favors an Ising magnetization. An intensity imbalance in such a field is therefore not, by itself, spontaneous symmetry breaking. Measuring how sensitively the imbalance changes with a small controlled field can be more informative. Slow domain equilibration introduces another practical issue: a measured crossover can depend on cooling rate when a random-field system becomes sluggish. A rounded experimental feature must be interpreted with both explicit crystal anisotropy and equilibration in mind.

Part 8

Why oxygen NMR is proposed and what remains unproved

The proposed NMR test focuses on the inequivalent oxygen environments associated with the two Cu–O bond directions. A quadrupolar frequency is sensitive to the local electric field gradient and hence to the local charge environment. Disorder produces a distribution of those environments, so the widths as well as the average positions of the two oxygen responses may carry information. The source distinguishes a mean oxygen charge difference from the difference in the variances of local charge distributions. These are separate quantities: a vanishing mean need not imply identical distributions. Controlled strain could select a preferred axis and help compare the two oxygen responses.

These are proposed diagnostic experiments, not oxygen spectra measured in this paper. Broadening also requires instrumental and other microscopic contributions to be considered. The theoretical correspondence to a random-field Ising description supports the survival of a symmetry remnant more broadly than the particular Gaussian calculation, but it does not identify the origin of the whole pseudogap. The assumed clean tendency is toward stripes; the paper does not derive that tendency from a realistic multiband cuprate Hamiltonian. Its external Supporting Information is absent from the supplied PDF and its detailed calculations are outside this guide's verified source coverage.

What the evidence supports—and where it stops

  • The model favors stripes over checkerboards and treats an incommensurate CDW. Commensurate CDW order breaks translations discretely and has a different disorder response.
  • Detailed phase boundaries use large-component and saddle-point approximations. The cited Supporting Information is not contained in this six-page PDF and was not independently reviewed. Strain, lattice orthorhombicity and slow equilibration can round observed signatures.

What is the central research question?

What remains of an incommensurate stripe CDW when weak quenched disorder disrupts its long-range order?

What is measured or assumed?

Landau–Ginzburg–Wilson theory, replicas, a large-component self-consistent Gaussian/saddle-point approximation, and a correspondence with random-field Ising nematic order.

What limits the interpretation?

The model favors stripes over checkerboards and treats an incommensurate CDW. Commensurate CDW order breaks translations discretely and has a different disorder response. Detailed phase boundaries use large-component and saddle-point approximations. The cited Supporting Information is not contained in this six-page PDF and was not independently reviewed. Strain, lattice orthorhombicity and slow equilibration can round observed signatures.

Why can an intensity difference survive a random stripe phase?

The phase disappears from |ψ|². An excess of one direction's squared magnitude does not require the maxima in different patches to coincide.

Does the finite-disorder nematic region apply to one isolated plane?

No. The critical disorder tends to zero with interplane coupling, and the source warns that the ordered strict-2D saddle point is spurious.

What extra evidence does controlled strain provide?

It tests the response to a conjugate orienting field. A finite anisotropy under that field alone cannot establish spontaneous symmetry breaking.
