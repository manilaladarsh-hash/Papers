Quasi-one-dimensional dynamics and nematic phases in the two-dimensional Emery model

A square-symmetric Cu–O model can produce one-dimensional charge motion and spontaneously choose rows over columns.

The research question: Can a microscopic model with fourfold symmetry be shown to support a nematic ground state in a controlled limit?

In two minutes

The whole guide at a glance

  1. The three-band Emery model has one Cu site per unit cell and two bond-centred oxygens, O_x and O_y; favouring one oxygen set breaks rotation without a new period.
  2. With strong repulsion and no direct O–O hopping, a hole moves freely along a row or a column, but turning a corner costs an energy related to V_pp.
  3. At small doping the x² crossing penalty can beat the x³ one-dimensional Fermi-pressure gain, so holes prefer all rows or all columns — a nematic.
  4. At the ideal x = 1 endpoint (total hole count 1 + x per cell) the ground state is a translation-preserving nematic insulator.
  5. This is an existence argument in a controlled limit: adding O–O and higher-order hopping and Coulomb effects needs new stability checks, and hole and electron doping differ.

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

A copper–oxygen plane can be square-symmetric while its electronic state chooses one bond direction. This paper proves that possibility in a strong-repulsion limit of a three-band model. With direct oxygen–oxygen hopping removed, turning a corner costs energy and low-energy charge motion becomes effectively one-dimensional. At low extra-hole density, avoiding crossings can outweigh the kinetic advantage of using both directions. At another ideal filling, two translation-preserving oxygen configurations form a nematic insulator. These mechanisms establish an existence result for electronic nematicity. Real materials have additional hopping, spin exchange and long-range forces, so the result is a starting point for stability tests rather than a complete measured cuprate phase diagram.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Emery model: A Cu–O model retaining one copper and two bond-centered oxygen orbitals per unit cell.
  • Strong-coupling projection: Restriction to low-energy configurations after large repulsion costs are excluded.
  • tpp: Direct oxygen–oxygen hopping energy, set to zero in the controlled limit.
  • Vpp: Repulsive energy between nearby oxygen occupations.

Part 1

Research question and system

Can a microscopic model with fourfold symmetry be shown to support a nematic ground state in a controlled limit? The three-band Emery model on a Cu–O plane: Cu sites form a square lattice and O sites occupy bond centers. x is additional holes per Cu unit cell, with total hole count 1 + x; this model's x = 1 endpoint is not ordinary experimental cuprate doping. Project into a low-energy subspace at strong repulsion with direct O–O hopping tpp = 0. Prove row/column quasiparticle conservation to leading order and exploit one-dimensional solutions; map a classical limit to an Ising model.

PDF p. 1

Part 2

Read the controlled limit as an existence argument

The three-band model includes copper and bond-centered oxygen sites, so choosing horizontal versus vertical oxygen participation can break rotational symmetry without introducing a new translation period. The projection to strong repulsion and vanishing direct oxygen–oxygen hopping simplifies charge trajectories. Conservation of the allowed row and column quasiparticle structures is part of that projected theory, rather than a generic law of every Cu–O plane.

At small x compare powers before comparing coefficients: the crossing penalty scales as x² while the one-dimensional Fermi-pressure term scales as x³. For sufficiently small x the former dominates if its coefficient is positive, making a directional configuration favorable. This is a useful mechanism, not a numerical phase boundary at experimental optimal doping. The x = 1 nematic insulator is another model endpoint, with total hole count 1 + x. Real oxygen hopping, higher-order motion and long-range Coulomb forces change the ideal limit; their inclusion requires a fresh stability calculation.

Part 3

Results and interpretation

A hole attempting to bend from a row into a column incurs a Vpp-related energy cost. In the strict strong-coupling, tpp = 0 limit, charge dynamics is confined to rows or columns; the relevant conserved quantities include quasiparticle structure, not just bare hole counts.

At x = 1, uniform unit cells choose horizontal or vertical oxygen occupation. The two ground states break 90° rotational symmetry, preserve translations and have a charge gap: a nematic insulator.

At small x, the fully nematic energy contains a Fermi-pressure term W x³. An isotropic arrangement gains Fermi-pressure energy but adds an effective crossing repulsion Veff x²; at sufficiently small x that repulsion favors nematicity. Quantum effects stabilize uniform nematic regimes near x = 0 and x = 1.

Authors' conclusion: nematicity exists in a controlled strong-coupling regime of a plausible cuprate model. This establishes a mechanism and an existence result, rather than demonstrating that real cuprates occupy this exact parameter regime.

Part 4

The copper and oxygen degrees of freedom

The Emery model keeps more microscopic information than a single-band square lattice. Each unit cell has one copper orbital and two oxygen orbitals on the horizontal and vertical bonds. The Hamiltonian includes an energy difference between copper and oxygen holes, hopping between neighboring Cu and O sites, and several repulsive interactions. Ud and Up penalize two holes on the same copper or oxygen orbital. Vpd and Vpp penalize close Cu–O and O–O occupation. In the regime used for the argument these repulsions are large compared with the kinetic scales, so many local configurations are energetically excluded. The model is nevertheless symmetric under exchanging the two oxygen directions.

At the parent count there is one hole per copper unit cell. The paper's x adds holes to that count, so the total is 1 + x. This definition is essential when reading its second endpoint: x = 1 is an ideal model filling with two holes per cell, not a claim that conventional cuprates can be doped to that value. Directional oxygen occupation can make the electronic state anisotropic without requiring a larger unit cell or putting literal elongated particles on the lattice.

Part 5

Why a bend costs energy

Set direct oxygen–oxygen hopping to zero and project onto configurations that avoid the large repulsion costs. A charge can move along a sequence of Cu–O bonds within a row or column. Turning a corner requires passing through an unfavorable local oxygen configuration and paying a Vpp-related cost. The geometry of allowed low-energy moves then makes the dynamics effectively one-dimensional despite the two-dimensional lattice. The source gives conserved row and column quantities for these projected quasiparticles; this statement is more careful than saying every bare hole in the full Hamiltonian permanently belongs to a row.

The local configurations in Figure 2 show how both singly and doubly occupied structures enter the counting. Their interpretation depends on the strong-coupling projection and on the inequalities among the Coulomb interactions established in the appendix. Small corrections outside the projected limit allow additional processes and eventual transverse motion. An existence proof can remain valuable even when those corrections change the lowest-energy dynamics: one first shows how a symmetric microscopic Hamiltonian can choose an axis, then asks which added terms destabilize that choice. It does not follow that real Cu–O transport is exactly one-dimensional.

Part 6

Two ways to understand the fully nematic endpoint

At x = 1, the ideal ground state has all unit cells choose the horizontal oxygen sector or all choose the vertical sector. There are two equivalent choices. Exchanging the axes maps one to the other, while translating by a unit cell leaves either state unchanged. The endpoint therefore has nematic rotational symmetry breaking without translational charge order. It is an insulator with a charge gap, demonstrating that an electronic nematic need not be a metal or a distorted Fermi surface.

In a classical limit the oxygen occupations can be rewritten as a two-valued orientation variable, leading to an Ising-model description. That variable identifies the favored oxygen direction, not an up or down copper magnetic moment. Its ordering explains a finite-temperature orientational transition in the classical model. The paper then considers how quantum motion modifies the phase diagram near the two endpoints. Figure 3 separates the classical structure from shaded regions where quantum fluctuations stabilize homogeneous states. The drawing does not supply a numerical experimental temperature scale. The controlled limits explain the existence and symmetry of phases rather than fit the entire measured cuprate diagram.

Part 7

The dilute energy comparison in slow motion

A fully aligned state puts the extra mobile holes into parallel one-dimensional channels. Filling those channels produces a positive Fermi-pressure energy proportional to x cubed at low density. An isotropic arrangement distributes the carriers between both directions and lowers that kinetic contribution, but now the perpendicular streams encounter one another. Their effective crossing repulsion adds a term proportional to x squared. For sufficiently small positive x, a positive x-squared cost falls more slowly than the x-cubed kinetic gain. The directional state can consequently win despite losing some kinetic benefit from using both orientations.

The coefficients still matter. This comparison is not a claim that x squared always beats every other energy at every doping, nor is it a numerical prediction for optimal doping. It relies on the sign of the effective interaction and on the dilute expansion being valid. At other fillings the source discusses coexistence and possible phase separation in its ideal model. Long-range Coulomb forces make macroscopic separation of charge expensive and can frustrate it into more complicated textures. That is an additional physical ingredient, not evidence that the displayed uniform nematic calculation automatically proves stripes at intermediate density.

Part 8

Spin physics, particle–hole asymmetry and restoring omitted terms

The leading projected charge dynamics does not exhaust all the physics of copper spins. Spin exchange arises at higher order and can favor magnetic correlations even when charge motion is directional. Thus calling the leading charge sector spin-independent does not mean the original material has no magnetism. The source also emphasizes a particle–hole asymmetry: adding holes to the parent system accesses oxygen-rich configurations subject to the directional constraints, while adding electrons removes copper holes and need not create the same constrained motion. The existence mechanism is therefore tied to the filling and orbital structure.

Direct O–O hopping is an especially important correction because it offers routes absent from the strict proof. The paper quotes real-material estimates that are not negligibly small relative to Cu–O hopping. Higher-order terms can reconnect rows and columns and alter the ideal one-dimensional non-Fermi-liquid behavior at the lowest energies. Whether the discrete nematic symmetry remains broken is a distinct stability question. The conclusion is appropriately limited: a plausible microscopic model supports nematicity in a controlled region of parameter space. Connecting that region quantitatively to a particular compound requires realistic parameters, the omitted terms and independent comparison with experimental observables.

What the evidence supports—and where it stops

  • The paper explicitly notes that cluster estimates give tpp/t about 1/3 in real materials, whereas the proof uses tpp/t → 0. Appendix proofs require additional inequalities among repulsions.
  • Corrections permit two-dimensional hopping and may replace the strictly one-dimensional non-Fermi-liquid behavior at the lowest energies. Weak corrections need not remove nematic symmetry breaking; long-range Coulomb forces also alter phase separation.

What is the central research question?

Can a microscopic model with fourfold symmetry be shown to support a nematic ground state in a controlled limit?

What is measured or assumed?

Project into a low-energy subspace at strong repulsion with direct O–O hopping tpp = 0. Prove row/column quasiparticle conservation to leading order and exploit one-dimensional solutions; map a classical limit to an Ising model.

What limits the interpretation?

The paper explicitly notes that cluster estimates give tpp/t about 1/3 in real materials, whereas the proof uses tpp/t → 0. Appendix proofs require additional inequalities among repulsions. Corrections permit two-dimensional hopping and may replace the strictly one-dimensional non-Fermi-liquid behavior at the lowest energies. Weak corrections need not remove nematic symmetry breaking; long-range Coulomb forces also alter phase separation.

Is the Ising variable at x = 1 a copper spin?

No. It labels horizontal versus vertical oxygen occupation; magnetic exchange is a separate higher-order process.

Why does the x² term matter at low density?

For small positive dimensionless x it falls more slowly than x³. A positive crossing coefficient can dominate the kinetic saving in the dilute expansion.

Does transverse hopping automatically remove nematicity?

No. It removes an exact directional-motion restriction, while the stability of the discrete orientational order requires a separate calculation.
