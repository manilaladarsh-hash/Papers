Anomalous Spin Excitation in the Metallic State of La2-xSrxCuO4 Observed by Cu NQR

A broad Cu NQR spectrum selects locally different relaxation responses; high-temperature departures from Curie-Weiss behavior cannot be read from one universal line.

The research question: How do underdoped LSCO spin dynamics depend on temperature and position within the NQR spectrum?

In two minutes

The whole guide at a glance

  1. Planar Cu NQR relaxation is followed up to 700 K in underdoped LSCO (x = 0.08, 0.10, 0.12) and compared with x = 0.06, 0.15 and 0.20.
  2. The spectrum mixes A and B electric environments, each with ⁶³Cu and ⁶⁵Cu peaks, so several peaks do not mean several magnetic phases.
  3. Within the A line, lower-frequency nuclei relax faster than higher-frequency ones; the contrast is strongest at low doping and small near x = 0.15.
  4. T₁T follows a straight Curie–Weiss baseline when hot; the departure on cooling defines T*, which falls with doping and stays well above Tc.
  5. The authors read the suppression as a pseudospin gap in a single-layer cuprate, while insisting that Sr disorder and local inhomogeneity matter.

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

A broad Cu NQR spectrum selects locally different relaxation responses; high-temperature departures from Curie-Weiss behavior cannot be read from one universal line. LSCO has a single CuO2 layer, so a suppression of low-frequency spin fluctuations in this system challenges explanations requiring a bilayer. The samples are checked by x-ray diffraction and superconducting onset is determined through the NQR probe-coil response. The central question is not just whether a relaxation maximum exists above Tc. It is whether the high-temperature baseline is left at a doping-dependent crossover, and whether one measured rate represents the whole sample.

Terms to understand before starting
  • LSCO: layered La2-xSrxCuO4; nominal x is Sr per formula unit
  • Local probe: a distribution can matter even when the bulk average barely changes
NQR
Nuclear quadrupole resonance selected by the local electric-field gradient without an applied magnetic field.
Electric-field gradient
Spatial variation of the electric field at a nucleus; sensitive to structure and electronic charge.
Hyperfine form factor
Momentum-dependent weighting of electronic spin fluctuations at a selected nuclear site.
T1
Longitudinal spin-lattice recovery time of nuclear populations.
T2
Timescale governing loss of transverse echo coherence; it limits signal accessibility.
A/B environment
Two spectrally distinct local Cu quadrupole environments, each also containing isotope contributions.
Knight shift
Field-induced resonance displacement with spin and orbital contributions.

Departure differs from a maximum

Look for: A pseudospin-gap effect in this paper means that low-frequency relaxation becomes suppressed relative to its high-temperature antiferromagnetic baseline.

A pseudospin-gap effect in this paper means that low-frequency relaxation becomes suppressed relative to its high-temperature antiferromagnetic baseline. That definition is more specific than saying that a curve falls on cooling, and broader than insisting on a pronounced maximum. Plotting T1T reveals the expected approximately linear Curie-Weiss baseline and shows where the measured curve departs. The source then compares how the departure scale evolves across doping. A visible maximum can move or weaken as local environments are mixed, while a systematic departure from the reference still remains.

The underlying question is whether the low-frequency spectral weight evolves as the simple high-temperature account predicts.

NQR uses the electric-field gradient at a quadrupolar nucleus to select a resonance without an external field. The gradient describes how the local electric potential changes around that nucleus; electronic occupations, neighboring ions and structural distortions can all affect it. A shift in NQR frequency therefore identifies a changed local electric environment before it establishes a changed hole concentration. Once a frequency slice is selected, the recovery of nuclear magnetization probes the fluctuations that exchange energy with those nuclei.

The distinction explains why the article examines both spectral shape and recovery instead of assigning a magnetic phase to every peak.

Part 2

Electric gradients and the A/B spectrum

The zero-field spectra are decomposed into A and B environments, each with 63Cu and 65Cu isotope peaks. NQR frequency depends on the nuclear quadrupole moment and local electric-field-gradient tensor. The A peak shifts upward with Sr concentration, while the broad linewidth varies much less over the doped range than a simple proportional lattice-distortion explanation would suggest. A lightly substituted metallic sample is already much broader than undoped La2CuO4. The authors associate this with a broad local electronic contribution to the gradient, but do not claim that frequency is a direct image of a stripe pattern or that the A/B microscopic assignment has been uniquely settled.

Part 3

Frequency selects different spin dynamics

Short excitation pulses select a narrow frequency slice compared with the full broad line. Across the A resonance, 1/T1 decreases as frequency rises, and this contrast is especially strong in lower-doping samples. Near optimal x = 0.15 it becomes small within accuracy. The authors examine contamination from the overlapping 65Cu B line and argue that its expected doping trend does not explain the observed effect. The observation therefore ties the inhomogeneous gradient distribution to locally varying spin dynamics. It remains an inference about a distribution of electronic environments, rather than proof that every local environment corresponds to one precisely known hole number.

Part 4

The crossover is defined by a baseline departure

For high-temperature measurements the apparatus tracks the moving A-site peak, reducing bias from the frequency dependence. The observed 1/T1 is not universally constant across the samples and often has a broad maximum around 200 to 300 K. Plotting T1T makes the high-temperature Curie-Weiss baseline linear. Its fit gives doping-dependent C and θ, and a downward-temperature departure defines T*. In underdoped samples T* falls as hole concentration rises, remaining well above Tc. A simplistic exchange-narrowed local-moment calculation overestimates the rate and does not capture the observed doping dependence. The source consequently compares nearly antiferromagnetic-metal and t-J/RVB descriptions.

Part 5

Interpretation without turning a crossover into a transition

The authors call the suppression relative to the Curie-Weiss expectation a pseudospin-gap effect. They find its doping trend qualitatively similar to a singlet-RVB scale and to underdoped YBCO, but point out failures in parts of the competing calculations. The high-temperature C variation is not reproduced by a model with a doping-independent exchange parameter alone. Moreover, a clear maximum of 1/T1T is not required to identify the departure; the local inhomogeneity can alter its shape. The paper ends by emphasizing that Sr-associated disorder and locally different spin environments cannot be neglected. This is a historical model comparison based on low-frequency NQR, not a complete measurement of the excitation spectrum.

Part 6

How to compare the evidence

Read a frequency-resolved relaxation plot as a comparison within one crystal or powder composition, not only between different nominal dopings. The excitation bandwidth selects a narrow portion of an inhomogeneous line. A rate that changes across that line is therefore additional information beyond its total width. Here the authors explicitly test whether overlapping A/B and isotope lines can account for the trend. Their argument favors local electronic variation, but NQR frequency still combines local charge, lattice and electric-field-gradient contributions. It should not be converted into a literal hole count without a calibration. When comparing T* across papers, also check its operational definition: departure from a high-temperature extrapolation need not occur at the same temperature as a maximum in 1/(T1T).

Part 7

Electric frequency and magnetic relaxation answer different questions

NQR uses the electric-field gradient at a quadrupolar nucleus to select a resonance without an external field. The gradient describes how the local electric potential changes around that nucleus; electronic occupations, neighboring ions and structural distortions can all affect it. A shift in NQR frequency therefore identifies a changed local electric environment before it establishes a changed hole concentration. Once a frequency slice is selected, the recovery of nuclear magnetization probes the fluctuations that exchange energy with those nuclei. This second measurement can be predominantly magnetic even though the first selection was electric. The distinction explains why the article examines both spectral shape and recovery instead of assigning a magnetic phase to every peak.

Magnetic relaxation is filtered. The electronic spin fluctuations must produce a field at the nucleus, so hyperfine couplings and the geometry of neighboring spins weight the momentum-dependent response. Different nuclei, orientations or local environments can weight the same electronic system differently. A large Cu rate does not automatically mean a similarly large uniform susceptibility, because antiferromagnetic fluctuations can dominate its weighted sum. Conversely, two unequal rates need not mean two unequal carrier densities if their couplings differ. The scientifically informative step is to combine a charge-sensitive environment label with its measured spin response, keeping the selection and the inferred electronic cause separate.

Part 8

Nuclear recovery and echo survival are separate clocks

Spin-lattice recovery describes how the populations of nuclear levels return toward equilibrium after excitation. Its timescale is T1. Echo decay describes how the transverse spin signal loses coherence before it can be detected; its characteristic times involve T2 and sometimes separate Gaussian and exponential components. A nucleus can have a measurable recovery only if its echo survives the pulse sequence and receiver dead time. This means that the published rate may describe a selected subset of nuclei rather than every Cu in the material. Rapid fluctuations, slow glassy fields, broad frequency distributions and overlap can affect those two measurements differently.

A recovery with several components also needs assignment before interpretation. Multiple nuclear levels, two Cu isotopes, overlapping A and B resonances, or different electronic environments can all produce more than one time dependence. A fitted exponential coefficient is not automatically the volume fraction of an electronic phase. When comparing different temperatures, check whether the spectrometer follows the moving resonance and whether the excitation bandwidth samples the same relative part of the line. When comparing different papers, check the pulse separation, frequency choice and fitted recovery convention. These procedural details explain why an apparent disagreement between rates can expose a selection effect rather than a contradiction about the whole spin system.

Part 9

Walk through the frequency-selection experiment

The source does something more discriminating than measuring a broad spectrum and declaring inhomogeneity. It selects different frequencies inside the A line of the same nominal composition and measures the recovery of each slice. Lower-frequency A nuclei relax faster than higher-frequency A nuclei, with a contrast stronger in the underdoped samples. The control is important: moving across the line does not change the sample's nominal Sr content, temperature or preparation. It changes the selected electric environment. The associated change in magnetic recovery connects electric-gradient variations with electronic spin-response variations in a way that linewidth alone could not establish.

This connection still requires excluding concrete overlap effects. The A and B environments each carry two Cu isotope contributions, so a frequency slice may contain a tail from a different resonance. The article examines the possible 65Cu B contribution and its doping behavior rather than assuming that every nonexponential recovery is a separate electronic domain. Tracking the shifting A maximum in the high-temperature study then avoids mixing a temperature trend with an accidental move to a different frequency slice. These controls are the practical reason the frequency dependence deserves attention before the high-temperature crossover is interpreted.

Part 10

Understand why the crossover need not be a peak

A pseudospin-gap effect in this paper means that low-frequency relaxation becomes suppressed relative to its high-temperature antiferromagnetic baseline. That definition is more specific than saying that a curve falls on cooling, and broader than insisting on a pronounced maximum. Plotting T1T reveals the expected approximately linear Curie-Weiss baseline and shows where the measured curve departs. The source then compares how the departure scale evolves across doping. A visible maximum can move or weaken as local environments are mixed, while a systematic departure from the reference still remains. The underlying question is whether the low-frequency spectral weight evolves as the simple high-temperature account predicts.

The comparison with bilayer cuprates has a clear purpose. A corresponding suppression in single-layer LSCO challenges a mechanism that requires two coupled planes to exist at all. It does not exclude every role of interlayer coupling in another material, nor does it fix the full momentum or energy dependence of LSCO's excitations. The model comparisons retain differences in fitted amplitudes and their doping dependence. Disorder is therefore part of the physics to explain, not a reason to replace the actual local-response distribution with a perfectly homogeneous theoretical curve.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Why distinguish 1/T1 from 1/T1T?

They have different temperature trends; the Curie-Weiss comparison uses the latter.

Does a broad NQR line alone prove stripes?

No. Both lattice and electronic gradient variations, and magnetic effects, require discrimination.

Is T* defined here as Tc?

No. It is a high-temperature baseline departure and remains above superconducting Tc.

Does changing excitation frequency change the specimen doping?

No. It selects different nuclear environments inside the same nominally doped sample.

Why follow the A peak as temperature changes?

The resonance moves, and measuring a fixed frequency could mix the thermal trend with the known frequency-dependent response.

What does the single-layer result challenge?

An explanation that requires bilayer coupling for the suppression to exist; it does not exclude all interlayer effects in other compounds.
