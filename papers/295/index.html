Nickelates provide answers about high-temperature superconductivity — and raise new questions

The supplied August 2025 review compares square-planar and octahedral nickelates as distinct tests of superconducting mechanisms, emphasizing synthesis, orbital filling and unresolved pairing symmetry.

The research question: Which nickelate similarities to cuprates are useful, and which differences prevent a simple one-to-one analogy?

In two minutes

The whole guide at a glance

  1. An August 2025 review uses two nickelate families as tests of cuprate superconductivity ideas: square-planar and octahedral (bilayer/trilayer).
  2. Square-planar RNiO₂ is made by growing an oxygen-rich precursor and chemically removing apical oxygen; carrier doping is a separate step.
  3. A shared nominal d⁹ count does not mean the same physics: holes sit more on Ni, and rare-earth 5d bands can self-dope.
  4. Octahedral La₃Ni₂O₇ superconducts under pressure, and strained bilayer films do so at ambient pressure; interlayer bonding activates a second (d_z²) orbital.
  5. Pairing symmetry remains open: square-planar data are consistent with d-wave, while bilayer models range from sign-changing s to d.

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

Nickelates test which ingredients of cuprate-like superconductivity are essential. This August 2025 review compares reduced square-planar materials, often studied as doped thin films, with octahedral bilayer and trilayer materials accessed through pressure or substrate strain. Similar formal electron counts do not guarantee the same oxygen-hole weight, exchange scale or rare-earth participation. The bilayer can also have an active second nickel orbital coupled between layers. Those differences motivate competing pairing models. The review reports several discoveries and opportunities for new measurements while leaving definitive pairing symmetry and links between the families open. Read its temperatures and sample constraints as a dated research map; the useful result is a set of concrete structural, spectroscopic and magnetic tests.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Square-planar coordination: A nickel–oxygen plane without the apical oxygen environment of the octahedral family.
  • Topotactic reduction: Chemical removal of oxygen from a grown precursor while retaining a related crystal framework.
  • Structural doping: Changing nominal charge balance through the ratio and stacking of layers.
  • Epitaxial strain: Lattice constraints imposed by bonding a thin film to a substrate.

Part 1

Read this as a dated map of a rapidly developing field

Goodge and Norman survey nickelate superconductivity as understood in August 2025. The PDF includes a publication cover, a photograph of a contacted thin film and a title page before the dense body; the small extracted-text counts on those opening pages do not mean the article is an unreadable scan. The review begins with the 2019 square-planar discovery and then contrasts it with pressure-induced superconductivity in octahedral bilayer and trilayer compounds. Its reported temperatures and synthesis milestones are historical statements in that review, not a freshly verified list of today's records. The useful purpose is conceptual comparison: changing coordination, electron filling, dimensional structure and external control gives tests of which ingredients matter for unconventional superconductivity.

Part 2

Why the synthesis route changes the experiment

The desired infinite-layer RNiO2 structure has nominal Ni+ with a d9 count, whereas the more stable RNiO3 precursor has a higher formal Ni valence. The review describes growing the precursor and chemically removing oxygen to obtain square-planar coordination. Substituting divalent ions for trivalent rare-earth ions supplies nominal hole doping; this substitution is a composition choice, not a claim that dopants are added after reduction. An alternative inserts extra rare-earth planes, making finite-layer reduced structures with tunable formal filling. These routes need not produce identical disorder or strain. At the source date, the square-planar superconductors discussed are very thin films, restricting probes that require large sample volumes. High-pressure octahedral crystals face a different experimental constraint: the sample environment inside a diamond-anvil cell limits access. Practical synthesis details therefore shape what can be inferred about intrinsic superconducting properties.

Part 3

Pressure, strain and chemical doping are distinct controls

Octahedral La3Ni2O7 and La4Ni3O10 retain apical oxygen and have nominal nickel counts different from the square-planar d9 parent. The source describes high-pressure superconductivity in these bulk structures and more recent bilayer thin films that superconduct at ambient pressure through epitaxial strain. The latter observation qualifies any blanket claim that all octahedral nickelates require externally applied pressure. Pressure can modify bond distances, angles and orbital overlap without the same formal carrier-count change as chemical substitution. Strain supplies another route to modifying the lattice, but is not numerically identical to hydrostatic pressure in every direction. The review's two phase sketches consequently have different horizontal axes: hole doping for one family and pressure for another. Their similar domes are an organizing picture, not evidence that the axes can be converted directly.

Part 4

A nominal d9 analogy does not fix the low-energy model

The electronic comparison uses oxygen 2p, transition-metal 3d and rare-earth 5d levels. Cuprates have charge-transfer character, with substantial oxygen weight for doped holes. In square-planar nickelates the higher charge-transfer scale shifts more hole weight toward nickel, and rare-earth bands can self-dope the system. Thus the nominally undoped material need not be the same kind of magnetic insulator as a cuprate parent. The review also discusses a smaller superexchange scale in square-planar nickelates, without proving that it alone sets Tc. Octahedral bilayers add an active dz2 sector coupled across layers through apical oxygen, alongside dx2−y2 states. Some models emphasize interlayer dz2 singlets; others treat that sector as comparatively inert. The actual low-energy degrees of freedom are therefore a question constrained by experiment, not settled by formal valence alone.

Part 5

What would settle a mechanism comparison

Gap symmetry is one way to test whether family similarities extend beyond structure. The review reports indirect results consistent with d-wave pairing in square-planar materials but states that the symmetry has not been decisively established. For octahedral bilayers, sign-changing s and d-wave proposals depend sensitively on model parameters and crystalline environment. One should not turn that model sensitivity into a measured ability to switch pairing symmetry at will. Thin-film access at ambient pressure creates new opportunities to measure relevant orbital and gap properties, while intermediate structures could help connect the two families. The final lesson is to track which claims are observations, which are theoretical interpretations and which are suggested future experiments. Similar superconducting domes and transition temperatures motivate a comparison; they do not uniquely identify a common pairing interaction.

Part 6

Why a second transition-metal family is a test rather than a duplicate

The motivation is to identify which ingredients are essential for unconventional superconductivity. Cuprates share copper–oxygen planes and a correlated electronic background, but their common chemistry makes it difficult to vary one central ingredient without changing many others. Nickelates offer a related transition-metal–oxygen platform with different orbital energies and additional bands. A resemblance in formal electron count therefore supplies a useful comparison, not proof that every microscopic feature or pairing mechanism must be identical. The review organizes the discoveries as a set of material tests of that assumption.

It first contrasts conventional lattice-mediated pairing with interaction-based explanations used for magnetic and correlated systems. That contrast is a conceptual starting point, not a claim that the existence of magnetism determines the pairing glue in every new compound. The superconducting nickelate families themselves differ substantially: square-planar reduced materials and octahedral layered materials require different preparation and tuning. Their shared element name does not make their low-energy physics interchangeable. The article is a dated August 2025 synthesis of rapid developments, including discoveries reported just before it appeared. Transition-temperature values, sample limits and unresolved hypotheses in the guide consequently belong to that snapshot. They are explained as the authors' context rather than silently promoted into current records or a completed consensus.

Part 7

Reduction creates a new coordination environment before doping is interpreted

The reduced square-planar structures are obtained through a two-step route. A more stable oxygen-rich perovskite precursor is grown first. Chemical reduction then removes selected oxygen and leaves planes with altered nickel coordination. The desired nominal monovalent nickel configuration is difficult to stabilize directly, so synthesis is not merely an engineering detail after the electronic problem has been solved. Oxygen removal changes local bonding, electron count and orbital splitting at once. Comparing a precursor and product therefore requires their actual structure rather than only their rare-earth formula.

In the infinite-layer limit, NiO2 planes alternate with rare-earth layers. The square-planar coordination is central to the d9 analogy with cuprates. The review discusses chemical substitution of some trivalent rare-earth ions by divalent ions as a way to add holes to this reduced framework. Charge neutrality gives the nominal count, but actual orbital spectral weight can be redistributed by covalency and other bands. The source describes favorable superconducting compositions around a fraction of a hole per Ni rather than requiring the undoped nominal d9 endpoint itself to be a magnetic insulator. The strict thin-film constraints reported in 2025 also limit which experiments can be performed. A nominal formula, reduction route and successful transport transition are different pieces of evidence and should not be treated as a fully known bulk electronic state.

Part 8

Layer counting is another way to change a nominal charge balance

Instead of randomly substituting an ion, structural doping changes how many rare-earth planes occur relative to nickel planes. In the reduced layered family Rn+1NinO2n+2, charge neutrality for trivalent R and divalent oxygen gives an average nickel valence of 1 + 1/n. The infinite-layer limit tends to nominal Ni1+, while finite layer count shifts the nominal filling. This arithmetic explains why inserting a layer can play a doping role even without a random chemical substitution on every equivalent site. It is an elementary count from the stated formula, not a direct measurement of the occupation of one selected orbital.

The synthesis begins with a layered octahedral precursor and removes oxygen to reach the reduced structure. Rock-salt-related blocks interrupt the stacking, making layer number and interface structure active controls. Atomic precision is valuable because unintended intergrowths can give a different average sequence and hence a different nominal valence. The review uses this route to connect the square-planar family with other layered nickelates while emphasizing that similar total counts do not ensure similar low-energy bands. A trilayer reduced material discussed in the article is not reported superconducting merely because a corresponding octahedral trilayer superconducts under pressure. Questions about doping it differently or pressurizing it are proposed future tests. Structural connection supplies a research direction, not an automatic transfer of a measured phase.

Part 9

Pressure and epitaxial strain access related but distinct experiments

The octahedral bilayer La3Ni2O7 and trilayer La4Ni3O10 retain nickel–oxygen octahedra rather than becoming square-planar infinite layers. Their formal nickel electron counts differ from d9, and high-pressure superconductivity appears without applying the same chemical-doping control as the reduced family. Figure 2 accordingly uses pressure in one panel and hole doping in the other. Reading both horizontal axes as carrier fraction would erase the main comparison. Compression changes bonding and orbital overlap, while chemical substitution changes nominal charge balance and can add disorder.

Bulk high-pressure crystals can be prepared directly but need a diamond-anvil environment for their superconducting state. That environment limits access for many spectroscopic or magnetic measurements. Thin films offer a different route: a carefully chosen substrate constrains the film lattice through epitaxial strain. The review includes recently reported ambient-pressure bilayer film superconductivity, so high external pressure is not stated as the only possible octahedral route. Strain can reproduce some consequences of compression while imposing directional lattice constraints and a substrate interface of its own. It is not simply identical to a calibrated hydrostatic pressure. The contrast is valuable because ambient-pressure films could make experiments feasible that are difficult inside a pressure cell. A higher reported Tc alone therefore does not rank an experimentally easier or technologically mature material.

Part 10

Formal d9 similarity meets a different charge-transfer landscape

A charge-transfer energy compares oxygen p and transition-metal d sectors, while U measures the energetic splitting associated with repulsion in the d sector. In the cuprate comparison, oxygen-related states play a major role in the doped-hole weight. The review describes larger charge-transfer separation in square-planar nickelates, moving much of that weight toward nickel. Figure 3 displays schematic distributions and relative energies rather than a measured density-of-states curve for every sample. The actual Fermi-level position can also change with oxygen content, pressure and chemical substitution.

Superexchange still connects metal sites through oxygen, but changing the charge-transfer scale changes its strength. The article discusses a smaller exchange scale in square-planar nickelates and asks whether it helps explain their lower typical Tc. That causal link remains debated in the source. Rare-earth d states add another distinction: their proximity to nickel levels allows self-doping and extra low-energy electronic participation. The nominally undoped reduced material is consequently not simply the same magnetic insulator as the cuprate parent. Calling the two systems d9 does not remove those extra bands or locate each doped hole exactly. The comparison shows why a single-band analogy must be tested against spectroscopy and magnetic information rather than fixed by an oxidation-state label alone.

Part 11

The second active orbital changes the bilayer model question

The square-planar orbital picture places the dz2 sector lower and filled relative to the active dx2−y2 sector. In the octahedral bilayer, apical oxygen bonds and coupling between nickel layers make dz2-derived states central to many proposed models. The review illustrates a splitting of those states and partial filling rather than simply appending a second identical copy of the cuprate band. Pressure can change their overlap, providing a reason that compression might alter both magnetic pairing tendencies and electronic bands. This is an orbital mechanism to investigate, not a direct experimental determination of the pair wavefunction.

Some models emphasize interlayer singlet tendencies and an unconventional sign-changing s-like pair state. Others find d-wave or different solutions depending sensitively on model parameters, and some consider a less active dz2 sector. Those alternatives involve how bonding, filling and the crystalline environment are represented. A symmetry label cannot be assigned by looking only at the orbital cartoon. Nor does the existence of two layers force one universal sign relation on every Fermi-surface sheet. The article treats potentially tunable or multiple pairing states as interesting possibilities rather than established phases. Measuring the actual low-energy orbital participation in the new ambient-pressure films could narrow the parameter choices and distinguish these hypotheses more directly.

Part 12

How to read a mechanism test and an unresolved pairing symmetry

Pairing symmetry concerns the momentum and internal structure of the superconducting order parameter, including its sign. A measured gap magnitude can suggest nodes or strong anisotropy, but does not automatically determine the phase sign. Several indirect results reviewed for square-planar nickelates are consistent with d-wave pairing, while alternatives remain in discussion. Thin-film geometry makes some decisive measurements difficult. The guide therefore retains the source's unresolved status rather than selecting a symmetry because it resembles the cuprates or because one theoretical curve fits a limited observable.

In the bilayer, proposals for sign-changing s-like and d-wave states depend on the active orbital model and interactions. Their relationship to the square-planar family is a central open question, not a contradiction to be concealed. A useful sequence is to establish the specimen's structure and stoichiometry, identify which orbitals contribute near the Fermi level, determine magnetic and gap information, and then test which model reproduces their linked trends. Linear-temperature resistivity, discussed as strange-metal behavior in both families, supplies a further phenomenological similarity without uniquely identifying the pairing mechanism. The article closes by connecting materials synthesis and theoretical predictions: new intermediate structures, chemical tuning and more accessible films can expose conditions under which one explanation succeeds or fails. Those proposed experiments are the research agenda generated by the review, not results already contained in it.
