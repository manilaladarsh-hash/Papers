Superconductor-to-nonsuperconductor transition in (La1−xSrx)2CuO4 as investigated by transport and magnetic measurements

Hall and susceptibility changes accompany the historical superconducting-to-metal crossover; the paper’s Sr variable is half the usual LSCO x.

The research question: How do charge transport and spin response change as oxygen-controlled LSCO crosses its superconducting composition range?

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

Hall and susceptibility changes accompany the historical superconducting-to-metal crossover; the paper’s Sr variable is half the usual LSCO x. Their x counts the Sr fraction on each of two La/Sr sites, so conventional doping z = 2x. Their maximum Tc near x = 0.075 is z = 0.15, and their measurements through x = 0.175 reach z = 0.35. Without this conversion, the paper would appear to place optimal superconductivity at half its actual Sr content. The authors study about 30 compositions with resistivity, Hall and SQUID measurements, emphasizing the high-doping nonsuperconducting metal. The supplied source is a historical 1989 study; its phase boundaries and mechanistic interpretation remain attached to its specimens and conventions.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Composition convention: source x is half conventional LSCO Sr content z = 2x
  • Hall coefficient: transverse electrical response sensitive to Fermi surface and scattering
  • Uniform susceptibility: bulk response to an applied magnetic field
Resistivity ρ
Electric-field-to-current-density ratio in the stated geometry; commonly reported in μΩ cm.
Hall coefficient RH
Transverse transport response per applied magnetic field under a defined sign convention.
Residual resistivity
Temperature-independent term used in a chosen low-temperature transport fit.
Magnetoresistance
Change of resistivity under an applied magnetic field.
Effective exponent
Local logarithmic slope of a fitted or measured temperature-dependent response.
Normal-state continuation
Inferred nonsuperconducting response where zero-field superconductivity prevents direct access.
Nominal doping
Chemical substitution label, which may differ from inferred mobile or effective carrier content.

Keep correlation distinct from cause

Look for: Both transport and spin trends change beyond the superconducting range.

Part 1

Part 2

Preparation controls and the resistive evolution

Spray drying creates a homogeneous acetate mixture before ceramic synthesis. Oxygen annealing, iodometric titration and systematic lattice parameters support near-stoichiometric oxygen content within the reported resolution. High-pressure oxygen comparisons were also made for heavily doped specimens. Very small Sr additions sharply lower resistivity, and high-temperature metallic slopes appear well before bulk superconductivity. At low doping, a low-temperature upturn and hopping-like behavior signal localization. Superconductivity appears near source x = 0.03, peaks near 0.075, and is absent down to 1.8 K above roughly 0.13 in this series. Those correspond to conventional z approximately 0.06, 0.15 and 0.26. Metallic conduction continues when superconductivity is lost, demonstrating that supplying mobile charge alone is insufficient for pairing.

Part 3

The Hall result is not just a carrier counter

At low Sr content the positive Hall coefficient decreases approximately as the inverse doping. With more doping it falls faster than that simple trend, becomes very small and eventually changes sign in the heavily doped metal. A nominal hole dopant can therefore coexist with negative Hall response. The paper checks a heavily doped single crystal with in-plane current and field perpendicular to the layers, finding qualitatively similar behavior to the ceramic result and reducing concern about an orientation-averaging artifact. Hall transport depends on Fermi-surface curvature, mobilities and scattering, so 1/(eRH) cannot automatically be read as the chemical hole density. The source proposes a crossover from a simple doped-Mott picture toward a Fermi-liquid-like electronic state; its Hall data do not uniquely establish that microscopic account.

PDF p. 4 · PDF p. 5

Part 4

Temperature dependence and the spin comparison

In the superconducting compositions the Hall coefficient increases upon cooling despite metallic resistivity; at source x = 0.075 it roughly doubles between 300 and 50 K. The source discusses skew scattering and multiple transport channels without selecting a settled origin. Susceptibility provides an independent change: long-range antiferromagnetic order is rapidly suppressed at small x, but a susceptibility increasing with warming persists, interpreted as two-dimensional antiferromagnetic correlations. A broad maximum shifts to lower temperatures with doping and disappears from the accessible window in the heavily doped region, where a Curie-like upturn dominates. The trend need not mean the short-distance exchange J vanishes; different magnetic scales and contributions can change differently.

Part 5

The oxygen control and the limits of the interpretation

A source x = 0.125 specimen is measured after different oxygen heat treatments. Reducing oxygen suppresses the Curie-like behavior and restores a positive temperature slope resembling lower Sr doping. The authors use this to argue that a simple vacancy-induced paramagnetic-moment explanation is inadequate and that vacancies act as donors, lowering effective holes. This is useful evidence, although reduction also changes disorder and does not isolate every microscopic effect. A two-dimensional spin model motivates a susceptibility maximum near kBT ≈ 0.95J; applying that undoped model to doped carriers is explicitly tentative. Charge and spin changes occur together as superconductivity disappears, favoring a magnetic interpretation in the authors’ discussion without proving a pairing mechanism. Compare both this electronic argument and the later structural-boundary paper as distinct historical hypotheses.

Part 6

How to compare the evidence

Use the composition conversion before placing any point from this paper on a modern LSCO diagram. The source formula contains two La/Sr sites, so its x = 0.125 oxygen-control specimen corresponds to conventional Sr content z = 0.25, not z = 0.125. The same warning applies to Hall-sign and superconducting-boundary comparisons. Next keep composition separate from transport inference: a negative Hall coefficient in a hole-substituted oxide does not imply the chemical Sr count became negative. Fermi-surface curvature and scattering can change the transverse response. Finally, varying oxygen at fixed Sr content changes effective carrier donation and can change disorder. The experiment is therefore a useful control against a simple vacancy-moment explanation, rather than an isolated test holding every electronic variable fixed.

Part 7

A fitted transport law is an observation before it is a mechanism

Electrical resistivity measures the response of charge motion to an applied current in the stated geometry. The measured value combines electronic structure, available states, scattering and sometimes sample connectivity. A useful temperature fit can summarize the data without uniquely separating those ingredients. A linear term, a quadratic term, a Hall sign or a hopping-like transformation each constrains a transport description, but none alone specifies a microscopic spin pattern or pairing interaction. The supplied paper therefore compares transport with magnetic or superconducting information instead of using one exponent as a complete electronic phase label.

Changing the plotted variables can help test a law but can also obscure mixtures. A straight segment against T² tests an approximate quadratic dependence over that interval. A local logarithmic exponent summarizes a derivative and may evolve smoothly when two additive contributions coexist. The fit range and residual offset matter because subtracting a different constant changes that derivative, especially at low temperature. Compare the source's alternative fits and controls before treating a coefficient as universal. A microscopic lifetime requires additional assumptions about Fermi-surface geometry and velocities, while a Hall-derived count requires a sufficiently simple transport model. The direct coefficient or sign trend should remain distinct from those stronger interpretations.

Part 8

Keep chemical tuning and access to the normal state separate

Nominal substitution identifies a preparation parameter, not automatically the number of mobile carriers in every electronic regime. Oxygen stoichiometry can compensate nominal holes and also change disorder. A magnetic field can suppress superconductivity and add magnetoresistance while leaving the chemical composition fixed. These controls alter different combinations of physical variables. The paper's inference is meaningful only when the measured state, field geometry, preparation and composition convention are preserved alongside the transport curve. Translating chemical notation should precede comparison with a modern doping diagram.

Access beneath the zero-field superconducting transition requires particular care. A high-field resistivity is directly measured in that field, whereas an inferred normal-state zero-field continuation depends on a model of magnetoresistance. Likewise, a missing superconducting transition means none was detected under the stated conditions and temperature floor, not a proof about every specimen at that nominal composition. The source uses complementary response and preparation checks to constrain these distinctions. This is why comparing samples, fitting protocols and limits is part of learning the physics rather than ancillary experimental detail. It prevents an approximate trend from becoming a universal boundary or a uniquely identified collision mechanism.

Part 9

Translate the chemical notation before interpreting any comparison

The formula in this source contains two La/Sr sites each carrying a Sr fraction x. Expanding it gives a total Sr content 2x per formula unit, so the conventional LSCO label z is twice the paper's x. This changes every comparison, not just the optimal composition. The source's x = 0.125 oxygen-control specimen is conventional z = 0.25; interpreting it as a one-eighth sample would put the entire experiment in the wrong electronic regime. Similarly, onset and endpoint comparisons need the factor of two before being placed beside the other supplied guides.

Once translated, the paper's broad transport result is easy to state: superconductivity occupies only part of a metallic range, and metallic conduction continues in heavily doped specimens where no transition is detected down to the stated floor. Mobile charge is therefore not by itself a sufficient condition for pairing. The authors compare concurrent magnetic changes to motivate a spin-related interpretation, but the coexistence of two trends does not determine a unique pairing mechanism. Retaining the chemical translation and the operational transition definition allows this historical series to be compared fairly with later samples without manufacturing a disagreement from notation alone.

Part 10

A Hall sign is a transport property, not a signed chemical Sr count

The simple one-carrier formula motivates comparing RH with inverse density at low substitution. The observed response then departs from that simple trend and eventually changes sign. Because Sr substitution remains a nominal hole-doping operation, the sign change is evidence that a naive chemical counting interpretation of Hall response has failed. It can reflect Fermi-surface curvature, mobilities or scattering weights. The single-crystal comparison reduces concern that the sign behavior is only an artifact of averaging ceramic orientations, but it does not choose one of those electronic mechanisms uniquely.

The oxygen-control susceptibility supplies another useful distinction. Removing oxygen at fixed Sr reduces the effective-hole count in the ionic picture and changes the shape toward a lower-doping-like response. A simple account in which vacancies only add independent paramagnetic moments is inadequate for that trend. Yet vacancies also alter disorder, so the experiment is not a pure carrier-count adjustment with every scattering parameter held fixed. The source's magnetic maximum and its tentative parent-model interpretation should remain separate from the direct transport signs and slopes. Together the observations demonstrate changing electronic character, while the exact relation between magnetic response, Hall behavior and pairing remains unsettled.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Does negative RH prove Sr adds electrons chemically?

No. The transport sign can change with Fermi-surface and scattering structure despite hole substitution.

Can a metal fail to superconduct?

Yes. The heavily doped samples remain metallic after their superconducting transition disappears.

Does oxygen reduction only add magnetic defects?

No. It also changes effective hole count and disorder, as the susceptibility control illustrates.

What conventional composition is source x = 0.125?

z = 0.25, because the formula has two La/Sr sites each with Sr fraction x.

Why is a negative RH not negative chemical doping?

RH weights transport geometry and scattering; Sr counting and transverse transport sign are different quantities.

What does metallic conduction without detected superconductivity establish?

A metal need not pair; mobile charge alone is not sufficient for a superconducting transition under the stated conditions.
