Electronic correlations on the verge of the Mott transition in La1−xSrxTiO3 by 47/49Ti and 139La nuclear magnetic resonance

Ti and La relaxation both remain Korringa-like while growing toward the metallic Mott edge, favoring broadly renormalized metallic dynamics over a prominent additional AF fluctuation peak in this study.

The research question: What microscopic correlations grow as Sr-doped LaTiO3 approaches its Mott boundary?

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

A heavy correlated metal can retain a Fermi-liquid-like temperature dependence. This paper tests that idea near the LaTiO₃ Mott boundary using titanium and lanthanum nuclei. The parent magnetic structure nearly cancels transferred antiferromagnetic fields at La, while Ti remains a broader probe. In the metallic compositions both relaxation rates are roughly proportional to temperature, but their slopes grow together toward the boundary. The comparison does not require a prominent new antiferromagnetic peak. With explicit coupling and band assumptions, square-root rate estimates track enhanced low-energy density of states and support mass renormalization. Spectral quadrupole effects and overlapping isotopes are treated separately. The second supplied PDF is the same article, not another experiment.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Mott-Hubbard insulator: An interaction-driven insulating state with a charge gap between Coulomb-split d states.
  • Sr fraction x: The substitution fraction in La1−xSrxTiO3; decreasing x approaches the parent insulator.
  • Hyperfine filter: A coupling geometry that weights electronic fluctuation wavevectors differently at different nuclei.
  • EFG: Electric field gradient that couples to a nuclear quadrupole moment.

A descriptive explanation, step by step

Follow the full argument.

Part 1

Choose the right endpoint and x convention

This paper writes La1−xSrxTiO3, with x the Sr fraction. Undoped LaTiO3 is a d1 antiferromagnetic Mott-Hubbard insulator; adding Sr removes electrons relative to that parent and makes metallic compositions near x around 0.05 and above. Approaching the Mott edge from the metal therefore means decreasing x. The earlier Tokura paper, source 341, writes Sr1−xLaxTiO3 and uses x as the La fraction, so its horizontal axis runs in the opposite direction. The NMR work aims to test the microscopic origin of the bulk mass enhancement inferred from heat capacity and other probes. In particular, it asks whether a rapidly growing antiferromagnetic fluctuation component near an ordering wavevector is needed, as often discussed for metallic cuprates.

Part 2

The insulating parent calibrates a geometric cancellation

Zero-field Ti NMR in LaTiO3 requires a large internal hyperfine field, reported as 91.4 kOe, together with quadrupole interactions. La spectra can be explained with an almost vanishing internal magnetic field. In the proposed magnetic structure, contributions from surrounding antiferromagnetically ordered Ti moments cancel at the La site. La's lack of a large field does not mean its neighboring Ti ions lack ordered moments. This geometric argument motivates treating La as a relatively weak probe of the AF wavevector, while Ti can sample a broader momentum range. It is a useful calibration of the probe comparison, though the paper's inference assumes the symmetry-based filtering remains relevant for the metallic samples and acknowledges uncertainty in very small spin canting.

Part 3

Read quadrupole broadening before assigning magnetism

The field-swept powder spectra have central lines and broader satellites because both La and Ti nuclei have spin greater than one-half. The central-line singularities receive second-order quadrupole shifts. In Sr-substituted samples their separation scales approximately inversely with resonance frequency, supporting a quadrupolar origin rather than simple magnetic broadening. The inferred quadrupole frequency decreases with increasing Sr content, but its accuracy deteriorates when inhomogeneous width becomes comparable with the splitting. Point-charge calculations alone do not explain the full composition variation, motivating an electronic-density contribution to the electric field gradient. The La Knight shift is nearly temperature independent and becomes more negative toward small x. Its sign comes from the hyperfine contribution, so a negative shift must not be mistaken for a negative spin susceptibility.

Part 4

What the relaxation comparison establishes

At fixed metallic compositions, both La and Ti obey approximately T1T = constant. Yet their 1/(T1T) values increase toward the low-Sr metallic boundary. If a strongly growing AF-q component affected Ti but were filtered out at La, one would expect a more pronounced difference between their evolution. Instead the source finds similar composition dependence and no strong extra temperature-driven AF enhancement. This favors broadly enhanced metallic response rather than a dominant new AF peak within the investigated range. Such reasoning is more precise than saying a large relaxation rate always means antiferromagnetism. Relaxation is a weighted momentum sum, whereas a static shift measures a uniform response. The conclusion is conditional on hyperfine couplings, the accessible temperature range and the sample compositions; it is not proof of zero AF correlations everywhere.

Part 5

Mass estimates need explicit assumptions

The discussion relates relaxation to orbital and core-polarization contributions and estimates local low-energy densities of states using assumed hyperfine and correlation factors. Ti dominates that estimate, with the local Ti DOS around forty-five times the La DOS. The ratio is approximate rather than a direct count of orbital electrons. The square root of 1/(T1T), not the raw relaxation slope, is compared with the DOS and electronic heat-capacity coefficient. For a simple spherical band that square root scales as effective mass times carrier density to the one-third power. Hall results supply the carrier-density behavior, allowing departures to be interpreted as mass enhancement. The study therefore supports the earlier bulk picture microscopically, while distinguishing its metallic titanate response from the enhanced AF fluctuations of cuprates. Part 6

A local measurement tests a bulk mass interpretation

Bulk heat capacity and susceptibility had already indicated enhanced low-energy response near the titanate Mott boundary. The NMR question is whether that enhancement comes with the sharply growing antiferromagnetic dynamics familiar from some cuprate comparisons. Titanium is the magnetic transition-metal site, whereas lanthanum occupies a position surrounded by several Ti neighbors. These nuclei consequently couple differently to the spatial arrangement of electronic moments. Measuring both makes it possible to ask which part of a fluctuation spectrum is growing, rather than assigning an entire microscopic mechanism from one large rate.

The parent antiferromagnet supplies an internal calibration. Zero-field Ti resonances require a large magnetic field, while the La resonances can be described primarily by quadrupole interactions with almost canceled magnetic contributions. This is consistent with ordered neighbors whose transferred fields cancel at the La site. Absence of a La field is therefore evidence about coupling geometry, not absence of Ti magnetism. Extending that geometric filter into the metallic comparison is an assumption of the analysis. The result is a differential probe argument and does not amount to directly measuring every wavevector of the susceptibility.

Part 7

A powder spectrum contains orientation and nuclear information

A field-swept powder measurement averages grains with many crystal orientations. For nuclei with spin greater than one-half, the electric field gradient splits or shifts resonance transitions as well as the magnetic interaction. The central transition receives a second-order quadrupole contribution, while the satellites span a larger range. Changing resonance frequency tests this origin: the central quadrupole separation falls inversely with frequency in the source's approximation. An ordinary distribution of magnetic shifts would have a different frequency scaling. The authors use that distinction before interpreting the composition-dependent width.

The two titanium isotopes have nearly equal gyromagnetic ratios, and their central features overlap. Recovering magnetization after saturation therefore requires both isotopic contributions, with their nuclear spin and intensity weights. A simple single-exponential fit chosen only because it looks convenient would ignore the actual nuclear transitions. The supplied article writes its recovery model explicitly. It also states that extracting quadrupole frequencies becomes less accurate once inhomogeneous broadening is comparable with the splitting. Those limitations matter especially at higher Sr fraction, where a narrower apparent line is not a high-precision structural refinement.

Part 8

Korringa behavior compares slopes, not equal absolute rates

At a fixed metallic composition, a straight 1/T1-versus-T relation gives an approximately constant 1/(T1T). The coefficient can be much larger in a strongly renormalized Fermi liquid than in a weakly interacting reference. It can also grow as the composition approaches an insulating boundary without producing a new temperature-dependent antiferromagnetic peak. Temperature tuning within one sample and changing the electronic filling between samples are separate comparisons. Figure 11 displays the latter for two nuclei whose absolute rate scales differ.

If a large additional AF component grew principally at the ordering wavevector, Ti would sample it more strongly while the La geometric filter would suppress it. Similar evolution of the two coefficients, together with their Korringa-like temperature dependence, therefore argues against a prominent extra AF enhancement in the measured metallic range. The authors favor single-site electronic renormalization as the dominant mechanism. This conclusion does not show that AF correlations vanish, nor does it directly resolve a flat susceptibility at every momentum. Hyperfine factors, measured frequency, temperature range and the validity of the site-filter comparison bound that inference.

Part 9

Take the square root only after specifying the relaxation channels

A nuclear relaxation coefficient includes orbital, core-polarization, contact and dipolar channels with different couplings. The paper simplifies the Ti and La coefficients to the dominant d-related contributions and adopts hyperfine and correlation factors to estimate a low-energy density of states at each site. With those factors held as assumed, the rate coefficient scales as the square of the DOS. Its square root is consequently compared with the heat-capacity coefficient, rather than equating the raw slope to a mass.

For a simple parabolic band, the DOS also depends on carrier density to the one-third power. Hall information supplies that changing density, so departures from a fixed-mass filling trend can support mass renormalization. The estimated Ti/La DOS ratio near forty-five means the Ti-related low-energy contribution dominates under the model; it is not forty-five times as many actual electrons on Ti. The nearly composition-independent ratio and parallel thermodynamic trend help form a coherent argument, but uncertainties in the orbital factors cannot be removed by the visual agreement alone. The additional supplied version, source 338, contains this same experiment and must not be counted as independent confirmation.

Translate the mathematical statement

Essential equations, meaning and assumptions

1/(T1T) ∝ Σq |A(q)|² χ″(q,ωN)/ωN

Symbols and units: A(q): hyperfine amplitude form factor; χ″: imaginary dynamical spin susceptibility; ωN: nuclear Larmor frequency. Prefactors and the paper's coupling-weight notation are suppressed.

Physical meaning: Different nuclei weight the same host dynamics differently. Comparing Ti with filtered La constrains whether a prominent AF component is needed.

PDF p. 6

T1T ≈ constant at fixed metallic x

Symbols and units: T1: nuclear spin-lattice relaxation time; T: temperature; x: Sr fraction in this source.

Physical meaning: Equivalent to 1/T1 proportional to T. The constant may change strongly with composition, so this law is compatible with mass enhancement.

PDF p. 4 · PDF p. 5

[1/(T1T)]^(1/2) ∝ N(EF) ∝ m* n0^(1/3)

Symbols and units: N(EF): renormalized DOS; m*: effective quasiparticle mass; n0: carrier density, in the simple parabolic-band interpretation.

Physical meaning: Use the square root of the rate coefficient and account for carrier density before inferring mass; coupling and correlation factors are assumed.

ΔHquadrupole ∝ νQ²/ν0

Symbols and units: ΔHquadrupole: central-line singularity separation, with field/frequency conversion understood; νQ: quadrupole frequency; ν0: resonance frequency.

Physical meaning: Inverse-frequency scaling tests the second-order quadrupole interpretation under the stated asymmetry assumptions.

What the evidence supports—and where it stops

  • Historical 1999 study; x is Sr fraction, opposite to source 341's La-fraction convention.
  • Momentum filtering and mass/DOS estimates depend on assumed hyperfine, band and correlation factors.
  • No prominent extra AF enhancement in the measured metal is weaker than a statement that all AF correlations vanish.
  • Source 338 is the same work in an additional supplied PDF encoding, not an independent replication.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Why is La an AF filter in the argument?

Transferred fields from symmetrically arranged AF Ti neighbors cancel; the parent spectrum supports that geometry.

Which power of the rate coefficient tracks the DOS in the simple model?

The square root, [1/(T1T)]^(1/2).

Does source 338 provide independent confirmation?

No. Its nine text pages and rendered pages match this same article, despite different PDF bytes.

Why must the Ti recovery include two isotope contributions?

Their central resonances overlap, but their nuclear spins and transition recovery coefficients differ.

Does a more negative La shift mean negative spin susceptibility?

No. A negative hyperfine contribution can turn an increasing positive spin response into a more negative shift.

Why account for density before interpreting a square-root rate as mass?

Even a fixed-mass simple band changes DOS with carrier density. Coupling factors and that filling dependence must be separated from mass renormalization.
