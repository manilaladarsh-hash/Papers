Nematic Fermi Fluids in Condensed Matter Physics

The same broken symmetry connects very different materials. Reliable detection requires separating an electronic tendency from lattice bias and domain averaging.

The research question: What defines an electron nematic, how can point-like electrons form one, and what evidence supports it in different correlated materials?

In two minutes

The whole guide at a glance

  1. An electronic nematic picks one of two equivalent crystal axes while keeping translation symmetry — unlike a stripe, which also adds a new period.
  2. Point-like electrons can form one: the order lives in their collective distribution, reached by melting stripes or by a Pomeranchuk distortion of the Fermi surface.
  3. Examples span high-Landau-level GaAs near half filling, Sr₃Ru₂O₇ in a bounded field window, cuprates and Fe-based superconductors.
  4. A conjugate field — strain or an in-plane field — aligns domains and reveals the anisotropy, but also explicitly breaks the symmetry being tested.
  5. Reliable detection separates an electronic tendency from lattice bias and domain averaging, and combines transport, thermodynamic and microscopic probes.

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

Nematic electrons collectively choose an axis even though individual electrons have no rod-like shape. This review organizes that idea by symmetry, then compares ways it can arise and ways experiments can detect it. Stripe melting and a quadrupolar metallic instability can produce similar orientation with different microscopic physics. Ultraclean quantum-Hall devices show selective low-temperature directional resistance; Sr₃Ru₂O₇ adds bulk evidence for a bounded metallic phase. Cuprates and iron compounds require careful treatment of crystal anisotropy, disorder and coupled magnetic or orbital effects. Conjugate fields can expose domains while also imposing a direction. The shared language is useful, but transport magnitude, entropy and quantum criticality remain material-specific problems in this historical 2010 synthesis.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Nematic order: Collective selection of formerly equivalent axes while preserving translations.
  • Director: An orientation axis that is unchanged by a 180-degree rotation.
  • Conjugate field: A controllable bias coupling linearly to the orientational order.
  • Pomeranchuk instability: Interaction-driven distortion of a metal's momentum occupation.

Part 1

Research question and system

What defines an electron nematic, how can point-like electrons form one, and what evidence supports it in different correlated materials? A review spanning high-Landau-level GaAs/GaAlAs two-dimensional electron systems, the bilayer ruthenate Sr₃Ru₂O₇, cuprates and Fe-based superconductors. Its evidence and open questions reflect the literature available in 2010. Synthesize symmetry arguments, stripe-melting and Pomeranchuk mechanisms, collective-mode theories, and previously published transport, thermodynamic, neutron and surface-probe experiments.

Part 2

Build a hierarchy of evidence

First specify the symmetry: an electronic nematic chooses equivalent crystal axes while retaining translation symmetry. Next identify the field conjugate to that choice, such as orthorhombic strain or an in-plane field component in a suitable system. An applied field can align domains and make anisotropy visible, but also explicitly breaks symmetry. A response measured under that field must be interpreted as an induced response unless its spontaneous-order evidence is established separately.

The review combines transport, thermodynamics and symmetry constraints. Quantum-Hall resistance differences must be separated from device geometry, and ruthenate phase evidence includes bulk thermodynamic signatures. Cuprates have an additional orthorhombic background that can both mimic an anisotropy and expose a large electronic susceptibility. Temperature dependence, tuning, cleanliness, reversibility and independent probes therefore matter. Use this hierarchy when comparing the other five nematicity sources: a theoretical existence result, a dynamical approximation, a disorder signature and an experimental phase identification answer different questions.

Part 3

Results and interpretation

Nematic order breaks an axis-interchange symmetry while preserving translations. In its pure form it also preserves inversion and time reversal. Stripe melting and quadrupolar Fermi-surface distortion are complementary routes to this symmetry pattern.

The quantum-Hall examples show very large directional resistance differences near half-filling of Landau levels N ≥ 2. A cited ν = 9/2 example has a resistance ratio about 100 at low temperature; anisotropy becomes essentially absent above roughly 150 mK. Device resistance and intrinsic resistivity must be distinguished.

In very clean Sr₃Ru₂O₇, transport anisotropy is revealed by an in-plane field component in a phase around 7.8–8.1 T, with maximum Tc about 1.2 K. Bulk thermodynamics supports a distinct phase; a large structural distortion does not explain the observed anisotropy.

YBCO's orthorhombicity both aligns domains and provides an anisotropic background. Strong temperature and doping trends, together with independent probes, motivate the electronic interpretation. Cuprate evidence is presented with more caveats than the clearest quantum-Hall and ruthenate cases.

Authors' synthesis: nematicity is a common language for spatial symmetry breaking, with mechanisms and transport magnitude dependent on each material. The review leaves quantum criticality, domain-wall scattering and several microscopic explanations open.

Part 4

The order belongs to correlations, not the shape of an electron

Point-like electrons can form a nematic because the order describes their collective distribution and correlations. A fluid need not consist of elongated particles to have an easier conducting direction or anisotropic momentum occupation. The defining operation exchanges directions that were equivalent in the higher-symmetry state. A pure nematic changes under that exchange but retains translations, inversion and time reversal. A stripe adds a spatial density period and therefore breaks translations too. Orbital occupation, bond correlations and Fermi-surface distortions can all realize the same orientational symmetry while involving different microscopic degrees of freedom.

On a square lattice the primary choice is between x and y, an Ising-like two-valued order. A nearly rotationally invariant continuum instead has a continuously orientable director. That director identifies an axis: rotating it by 180 degrees gives the same state. The review writes the order as a tensor whose diagonal difference or off-diagonal component detects anisotropy. Conductivity, magnetic correlations or scattering intensity can serve as observables with this transformation property. Their absolute magnitudes and microscopic interpretations are not interchangeable. A structural transition with the same symmetry is also possible, so identifying a distortion does not alone establish that electronic correlations drove it. The useful definition is a symmetry statement combined with evidence about the relevant degrees of freedom.

Part 5

Two routes to one symmetry pattern

The stripe route begins with an ordered spatial pattern that already distinguishes axes. Fluctuations or defects can destroy the common positions of its density maxima before they destroy the directional preference. The resulting fluid retains orientation without stripe translation order. Figure 1 illustrates this melting hierarchy. It is especially natural in strongly correlated systems where local density textures are meaningful and a quasiparticle Fermi surface may not be the most useful starting point. The three-band Emery example demonstrates another strong-coupling construction rather than asserting that every nematic must descend from visible stripes.

The Pomeranchuk route begins from a metal. Interactions make a circular or crystal-symmetric distribution of occupied momenta unstable to a quadrupolar distortion. The density need not acquire a new modulation period: states in one momentum direction are redistributed relative to the perpendicular direction. The particle–hole character of this instability differs from superconductivity, where two particles form a pair condensate. Both routes can break the same spatial symmetry, but they use different limits, order-parameter constructions and low-energy excitations. A symmetry-based experiment can identify an axis preference without choosing between them. A microscopic explanation must subsequently account for the material's orbitals, interactions, disorder and measured dynamics; the two schematic routes should not be combined as though they were one calculation.

Part 6

Disorder, native anisotropy and how hidden order becomes visible

An orienting field couples linearly to nematic order. In a crystal it can arise from strain or orthorhombicity; in an appropriate device an in-plane magnetic component can provide a tunable bias. A weak field can align domains whose opposing orientations previously canceled in a bulk measurement. The same field explicitly selects an axis, however, and therefore prevents a finite induced anisotropy from serving alone as proof of spontaneous order. Varying the bias and comparing its response with temperature, sample cleanliness and independent probes is more informative than taking one anisotropic image or resistance value.

Spatial disorder can act as a random orienting field. Domains then favor different axes, and the tendency toward macroscopic order depends on dimensionality and whether the order is discrete or continuous. Local probes can reveal correlations invisible to a bulk average. Hysteresis and resistance noise can supply complementary evidence for slow interacting domains, although the source does not claim that these signals have a unique microscopic origin. The native orienting field in the high-Landau-level devices was itself not fully understood in the literature reviewed here. That uncertainty is part of the 2010 account. It is useful to distinguish an unknown weak alignment mechanism from the much larger electronic response it exposes, rather than infer that the weak field explains the full resistance ratio.

Part 7

High Landau levels: read the device before its theory

The quantum-Hall examples are ultraclean two-dimensional electron systems in semiconductor heterostructures. A perpendicular magnetic field organizes orbital motion into Landau levels, and the filling factor ν is the electron density times h divided by eB. The striking anisotropy appears near half filling of high levels, beginning with N = 2, rather than in every quantum-Hall state. Figure 2 compares orthogonal resistance measurements while sweeping magnetic field through successive filling factors. Temperature measurements show that the large difference disappears on warming through the low-temperature scale. Above that scale a small geometry-related background can remain.

A resistance measured between contacts is not identical to the local resistivity tensor. Current paths and sample shape can amplify an anisotropic response. Quantitative interpretation therefore requires the device geometry and an appropriate conversion; the plotted large resistance ratio should not be copied indiscriminately as an intrinsic resistivity ratio. Early Hartree–Fock calculations favored stripes in these levels, while thermal or quantum melting offered a route to retaining orientation without a static stripe lattice. These are mechanisms reviewed alongside the experimental data, not direct microscopic images of the devices. The contrasts with lower Landau levels and with warmer temperatures give the experiment its force: the anisotropy is selective in filling and develops with an electronic scale rather than being a fixed contact artifact.

Part 8

Rotating and rounding the semiconductor anisotropy

An in-plane field provides an additional control over the high-Landau-level orientational state. The review discusses reorientation and the enhancement of an anisotropic response when a direction is explicitly selected. Figure 4 places measured temperature-dependent hard-direction resistivity beside a model calculation of orientational alignment under different fields. In the experimental panel the field is in tesla, resistivity in resistance per square and temperature in millikelvin. In the model panel temperature and field are measured relative to the model interaction scale J, while the observable is a dimensionless angular average. These are deliberately different axes; their visual resemblance is an analogy rather than a direct unit-preserving fit.

The externally selected direction can broaden the temperature range of detectable anisotropy, consistent with rounding an orientational transition by a conjugate field. The source uses this comparison to support a liquid-crystal interpretation of a system whose underlying electron motion is very different from ordinary molecular rods. It does not settle the native alignment field or the microscopic melting mechanism. Contact geometry, material width and disorder remain important practical ingredients. The appropriate lesson is to ask how the response changes when a controlled symmetry-breaking field is applied, rather than to interpret the presence of such a field as proof that no collective electronic tendency exists.

Part 9

The ruthenate has a thermodynamic phase as well as anisotropic transport

Sr₃Ru₂O₇ is a layered, multiband metal with substantial spin–orbit effects and magnetic-field-driven metamagnetism, meaning a rapid increase of magnetization with field. Its electronic structure is not the semiconductor Landau-level system. Several Fermi-surface sheets and nearby van Hove features matter, and photoemission and quantum oscillations provide complementary constraints. Increasing sample purity changes the single broad magnetic feature into multiple features. In the clean regime two first-order transitions bound a low-temperature region around 7.8–8.1 tesla, with a maximum transition temperature of about 1.2 kelvin in the reviewed measurements.

A field perpendicular to the planes can leave differently oriented domains averaged together. Adding an in-plane component reveals a large directional transport difference, and rotating that component changes the preferred direction. The case is strengthened by susceptibility, thermal expansion, magnetostriction, magnetization and heat measurements that identify equilibrium phase boundaries. Quantum oscillations show metallic states below, within and above the region. Within the resolution discussed by the review, the in-plane structural response does not provide a large distortion capable of simply explaining the transport anisotropy. Cleanliness remains decisive: a small electronic ordering scale can be obscured by scattering. These linked observations establish much more than the existence of two unequal resistance values, while still leaving the detailed scattering mechanism open.

Part 10

Entropy and quantum criticality are unresolved tests of the ruthenate explanation

Figure 7 displays entropy divided by temperature across magnetic field and temperature, then compares low-temperature field dependences of entropy and specific heat. The rising thermodynamic response outside the intervening phase motivates a nearby quantum-critical interpretation. However, the source stresses that a simple van Hove density-of-states model and the particular fluctuation theories then available did not account for all the observed behavior. Experimental thermodynamics contains both band-structure and critical contributions; comparing an isolated theoretical exponent with the full measurement requires that distinction. The intervening phase cuts off the growth, so extrapolating the exterior curve through it describes a counterfactual rather than measured divergence.

The entropy is higher inside the nematic region than in adjacent fluids at the same boundary conditions, a surprising result if one imagines ordering as always removing degrees of freedom. The first-order boundary slopes and direct thermal measurements constrain this conclusion. Domains were suggested as a possible source of extra entropy, but the review did not establish that explanation. Large resistive anisotropy presents another challenge: a small Fermi-surface distortion with simple point scattering need not produce the observed magnitude. Domain-wall scattering on a mesoscopic scale is discussed as a plausible route, not a completed quantitative theory. These open problems should remain part of understanding the phase rather than be replaced by a tidy universal narrative.

Part 11

Cuprates, iron compounds and the scope of the synthesis

The cuprate section deliberately concerns a harder inference problem. YBCO is already orthorhombic, so its lattice and chains both select a direction and provide a background anisotropy. The reviewed argument emphasizes additional electronic anisotropy that grows strongly on cooling or increases as doping decreases, even when the structural trend goes the other way. Transport, low-energy magnetic correlations and the Nernst response supply different observations. The latter is a transverse electrical response to a thermal gradient in magnetic field; it is not interchangeable with ordinary longitudinal resistivity. The reported trends motivate a relationship to the pseudogap in the historical literature, rather than prove that nematicity explains its entire microscopic origin.

For Bi-based cuprates, cleaved surfaces permit tunneling maps of electronic inhomogeneity and bond-centered x/y differences. Disorder, energy dependence and extraction algorithms matter when interpreting glassy local patterns. In iron-based parent compounds, structural symmetry lowering occurs near the magnetic ordering scale and evolves with doping, motivating an electronic origin with coupled lattice, spin and orbital effects. The review then discusses orbital multiplicity, valley order and other possible nematic systems as future directions. Its central synthesis is a shared symmetry language across diverse systems. Transport magnitudes, transition mechanisms and quantum-critical behavior remain material dependent. All statements about unresolved examples and proposed directions describe the literature available in 2010; they are not an automatic update to present-day consensus.

Essential equations, meaning and assumptions

Nρ = (ρxx − ρyy)/(ρxx + ρyy)

Symbols and units: ρxx, ρyy: longitudinal resistivities in the same units; Nρ dimensionless; equivalent axes in the high-symmetry reference state.

Physical meaning: A transport quantity with nematic transformation symmetry. It is not automatically equal to a microscopic order parameter or a contact-resistance ratio.

ν = ne h/(e B)

Symbols and units: ν: dimensionless Landau filling; ne: electron number per area; h: Planck constant; e: magnitude of electron charge; B: perpendicular magnetic flux density.

Physical meaning: Varying B at fixed density moves through the Landau-level fillings associated with anisotropic transport.

What the evidence supports—and where it stops

  • This is a 2010 review rather than a current survey or a new experiment. The illustrated data belong to the original experimental studies cited there.
  • Anisotropy alone does not establish spontaneous nematicity. Crystal fields, domains, disorder and measurement geometry can obscure or mimic the expected response; the quasiparticle critical theory also has unresolved beyond-leading-order issues.
