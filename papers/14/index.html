Low-temperature electronic specific heat of La2-xSrxCuO4 and La2-xSrxCu1-yZnyO4 - Evidence for a d wave superconductor

The research question: How does low-temperature heat capacity distinguish nodal excitations and impurity-induced residual states?

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

Clean samples show a low-temperature electronic T² term, while Zn restores a residual linear term and suppresses Tc, supporting a strong-scattering nodal-gap interpretation. Zn strongly suppresses superconductivity even though it is a nominally nonmagnetic substitution. The source contrasts two explanations: magnetic pair breaking from induced neighboring Cu moments and strong ordinary potential scattering in a d-wave state. For a sign-changing nodal gap, impurity scattering can create low-energy quasiparticle spectral weight, making the residual linear heat-capacity term a useful complementary observable to Tc. The paper does not deny induced moments; it asks whether they alone explain the strong suppression.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Low-temperature heat capacity: separate electronic and phonon contributions
  • Nodal gap: low-energy excitations can survive near zeros of the gap
  • Zn substitution: a nonmagnetic ion can cause scattering and induce neighboring moments
Heat capacity C
Heat needed per temperature increment, with electronic, lattice and magnetic contributions.
Sommerfeld coefficient γ
Coefficient of the linear electronic low-temperature heat contribution under an appropriate model.
Density of states
Number of electronic states per energy interval; the low-energy value influences thermal response.
Line node
Direction or line in momentum space where a superconducting gap vanishes.
Residual DOS
Finite low-energy electronic spectral weight remaining under the chosen model.
Condensation energy
Free-energy gain of the collective superconducting state relative to the assumed normal state.
Phonon reference
Estimate of the lattice heat used to subtract non-electronic background.

Transform each heat term correctly

Look for: A quadratic electronic heat term looks different depending on the axes.

A quadratic electronic heat term looks different depending on the axes. In C(T) it is αT²; in C/T it becomes αT; against a horizontal T² axis that produces curvature rather than another straight phonon slope. A finite residual γ contributes a vertical offset in C/T, while the low-temperature phonon term contributes a βT² slope. Identifying the electronic quadratic part therefore requires the full decomposition and a valid fit window, not simply calling every slope on a C/T plot a nodal signal.

The source compares clean and Zn-substituted specimens under that decomposition.

Part 1

Why Zn provides a useful perturbation

Part 2

Sample checks and the normal reference

Ceramic synthesis, oxygen annealing and SQUID transitions characterize the samples. The increase of the structural transition temperature with Zn, inferred from the resistivity derivative, supports successful substitution. Critical Zn content depends on Sr content: about 2 percent at x = 0.10 and 3.5 to 4 percent for x = 0.16 to 0.22 suppresses superconductivity. Near the critical content, some resistive traces retain filamentary signatures. At lower x, Zn-substituted samples show localization, whereas higher-x samples remain metallic to low temperature. Therefore Zn is not a universally clean way to expose an unchanged normal state; carrier localization and impurity response become important in the underdoped regime.

Part 3

The heat-capacity decomposition

Clean superconducting x = 0.16 to 0.22 samples show a downward curvature in C/T versus T², fitted by electronic αT² plus phonon βT³. The quadratic electronic contribution is more visible at larger x; phonons dominate near optimal doping. Nonsuperconducting Zn-rich samples are fitted by γNT + βT³ above the very lowest temperatures. The inferred phonon coefficient is similar within this low-temperature comparison and gives a reasonable Debye scale of roughly 420 to 450 K. Below about 3.5 K, impurity-induced magnetic contributions produce an upturn; the lattice background is extrapolated from higher temperature rather than assigning that upturn to electronic DOS. The usable fit window is thus essential.

Part 4

What changes when Zn is introduced

For superconducting Zn-substituted samples, the model is C = γT + αT² + Cph. γ grows rapidly with Zn while Tc falls. For x at least approximately 0.16, γ saturates above the critical Zn content and is treated as a normal-state value γN; at lower doping an extrapolation is used because localization changes the reference. The source compares Tc/Tc0 with γ/γN across several x values and finds agreement with strong-scattering nodal-superconductor calculations, better for one theory than another. For x = 0.10 a preexisting residual γ is subtracted when discussing the impurity-induced restoration. The result supports a d-wave interpretation but does not directly measure the impurity scattering phase shift or distinguish all possible disorder models.

Part 5

Nodes, residual states and the inferred gap

A clean line-nodal spectrum has low-energy DOS proportional to energy, giving electronic heat capacity proportional to T². A finite residual DOS adds a linear γT term. Under the nodal model α is proportional to γN/Δ0, so the authors estimate a decreasing gap amplitude above optimal doping from measured α and γN. This is a model-based gap estimate, not a spectroscopic peak measurement. Their normal DOS maximum is not at the maximum Tc, showing that DOS alone with an unchanged coupling cannot explain the observed dome. The clean samples’ very small residual γ contrasts with residual nuclear response in other supplied samples. Different specimens, orbital/background subtractions and probe weightings must accompany that comparison. The later condensation-energy paper also revisits phonon-reference choices, warning against extending this low-temperature Zn background agreement to all temperatures.

Part 6

How to compare the evidence

A residual linear term and a nodal quadratic term in electronic heat capacity represent different low-energy information. The latter is compatible with a density of states increasing away from a node, while a residual γ samples states remaining arbitrarily close to the chemical potential within the model. Zn substitution increases γ and suppresses Tc, supporting a strong-scattering interpretation for a sign-changing gap. Yet the low-temperature upturn from induced moments must be excluded from the phonon-reference fit; otherwise a magnetic contribution could be absorbed into an incorrect electronic intercept. The weak low-temperature phonon change in this analysis does not imply Zn is an interchangeable reference over the wider temperature interval studied in source 281. The subtraction must be validated over the actual interval used.

Part 7

Background subtraction is part of the physical inference

A calorimeter measures the total heat needed to change the specimen temperature. Electrons, lattice vibrations and magnetic excitations can all contribute. To discuss an electronic density of states, the source must separate the electronic heat from the larger background over the chosen interval. A reference sample is useful only if its lattice contribution is sufficiently comparable; an impurity used to suppress superconductivity can also alter phonons, localization or magnetic response. The original article therefore explains its reference and fitting window rather than treating the measured total as a direct electronic spectrum.

Plot transformations help reveal the terms but do not eliminate the subtraction assumptions. A linear electronic term γT becomes a constant in C/T; a quadratic electronic term becomes proportional to T; a cubic phonon term becomes proportional to T². A low-temperature magnetic upturn can distort those comparisons and should not be absorbed blindly into a residual electronic γ. Examine which interval supplies the fitted lattice baseline and which data are excluded or extrapolated. The useful conclusion is the consistency of a physically motivated separation under its stated conditions, not an assumption-free count of electronic states at every temperature or a guarantee that two impurity references are interchangeable.

Part 8

The largest gap is not the whole low-energy spectrum

A superconducting gap depends on momentum and may vanish along nodes. Low-energy states near those nodes contribute differently from states at a large antinodal gap maximum. An impurity or other source of broadening can also leave finite spectral weight near zero energy. Thermal measurements average the excitations available within a temperature-dependent energy window, so they do not simply read off the largest spectroscopic gap. In a clean line-nodal model, a density of states proportional to energy gives a quadratic electronic heat contribution, while a finite zero-energy density adds a linear term. Those statements describe a low-energy model and require an appropriate background before being applied to the measured heat.

A collective energy gain introduces a further weighting. Condensation energy concerns the difference between superconducting and normal free energies, so it depends on how many states contribute and how their occupation and coherence change, not just on one maximum excitation threshold. A reduced participating density can offset an increasing maximum gap. Conversely, a large normal-state density of states can enhance heat capacity without uniquely setting the pairing interaction. This is why the source compares several observables and gap scales. The direct thermal result, the inferred density or effective gap, and the proposed microscopic mechanism remain separate stages of the argument.

Part 9

Follow the power law through the plotted variables

A quadratic electronic heat term looks different depending on the axes. In C(T) it is αT²; in C/T it becomes αT; against a horizontal T² axis that produces curvature rather than another straight phonon slope. A finite residual γ contributes a vertical offset in C/T, while the low-temperature phonon term contributes a βT² slope. Identifying the electronic quadratic part therefore requires the full decomposition and a valid fit window, not simply calling every slope on a C/T plot a nodal signal. The source compares clean and Zn-substituted specimens under that decomposition.

The lowest-temperature impurity upturn complicates the separation. Induced magnetic contributions can add heat and mimic excess electronic weight if fitted carelessly. The paper uses a higher-temperature interval to establish the background and treats the upturn separately. In lower-doped samples, localization also complicates the nonsuperconducting reference, so the normal γ is not always obtained by the same direct plateau method. These qualifications explain why its joint Tc and heat-coefficient comparison is more informative than a raw low-temperature excess, while retaining the model dependence of the inferred DOS and gap.

Part 10

Why nominally nonmagnetic Zn is still a strong test

Calling Zn nonmagnetic describes the substituting ion's nominal atomic spin, not the response of the correlated Cu environment around it. The source discusses induced neighboring moments as well as strong potential scattering. In a sign-changing gap, even potential scattering can disturb pairing and restore low-energy quasiparticle weight. The useful observation is therefore the combination of lowered Tc and increased residual linear heat, compared at fixed Sr composition. The source then tests whether the normalized relationship agrees with strong-scattering nodal calculations. The horizontal quantity in that test is γ/γN, not the Zn concentration itself, which is essential for reading the original figure correctly.

Agreement supports the tested interpretation without directly measuring a scattering phase shift or excluding every magnetic effect. The nodal coefficient and chosen normal-state coefficient also yield a model-based gap estimate; they are not a tunneling maximum. Comparing that estimate with the DOS maximum shows why a simple unchanged pairing interaction multiplied by DOS is not enough to determine the superconducting dome. Finally, the low-temperature phonon similarity is a window-specific result. It should remain separate from later wide-temperature reference choices and from residual nuclear measurements in differently prepared LSCO samples.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Why exclude the impurity upturn from a naive electronic fit?

Induced local moments contribute heat and can inflate an apparent residual DOS.

Does nominally nonmagnetic Zn mean no magnetic response?

No. The source discusses moments induced on neighboring Cu sites.

Is the estimated Δ0 a direct tunneling measurement?

No. It follows from α and γN under a nodal-gap model.

What does αT² become in C/T?

It becomes αT; a T² horizontal axis therefore does not turn it into the same straight phonon contribution.

Which quantity is on the normalized strong-scattering comparison axis?

The restored residual fraction γ/γN, rather than the Zn concentration y/yc.

Does agreement determine the impurity phase shift uniquely?

No. It supports a strong-scattering nodal model under the background and reference assumptions, without independently measuring the phase shift.
