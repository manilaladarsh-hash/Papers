Itoh · supplied source 2001 · experiment

Cu NQR study of host and guest spin dynamics in impurity-doped YBa2Cu4O8

The supplied 2001 Cu NQR study separates host and impurity-induced recovery terms: spinless Zn causes a larger inferred wipeout region and stronger low-temperature host enhancement than magnetic Ni, while the normal-state pseudo-spin-gap remains robust at the studied small concentrations.

The research question: How can a nonexponential Cu recovery distinguish an impurity's local effect from the surrounding cuprate's spin dynamics?

In two minutes

The whole guide at a glance

  1. Cu NQR compares magnetic Ni (spin 1) and spinless Zn substituted into YBa₂Cu₄O₈ (Y124).
  2. The Cu recovery is nonexponential: a host exponential times an impurity term arising from a spatial distribution of local rates.
  3. At 4.2 K Zn implies a much larger undetected (wipeout) region — roughly 10–20 unit cells — than Ni (about one).
  4. Above Tc the host 1/(T₁T) still falls below T_s ≈ 160 K for the small Ni and Zn amounts studied.
  5. Below Tc Zn enhances the host relaxation far more than Ni: an impurity's own spin does not rank its effect on a correlated host.

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

Impurities make copper nuclear recovery nonexponential because nearby and distant copper sites experience different relaxation. This paper fits that distribution in Ni- and Zn-doped YBa₂Cu₄O₈, separating a host contribution, an impurity-induced contribution and a model-dependent undetectable region. Spinless Zn has a larger inferred wipeout region than magnetic Ni. For temperature trends the authors change to a limiting fit that still retains impurity relaxation. The guest rates can be comparable while Zn strongly changes the low-temperature host coefficient. Above the superconducting transition, the normal-state pseudo-spin-gap fall remains robust for the studied small concentrations. Shielding, detection and fitting assumptions limit exact spatial counts; proposed resonance states or magnetic textures are not directly imaged.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Host term: The approximately homogeneous copper relaxation component of the fitted recovery.
  • Guest term: Impurity-induced relaxation of detected copper nuclei, not a direct impurity resonance.
  • Unrecovered fraction p(t): The dimensionless magnetization deficit relative to equilibrium after inversion.
  • Wipeout: Loss of nuclei from the detectable resonance echo when their response is too fast or otherwise unobservable.

A descriptive explanation, step by step

Follow the full argument.

Read the full detailed argument9 parts · about 6 min

Part 1

Why a spinless impurity is not necessarily weak

Itoh and colleagues compare Ni and Zn substitutions in YBa2Cu4O8, often called Y124. Ni is described as a magnetic 3d8, spin-one impurity, whereas Zn is a spinless 3d10 defect. In a correlated CuO2 plane, removing a magnetic site can reorganize the neighboring spins, so this atomic comparison does not determine the eventual magnetic response. Powder samples have impurity contents measured by ICP spectroscopy, which differ somewhat from earlier nominal concentrations. The studied measured fractions are Ni0.009,0.02 and 0.03, and Zn0.005 and 0.010, alongside the pure material. Zero-field planar 63Cu(2) NQR recovery is measured from 4.2 to300 K. Thus the observed signal is from host Cu nuclei experiencing both intrinsic and impurity-induced fluctuations; the word 'guest' does not refer to a Zn nuclear relaxation measurement.

Part 2

A nonexponential recovery is useful spatial information

The measured quantity p(t) is the fraction of magnetization still unrecovered after an inversion pulse. It is not described by one simple exponential once impurities create a distribution of local relaxation strengths. The model assumes randomly positioned impurity centers whose contribution to a nucleus's local rate decreases with distance. A homogeneous host term and an impurity-dependent term then multiply in the recovery function. The finite-exclusion model additionally removes nuclei within a radius rc because they relax too rapidly to appear in the NQR experiment. Its fitted Nc is an inferred excluded fraction, not a directly counted map of individual Cu sites. A successful recovery fit indicates inhomogeneous dynamics within this model; it does not uniquely establish the microscopic origin or exact shape of the impurity's spin environment.

Part 3

Wipeout estimates and their important detection limit

At4.2 K the finite-exclusion fits imply a substantially larger region around Zn than around Ni. The source estimates roughly 10–20 excluded unit cells per Zn at the stated in-plane concentrations, compared with roughly one per Ni. Converting that fraction into a radius depends on whether the region is circular, square or counted along a diagonal; the resulting Zn lengths are a few lattice constants, comparable to cited AF correlation-length estimates. Those geometric choices are not interchangeable measurements of one exact radius. Below Tc, integrated NQR intensity also changes because of Meissner shielding, even in powder. The authors therefore estimate Nc from recovery fitting rather than simply equating missing intensity with vanished sites. Their magnetic-vortex or Skyrmion analogy is an interpretation of a possible texture, not a directly resolved real-space spin image.

PDF p. 2 · PDF p. 3

Part 4

Do not carry a problematic fit unchanged through Tc

The finite-Nc formula reproduces Zn's low-temperature recovery better, but overestimates Nc above Tc, partly because the underlying spatial approximation treats the mean impurity separation as effectively infinite. For temperature-dependent rate analysis the authors instead use the limiting recovery expression with Nc fixed to zero and a finite square-root-in-time impurity parameter 1/τ1. This is a change of fitting policy, not a claim that Zn physically has no exclusion region. The resulting impurity-induced rates level off at low temperature and are of comparable order for Ni and Zn. The fitted host 1/(T1T), however, behaves differently: Ni is nearly temperature independent below about 15 K, while Zn grows rapidly within the superconducting state. Comparing only the guest term would miss the paper's central host-response distinction.

Part 5

Separate the robust pseudo-spin-gap from a proposed mechanism

In the normal state, the host 1/(T1T) falls below a scale Ts of about 160 K in pure Y124. That behavior remains robust for the small Ni and Zn amounts examined, even while superconducting transition temperatures change. It does not imply that every pseudogap property is impurity independent at all concentrations. The source interprets Zn's low-temperature non-Korringa host enhancement using stronger AF fluctuations associated with a resonance state in a d-wave superconductor; Ni is discussed in terms of weaker enhancement or intermediate scattering. These microscopic explanations go beyond the directly fitted rates. A final conditional argument says that if the relevant dynamics were stripes and these impurities pinned them by softening their fluctuation energy, a low-temperature divergence should follow. The observed robust normal-state behavior challenges that particular pinning scenario; it does not directly test every form of static stripe order.

Part 6

Host and guest name contributions to the detected copper recovery

The measured nuclei are planar copper, not the zinc impurity or a direct image of its magnetic surroundings. After an inversion pulse, the experiment records how their spin-echo magnetization returns toward equilibrium. In the pure material a relatively simple host recovery can describe the signal. Randomly positioned impurities add rates that vary strongly with distance, so the ensemble of detected copper sites no longer shares one exponential time. The source calls the uniform fitted part the host contribution and the distributed impurity-induced copper part the guest contribution. These names refer to components of one copper measurement.

Nickel and zinc offer a useful contrast because their nominal isolated-ion spins differ, but that does not preassign their effects in a correlated host. A spinless vacancy-like perturbation can enhance surrounding copper dynamics more strongly than a magnetic impurity. The article uses measured ICP concentrations rather than simply repeating nominal recipes; those counts are important before comparing transition suppression and inferred regions. Its sharp superconducting transitions support a reasonably homogeneous preparation, but do not show that local relaxation is spatially identical. Macroscopic composition uniformity and impurity-centered dynamical inhomogeneity can coexist.

Part 7

Why a fast region can disappear rather than recover visibly

In the model, the local impurity-driven relaxation time grows steeply with distance from the impurity. Nearby nuclei can relax so rapidly that the experiment no longer records their echo. A finite exclusion radius removes those nuclei from the recovery ensemble and creates the fitted wipeout parameter Nc. The remaining nuclei still span a distribution of rates, which gives a nonexponential recovery. This detection rule differs from physically eliminating copper atoms, setting their moments to zero or proving that they belong to a new crystal phase.

At 4.2 K the finite-exclusion analysis gives many more affected cells per Zn than per Ni. Turning that number into a radius requires a geometric shape and an in-plane impurity concentration, so the circle, square and diagonal estimates should remain alternative conventions. A few-lattice-constant radius is not an experimentally resolved boundary in a microscope image. Below the superconducting transition the detected integrated NQR intensity is additionally modified by Meissner shielding. That makes an intensity-only site count particularly unreliable. The source estimates the region through recovery fitting, with explicit assumptions, and discusses possible magnetic textures as interpretations rather than observed spin maps.

Part 8

The limiting fit preserves an impurity contribution without a finite excluded fraction

A finite-exclusion formula is useful for the very-low-temperature comparison, but the authors find it overestimates the excluded fraction above Tc. Part of the spatial approximation treats the impurity spacing as effectively infinite, which becomes problematic for the fitted region. They therefore use a limiting recovery formula for temperature trends. Its exponent combines a term linear in time from the host and a square-root-in-time term from distributed impurity relaxation. That is not a sum of two ordinary exponentials with two visibly separable decay times.

In the limiting combination Nc tends to zero while the local exclusion time also changes so that the impurity rate parameter remains finite. It is consequently incorrect to interpret setting Nc to zero in this fit as physical disappearance of the impurity effect. One must also keep units separate: the guest parameter 1/τ1 is a rate, whereas the host comparison 1/(T1T) is a rate divided by temperature. Their low-temperature curves answer different questions. The comparable guest rates for Ni and Zn coexist with very different host behavior. Looking only at the distributed term would miss the main contrast emphasized by the paper.

Part 9

Two temperature windows constrain different mechanism claims

Above Tc the fitted host rate divided by temperature falls below a normal-state scale near 160 K, the source's pseudo-spin-gap behavior. Small amounts of either impurity preserve that qualitative fall even while Tc is reduced. In the superconducting state the Ni host coefficient becomes nearly constant at the lowest temperatures, while the Zn coefficient rises strongly. There is therefore no contradiction between a robust normal-state suppression and a new impurity-sensitive low-temperature response. Neither window establishes that every spectral pseudogap observable remains unchanged at arbitrary impurity content.

The authors interpret the Zn enhancement in terms of strong scattering resonance states and enhanced antiferromagnetic dynamics in a d-wave superconductor; for Ni they discuss weaker enhancement or intermediate scattering. Those microscopic accounts go beyond a direct fit of the nuclear recovery. A final argument about stripes is explicitly conditional: if the relevant fluctuations were dynamical stripes and the impurities pinned them through softening, one would expect a particular low-temperature enhancement. The robust normal-state behavior challenges that combined assumption. It is not a direct exclusion of every stripe texture or proof that no static charge order exists. The guide keeps the inferred magnetic region, fitted host/guest rates and proposed mechanisms at these different levels.

What the evidence supports—and where it stops

  • Historical 2001 source; fitted spatial regions and microscopic scattering mechanisms remain model dependent.
  • Recovery probes detected Cu nuclei; directly lost nuclei and integrated intensity require detection and shielding corrections.
  • Finite-Nc and limiting fits have different roles; host 1/(T1T) and impurity-induced 1/τ1 must remain separate.
  • The Ni0.009 sample's Tc is66 K in experimental prose but 63 K in figure labels; preserve the concentration and avoid assigning unwarranted precision.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Why can spinless Zn have a strong magnetic effect?

It can reorganize and enhance surrounding host Cu moments and fluctuations despite carrying no nominal local spin itself.

Why not read Nc directly from missing integrated NQR intensity below Tc?

Meissner shielding also alters the detected intensity, so the paper uses a recovery-model estimate.

Does fixing Nc=0 for the temperature-trend fit prove that wipeout is absent?

No. It is a limiting fitting policy used because the finite-exclusion formula overestimates Nc above Tc.

Which normal-state scale is reported as robust?

The host pseudo-spin-gap onset around Ts160 K for the small studied Ni/Zn concentrations, not every property at arbitrary impurity content.

Does guest mean a directly observed Zn nucleus?

No. It is the impurity-induced component of detected planar copper relaxation.

Why can a sharp superconducting transition coexist with nonuniform relaxation?

A broadly homogeneous impurity concentration still produces local impurity-centered environments with different rates.

What does the stripe argument actually test?

It challenges a conditional combination of dynamical-stripe fluctuations and impurity pinning by softening; it does not directly exclude every static stripe pattern.
