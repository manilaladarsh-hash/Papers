Sato · supplied source 2017 · experiment

Thermodynamic evidence for a nematic phase transition at the onset of the pseudogap in YBa₂Cu₃Oᵧ

In-plane torque reveals additional susceptibility anisotropy near the pseudogap onset, beyond the background already imposed by YBCO chains.

The research question: How can electronic nematicity be investigated when the crystal already breaks fourfold symmetry?

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

YBCO already distinguishes its two in-plane directions because it contains copper-oxygen chains. This experiment therefore looks for an additional temperature-dependent anisotropy rather than treating any twofold signal as new order. A rotating magnetic field produces torque that measures the susceptibility difference. Normalization by the average response reveals a kink and excess anisotropy near the pseudogap scale. Across compositions, an extrapolation to zero background retains an intrinsic component, and normalized excess curves approximately collapse. These observations support the authors’ nematic-transition interpretation while depending on background subtraction and a small-orthorhombicity assumption. They do not identify a unique microscopic order parameter or directly observe a perfectly tetragonal control sample.

Terms to understand before starting
  • nematicity: directional electronic anisotropy without requiring a new spatial period
  • orthorhombicity: inequivalence of crystallographic a and b axes
  • magnetic torque: response sensitive to anisotropy of the susceptibility tensor
Torque
Angular response generated when a field is not aligned with a susceptibility principal direction.
Untwinned crystal
A crystal prepared with a selected orthorhombic-domain orientation.
Susceptibility tensor
Direction-dependent linear magnetic response.
Orthorhombicity
Structural inequivalence between two in-plane directions.
Nematicity
Electronic directional symmetry breaking.
Reduced temperature
Temperature divided by a chosen characteristic scale.
Scaling collapse
Agreement of normalized curves after rescaling their axes.

A descriptive explanation, step by step

Follow the full argument.

Part 1

Why ordinary twofold symmetry is insufficient

Sato and colleagues examine untwinned underdoped YBCO crystals with hole concentrations around 0.10–0.15. YBCO already has inequivalent a and b directions because CuO chains run along b. Its normal crystal structure therefore supplies an explicit symmetry-breaking field for any electronic nematic tendency in the planes. A nonzero in-plane susceptibility difference cannot alone establish a newly formed nematic state. The paper seeks an extra component that turns on at the pseudogap temperature. Its central question is how to separate that electronic behavior from the structural background, rather than whether an ideal fourfold crystal becomes twofold. Doping also changes the chains and background anisotropy, so comparing several compositions is an important part of the analysis.

PDF p. 1 · PDF p. 2

Part 2

What the torque measures

A microcantilever measures magnetic torque as a 7 T field rotates within the ab plane. Alignment better than about 0.1° suppresses contamination from the much larger out-of-plane anisotropy. The dominant twofold harmonic yields Δχ = χaa − χbb; the measured orientation is consistent with χaa exceeding χbb and negligible off-diagonal χab. The average in-plane susceptibility is reconstructed using additional susceptibility information described in Methods. This enables the normalized anisotropy η = Δχ/(χaa + χbb), rather than comparing only raw torque amplitudes. Near Tc, higher harmonics and nonlinear superconducting fluctuations complicate the linear-response interpretation and are excluded from the relevant onset analysis. Thus the thermodynamic inference rests on a specific linear susceptibility regime and careful geometry. The reconstruction also uses powder-averaged and out-of-plane susceptibility information, so η contains more experimental input than the torque amplitude alone.

Part 3

The kink and excess anisotropy

On cooling, Δχ initially decreases but turns upward below a kink temperature Tτ that matches the pseudogap scale T*. Meanwhile the average spin response continues to decrease. The normalized η is approximately temperature independent above T* and grows below it. The authors define the excess Δη(T) by subtracting η(T*), which represents the structural background within their analysis. Across measured dopings, the excess scales approximately with that background. Extrapolation to zero background produces a finite intercept below T*, supporting an intrinsic component beyond chains alone. This is an inference from the composition series: it is not an observation made on a separate tetragonal YBCO sample. Its validity depends on treating orthorhombicity as a sufficiently small perturbation and on the chosen background subtraction.

Part 4

Scaling and the transition interpretation

After normalizing Δη by its value at 0.7T*, the curves approximately collapse as functions of T/T*. The onset is superlinear and differs from simple mean-field or two-dimensional Ising order-parameter forms. The authors discuss domain growth and more complex order as possible explanations, leaving the universality class unsettled. They interpret the kink, finite extrapolated intercept and scaling as evidence for a second-order nematic transition at the pseudogap onset. They also acknowledge that an explicit symmetry-breaking field normally rounds an ideal symmetry transition. The chain background may select domain orientation and help a bulk signal become visible, but this same fact makes the interpretation conditional on the small-perturbation treatment. Neither the torque nor the collapse uniquely determines the microscopic nematic order parameter.

Part 5

Relation to charge order and other pseudogap probes

No corresponding anomaly is resolved at the lower charge-density-wave onset in these measurements. That does not prove charge order is absent or isotropic locally: nearly balanced orthogonal stripe domains can cancel the bulk anisotropy, and a bidirectional pattern can also preserve an average rotational response. The paper compares its onset scales with other pseudogap probes, whose anomalies need not all coincide. Whether nematicity drives the transition or emerges as a secondary response remains open. A proposed quantum-critical continuation near higher doping is not directly measured by this limited composition series. Use the experiment as a thermodynamic anisotropy constraint alongside shifts, relaxation, transport and diffraction, keeping each observable's sensitivity and the chain-background extrapolation explicit.

Part 6

Torque reads a direction-dependent energy

A magnetic sample has an energy that depends on how a field is oriented relative to its susceptibility tensor. Differentiating that energy with respect to angle gives the torque. In the linear regime, the in-plane difference between the a and b susceptibilities produces a twofold angular harmonic proportional to the square of the applied field. The harmonic’s phase also tests whether an off-diagonal susceptibility term is significant. This connects a measured cantilever response to a thermodynamic anisotropy rather than simply a transport difference. The connection requires that the field stays in the plane and that nonlinear superconducting contributions do not dominate. The source’s careful alignment and exclusion of the near-Tc regime are therefore part of the physical measurement, not optional instrumental details.

Part 7

A structural background is an explicit directional preference

In an exactly fourfold crystal, a new twofold response can indicate spontaneous directional symmetry breaking. YBCO is a more subtle test because its chains already distinguish a from b. Those chains supply a directional field that can select electronic domains and round an ideal transition. They also create a background susceptibility difference above the pseudogap onset. The experiment asks whether an extra contribution appears beyond that background. Calling all the measured anisotropy nematic order would ignore the existing crystal symmetry. Conversely, rejecting any additional electronic interpretation because the crystal is orthorhombic would ignore the kink and systematic composition dependence. The source’s inference lies between those shortcuts and depends on separating normalized background from the additional response.

Part 8

Why normalization changes the comparison

Both susceptibilities can decrease as the uniform spin response is suppressed. A raw difference alone then mixes the overall response scale with its directional imbalance. Dividing by the sum yields the normalized anisotropy eta, allowing the fraction of the response that is unequal between directions to be followed. The source finds eta approximately temperature independent above T-star even while the raw difference changes. Subtracting its value at T-star defines the excess. This procedure is a specific analysis choice, supported by the above-onset behavior. It does not prove that the chains have no other temperature dependence under every condition. The reconstructed susceptibility sum also uses additional measurements described in Methods, so eta is not obtained from a single torque amplitude alone.

Part 9

An extrapolated intercept is a conditional control

The composition series contains different structural backgrounds. At matched reduced temperatures, the authors compare the excess anisotropy with that background and extrapolate toward a zero-background limit. A nonzero intercept below T-star supports an additional response that would remain in that limit. This is the central test against attributing the full onset simply to chains. It remains an extrapolation from orthorhombic specimens, not a measurement on an otherwise identical tetragonal YBCO crystal. Its force depends on the background acting as a sufficiently small perturbation and on the analysis relation remaining meaningful toward zero. Showing those assumptions beside the intercept helps the reader understand both why the result is suggestive and what experiment would provide a stronger independent control.

Part 10

Scaling collapse does not identify a universality class

Normalizing the excess by its value at 0.7T-star and the temperature by T-star reduces the curves to dimensionless comparisons. Their approximate collapse suggests a shared onset behavior across the studied compositions. It does not automatically determine a unique order parameter, critical exponent or microscopic interaction. The source notes that the onset is superlinear and differs from simple mean-field or two-dimensional Ising forms. Domain growth and more complicated order are discussed as possibilities. The explicit structural directional field also complicates an ideal sharp-symmetry-transition interpretation. Read the collapse as a strong empirical organizational constraint, then assess proposed mechanisms separately. The higher-doping quantum-critical continuation is beyond the directly measured composition series.

Part 11

No bulk anisotropy anomaly does not mean no charge order

The lower charge-density-wave scale has no comparably clear feature in the measured anisotropy. A macroscopic directional signal can nevertheless vanish if two orthogonal stripe-domain populations contribute nearly equally, or if the local pattern is bidirectional. Torque measures the averaged susceptibility tensor, while diffraction can detect spatial modulation even when its average directional contribution cancels. Thus the absence of one anomaly is a constraint on this bulk observable, not a universal absence statement about electronic ordering. The same distinction applies when comparing nematicity, Knight shifts, optical probes and transport. Each weights the state differently. The source leaves open whether nematicity is primary at the pseudogap onset or a coupled secondary response to another ordering tendency.

What the evidence supports—and where it stops

  • Orthorhombic background explicitly breaks fourfold symmetry and rounds a pristine transition.
  • Zero-background interpretation uses extrapolation and a small-perturbation assumption.
  • Primary versus secondary nematicity and microscopic order remain unresolved.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

What is the measured anomaly?

A kink and rapid growth in additional in-plane susceptibility anisotropy near T*, despite a decreasing average response.

Was the tetragonal limit measured directly?

No. It was inferred by extrapolating excess anisotropy versus the structural background across dopings.

Does no torque anomaly at TCDW exclude stripes?

No. Orthogonal domains or bidirectional order can have little net anisotropy.

Why is a twofold harmonic insufficient by itself?

The chains already make the crystal orthorhombic and provide a twofold background.

Why divide by the susceptibility sum?

It distinguishes directional imbalance from changes in the overall uniform response scale.

Was a zero-chain-background sample measured?

No. The intrinsic component is inferred by extrapolating the composition series.

Does curve collapse identify an Ising exponent?

No. The source explicitly discusses a superlinear onset different from simple benchmark forms.

Why can local charge order lack a torque anomaly?

Orthogonal domains or a bidirectional pattern can cancel in the bulk tensor average.
