The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

The 1999 paper linked lost Cu NQR intensity with stripe development; later work shows that wipeout is a detection-weighted quantity whose onset is not universally charge-order onset. Stripe dynamics are glassy: spin fluctuations slow gradually and different instruments become sensitive at different rates. The paper compares Ba-, Nd- and Eu-containing La214 samples with known stripe behavior, then applies the same procedure to LSCO. It proposes that slowly fluctuating charge-rich and charge-poor regions broaden the instantaneous electric-field-gradient distribution and enhance spin relaxation enough to hide their Cu NQR signals. Its illustrative charge distribution of zero and one-half hole per Cu, and the resulting approximately 8 MHz frequency range, are assumptions of an idealized stripe picture, not a measured local charge amplitude.

Terms to understand before starting
  • LSCO: layered La2-xSrxCuO4; nominal x is Sr per formula unit
  • Local probe: a distribution can matter even when the bulk average barely changes
Wipeout
Loss of detectable nuclear intensity under the chosen echo and frequency conditions.
Echo delay τ
Separation between echo-sequence pulses; detection occurs after approximately 2τ with finite-pulse corrections.
Boltzmann correction
Correction for temperature-dependent equilibrium nuclear polarization.
Charge order
Spatially modulated electronic charge response established with an appropriate structural or electronic probe.
Spin freezing
Spin fluctuations slow enough to appear static within a specified probe window.
Spectral weight
Integrated detected nuclear response after the stated corrections; distinct from charge modulation amplitude.
Broad wings
Wide, often rapidly decaying nuclear spectral components around a narrower surviving peak.

Keep the probe window

Look for: Glassy spin slowing is seen at different apparent temperatures by different probes.

In the supplied 1999 data, the wipeout fraction F grows below about 65 K in the compared stripe materials and closely follows a charge-order quantity inferred from elastic neutron diffraction. Diffraction intensity scales as the square of an order amplitude, so the authors compare the corresponding amplitude rather than blindly equating intensity with amplitude. They fit the temperature dependence with a weak-coupling BCS-like order-parameter form, obtaining a characteristic ratio Δ = 1.75 kBTcharge. Part 1

Why this experiment was proposed

Hunt and colleagues sought a nuclear-resonance signature of stripe order despite the absence of an ordinary critical divergence in the measured Cu relaxation rate. Stripe dynamics are glassy: spin fluctuations slow gradually and different instruments become sensitive at different rates. The paper compares Ba-, Nd- and Eu-containing La214 samples with known stripe behavior, then applies the same procedure to LSCO. It proposes that slowly fluctuating charge-rich and charge-poor regions broaden the instantaneous electric-field-gradient distribution and enhance spin relaxation enough to hide their Cu NQR signals. PDF p. 1

Part 2

How the intensity was measured

Spectra were acquired with a 90-degree and 180-degree echo sequence, commonly separated by about 10 to 12 microseconds. The A-line spectral intensity was integrated after fitting the line shape, with corrections for the thermal Boltzmann population and the measured echo decay T2. These corrections are essential: cooling increases the equilibrium nuclear polarization while a shorter T2 decreases the recovered echo. A and B lines were compared in an isotope-enriched Ba sample and showed similar temperature-dependent intensity loss. The observed line itself broadens only moderately because it samples surviving nuclei; the hidden part need not have the same frequency or decay distribution. Thus a quiet visible spectrum does not establish a quiet whole sample.

PDF p. 2

Part 3

The original stripe comparison

In the supplied 1999 data, the wipeout fraction F grows below about 65 K in the compared stripe materials and closely follows a charge-order quantity inferred from elastic neutron diffraction. Diffraction intensity scales as the square of an order amplitude, so the authors compare the corresponding amplitude rather than blindly equating intensity with amplitude. They fit the temperature dependence with a weak-coupling BCS-like order-parameter form, obtaining a characteristic ratio Δ = 1.75 kBTcharge. This fit is an analogy for the stripe evolution, not a measured superconducting gap. Different structural-transition temperatures and similar wipeout behavior motivated an electronic explanation rather than assigning the anomaly solely to the LTO-to-LTT transition.

Part 4

What happens to spin dynamics

The paper reports a change from an echo containing Gaussian and Lorentzian decay to a very short, nearly exponential echo in the stripe regime. It relates this to changes in indirect nuclear coupling and low-frequency spin fluctuations. La relaxation rises gradually and exhibits a lower-temperature hump rather than a divergence exactly at charge onset. Elastic neutrons, muons and NQR assign different apparent spin-order temperatures because their frequency windows differ. This is compatible with glassy slowing, but does not uniquely identify a microscopic real-space arrangement. Below still lower temperatures the resonance can reappear as broad, magnetically perturbed NQR: disappearance from one narrow measurement window does not imply an absence of magnetic nuclei.

Part 5

The LSCO claims require the later correction

The source interprets wipeout for x = 0.07 to 0.125 as stripe instability and assigns about 50 K onset near x = 0.115 or 0.125. It also observes strong loss at x = 0.04, where it discusses localized holes and commensurate correlations, already showing that signal loss is not stripe-specific. The later supplied ID29 directly revisits the old method: charge-influenced fast echoes start near 80 K in its x = 0.115 crystal, and early Gaussian extrapolation missed them. At lower dopings, localization can produce wipeout without identifying charge order. The useful durable result here is the sensitivity of Cu echo detectability to heterogeneous slowing. F should be described as a detection-weighted fraction with sample and pulse conditions attached, not as a general measure of charge modulation amplitude. Microscopic coexistence of superconductivity and the inferred stripes remains unresolved in this historical paper.

Part 6

How to compare the evidence

Read this original interpretation alongside source 29, which improves the ability to detect very rapidly decaying echoes. The original paper treats corrected missing NQR intensity and the change in echo shape as evidence for stripe fluctuations in selected compounds. It also discusses lightly doped x = 0.04, where intensity is lost without its stripe criterion. That counterexample already prevents wipeout from becoming a universal synonym for charge order. The later short-delay NMR experiment revises the onset assigned to a specific x = 0.115 specimen by recovering previously inaccessible broad wings. Both results concern detectability and spin dynamics. Neither missing weight alone nor an assumed charge-to-frequency conversion is a direct image of the real-space charge pattern or an absolute modulation amplitude.

Part 7

See the experiment as a visibility filter

A spin echo is recorded only after the excitation pulses and the receiver recovery interval. Nuclei whose transverse signal decays before that time can be present physically but absent experimentally. A long pulse separation therefore filters out fast-decaying environments more strongly than a short one. A broad frequency distribution creates another filter: a narrow sweep or excitation bandwidth may miss nuclei outside the chosen window. The article's intensity is consequently an operational quantity measured under specified pulse and spectral conditions. Counting the visible area without accounting for those conditions can confuse redistribution of response with a change in the number of nuclei.

Temperature corrections are equally important. The equilibrium nuclear polarization changes with temperature, and the echo amplitude changes with decay between excitation and detection. A raw rising signal on cooling could reflect greater Boltzmann polarization, while a falling signal could combine that increase with a much stronger loss of visibility. The source corrects for those effects before discussing missing spectral weight. The correction is still limited by what is actually observed: an extrapolation based only on slow surviving echoes cannot reconstruct an unobserved, differently decaying population without additional measurements. This is why shortening the delay or expanding the frequency sweep can alter the scientific conclusion rather than merely improve the appearance of a spectrum.

Part 8

Charge order and spin freezing have different observables

A charge modulation changes the electronic density and potentially the local electric gradient. Slowing spin fluctuations alter the local magnetic response and nuclear relaxation. In correlated materials these changes can influence one another, but their measured onset temperatures need not coincide. Diffraction supplies a momentum-specific order signature under its time and spatial windows; nuclear intensity supplies accessibility under an echo protocol; a muon supplies a local magnetic time window. A comparison is strongest when each quantity is kept in its own units and interpreted through its own measurement, rather than labeling every anomaly the same order parameter.

Glassy slowing makes this distinction especially important. As a distribution of electronic fluctuation rates shifts to slower values, different instruments can declare the system effectively static at different temperatures. A nuclear experiment can even lose its echo when fluctuations enter an unfavorable relaxation window before recovering a broader low-temperature signal elsewhere. Such behavior is not a simple monotonic meter of the charge amplitude. Independent diffraction evidence can anchor a charge-order onset, while local nuclear measurements describe how much response becomes strongly affected and how heterogeneous that influence is. The source's historical interpretation, later methodological revision and unresolved spatial questions should therefore be read together.

Part 9

Understand the original analogy without making it an identity

The historical argument begins with materials where independent diffraction evidence already supports stripes. Their corrected Cu NQR loss evolves with temperature in a way similar to a charge-order quantity inferred from diffraction. This correspondence motivates using wipeout as a local signature in LSCO, where the diffraction evidence then available was more limited. The logical step is an analogy: an empirically similar local response is interpreted as arising from related stripe dynamics. It is not a measurement showing that the nuclear missing-signal fraction equals the microscopic charge modulation at every doping.

The conversion of diffraction intensity to an amplitude is a separate part of that comparison. Since diffraction intensity scales with an order amplitude squared, its temperature evolution cannot be equated to amplitude without taking that relation into account. The BCS-like fit then parameterizes the shape of the inferred development; its gap-like parameter is not a superconducting excitation gap measured in the nuclear spectrum. Keeping the analogy, mathematical conversion and fitted temperature shape distinct allows the paper to be read fairly as a historical hypothesis. It also exposes exactly which step later short-delay experiments challenge.

Part 10

The source already contains a counterexample to a universal stripe meter

The lightly doped x = 0.04 sample loses substantial NQR signal even though the article discusses localization and commensurate spin correlations in that regime. This is an important internal warning: echo loss can accompany a change in local dynamics without uniquely selecting the same charge-stripe mechanism proposed elsewhere. An inaccessible nucleus can be hidden by fast transverse decay or by a changed frequency window, and the observed surviving line may broaden only moderately because the more affected environments are no longer represented. The visible linewidth and the missing fraction therefore sample different aspects of a heterogeneous response.

The later x = 0.115 remeasurement supplies the specific methodological revision. Shorter delays capture broad echoes already appearing near an independently established higher charge-order onset; older fitting procedures missed them and assigned an onset too low. Reading the two supplied papers together retains the original discovery that local nuclear visibility is sensitive to glassy electronic changes while correcting its interpretation as a universal charge-order thermometer. The disagreement concerns how much signal was accessible and how it was extrapolated, not the disappearance of Cu atoms or a requirement that all instruments measure the same apparent spin-freezing temperature.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Does the fitted Δ represent a superconducting gap?

No. It parameterizes a BCS-like temperature dependence used for the stripe interpretation.

Why might the visible linewidth change little during wipeout?

The rapidly relaxing or very broad population is removed from the visible spectrum.

Why include ID29 when studying this paper?

It explicitly corrects the earlier onset assignment and demonstrates recovery of fast, broad signal with shorter delays.

Why compare diffraction amplitude rather than raw intensity?

Diffraction intensity is proportional to amplitude squared, so the temperature dependence of the two quantities differs.

What is the purpose of the x = 0.04 counterexample?

It shows that strong wipeout can arise in a localized regime and is not uniquely a stripe-specific meter.

Which step is corrected by the later short-delay work?

The accessibility and extrapolation of fast broad echoes, which biased the inferred onset and missing-signal fraction.
