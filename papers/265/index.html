Singer · supplied source 2002 · thesis

¹⁷O and ⁶³Cu Nuclear Resonance Study of the Inhomogeneous Electronic State in La₂₋ₓSrₓCuO₄

Frequency-resolved Cu relaxation and oxygen shift–relaxation scaling support a distribution of local electronic states; a random-donor averaging model assigns a nanometre scale with explicit structural and temperature limits.

The research question: Does nominal Sr doping describe a uniform electronic state, and what can Cu NQR and oxygen NMR separately establish about local hole variation?

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

This thesis asks whether one nominal LSCO composition hides different local electronic responses. It measures recovery across Cu spectra and corrected spin response across oxygen spectra, then checks whether those responses can be calibrated as a local-hole distribution. A separate random-donor model converts the inferred distribution into an effective averaging radius. The radius is model dependent, and structural broadening can make the Cu estimate a lower bound. Local tilts, failed low-temperature scaling and echoes that decay before detection limit particular mappings. Two nuclear probes strengthen the evidence for local variation while their different filters and visibility conditions remain explicit. The chapter route explains each inference rather than treating a broad line as a photograph of patches.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Nominal x: Sr atoms per LSCO formula unit, used as the average hole-doping label.
  • Electric field gradient (EFG): curvature of the electric potential at a nucleus; determines the Cu quadrupole resonance.
  • Knight shift: the nuclear resonance shift caused by electronic magnetic response, with orbital and spin contributions.
  • T₁ and T₂: recovery of longitudinal nuclear magnetization and decay of transverse coherence; they answer different questions.
  • Hyperfine form factor: the wavevector-dependent weighting through which a nucleus samples electronic spin fluctuations.
  • HWHM: half width at half maximum; the thesis also defines an effective width from the second moment.
NQR
Nuclear quadrupole resonance selected by the local electric-field gradient without an applied magnetic field.
Electric-field gradient
Spatial variation of the electric field at a nucleus; sensitive to structure and electronic charge.
Hyperfine form factor
Momentum-dependent weighting of electronic spin fluctuations at a selected nuclear site.
T1
Longitudinal spin-lattice recovery time of nuclear populations.
T2
Timescale governing loss of transverse echo coherence; it limits signal accessibility.
Rpatch
Radius of an assumed circular donor-sharing area;2Rpatch is its diameter and neither is a directly imaged boundary.

A larger patch narrows donor fluctuations

A finite circle samples a fluctuating number of nearby donor contributions. At fixed mean donor probability, a larger circle averages more sites and narrows the relative distribution; a smaller circle broadens it. Part 1

The question hidden inside the linewidth

Singer's 2002 thesis asks whether La₂₋ₓSrₓCuO₄ is electronically uniform when its chemical composition is described by one nominal x. The starting observations are broad Cu NQR and oxygen NMR spectra, the Cu B-line, and temperature-dependent loss of Cu signal. A linewidth by itself is ambiguous: random donor positions, lattice distortions, magnetic impurities, and variations in electronic response can all broaden a resonance. The central strategy is therefore to measure dynamics at different positions across each spectrum, rather than report only the rate at its peak. In the Cu experiments, lower-frequency and higher-frequency nuclei have different temperature-dependent relaxation. This is evidence of a correlation between the local charge environment selected by resonance frequency and the low-energy spin response. Calling those responses a distribution of local hole concentrations also uses empirical calibration against samples of known nominal x. The thesis's background discussion of stripes and superconductivity motivates the experiment; it does not make every broad resonance a direct image of static stripes.

Part 2

What Cu and oxygen actually measure

The Cu study uses ⁶³Cu-enriched polycrystalline samples to separate otherwise overlapping ⁶³Cu and ⁶⁵Cu spectra. The quadrupole resonance is set by the EFG, whereas Cu T₁ is predominantly magnetic and strongly weights antiferromagnetic fluctuations. Isotope relaxation ratios support that magnetic assignment. The oxygen measurements instead use enriched single crystals with the field along c. The planar ¹⁷O central transition is sensitive to the spin Knight shift after subtracting the orbital shift and a small second-order quadrupole correction. Its hyperfine geometry suppresses contributions exactly at the commensurate antiferromagnetic wavevector, so it samples a different part of the fluctuation spectrum from Cu. Neither measurement is a real-space microscope returning coordinates for individual patches. They are local, bulk-sensitive spectroscopies that build statistical distributions, with visibility determined by pulse bandwidth, echo delay and relaxation. Comparisons with single-crystal Cu measurements, magnetometry and diffraction help test sample-quality explanations.

Part 3

Evidence for a local-state distribution

For Cu, Singer first measures the relaxation rate at each line's center of gravity as a function of x and temperature. At a fixed temperature this supplies an interpolation: a rate on a wing of one sample's spectrum is matched to the center rate of another nominal composition. For x = 0.07 at 295 K, the reported wing assignments are approximately 0.10 and 0.043, giving a characteristic half-intensity deviation about 0.028. This avoids treating all linewidth as hole variation; a separate random-lattice EFG width is already substantial. The calibration becomes poorly conditioned where rates for different x converge and is not extrapolated blindly into the overdoped branch. Oxygen supplies an independent check. Across its spectrum, 1/(T₁T) is approximately proportional to the spin Knight shift over a specified window. A shift distribution can then be converted using the measured change of the mean shift with nominal x. The Cu and oxygen estimates of local variation agree semi-quantitatively in their valid regimes.

Part 4

What the patch model adds

The real-space model randomly substitutes Sr²⁺ for La³⁺ and computes the lattice EFG by a point-charge sum, with empirical on-site and lattice calibration. Even if the donated holes are uniform, the ions' random positions produce intrinsic broadening and a secondary B-line. The thesis assigns B-site Cu nuclei to a nearby Sr above or below the Cu position; relaxation-corrected fractional B intensity approximately follows x. To account for additional broadening, holes donated inside a circular radius R are shared over the CuO₂ patch. Local concentration is the donor count divided by the number of Cu sites, while surrounding regions retain nominal x. Smaller averaging areas give larger counting fluctuations. Fitting the spectra yields radii of a few nanometres, commonly 3–4 nm in the relevant high-temperature comparisons. R is a radius; the corresponding patch diameter is 6–8 nm. The circular boundary is an assumed averaging construction. It is not a measured sharp domain wall or an independently reconstructed spatial arrangement.

Part 5

Temperature, lattice effects and missing signal

The characteristic local variation grows on cooling, with relative amplitudes as large as 0.4–0.5 in the thesis's reported temperature range. That behavior cannot be explained simply by a frozen macroscopic Sr-composition spread. It also complicates the length inference. Cu EFGs respond to local orthorhombic distortions; assigning their unseparated linewidth to holes makes the fitted Cu R a lower bound and the inferred hole spread an upper bound. Below the local structural onset TQ, Singer separately models the squared octahedral tilt and its distribution, using constraints imported from diffraction. Oxygen avoids the dominant first-order quadrupole ambiguity at the central transition, but its shift–relaxation scaling fails below a composition-dependent Tscale. Saturating linewidth there does not securely prove that the patch size stops changing. Finally, wipeout is loss of detectable echoes: fast-decaying nuclei disappear from the observed ensemble. A surviving-line average can therefore underrepresent the fastest relaxing regions, especially when a finite echo delay is used.

Part 6

How to read the conclusion

The strongest conclusion is a distribution of correlated local electronic responses in the studied LSCO samples, supported through two nuclear probes. The proposed length scale and B-site assignment come from additional modeling and cross-checks. The thesis connects low-hole patches with the gradual onset of Cu wipeout and discusses stripe slowing, but does not uniquely settle the microscopic origin of every low-temperature anomaly. In x = 0.16, relaxation at several positions drops near the common bulk superconducting transition despite different inferred local doping; this argues against assigning every patch an isolated transition temperature. The appendices are part of the reasoning: empirical EFG calibration reduces dependence on the chosen bare quadrupole moment, nonexponential recovery exposes distributions hidden within one frequency bin, and probe quality factor explains a tradeoff between signal strength and early-echo access. Read the guide as an account of this historical source. Its schematics explain inference and assumptions; the linked original pages remain the evidence for quantitative curves, fits and quoted experimental values.

Part 7

Trace the inference from response to concentration to length

Singer's central argument contains three steps that should be studied separately. The direct local-response observation is that different parts of a spectrum have different magnetic recoveries or shifts. The next step uses an empirical relationship established across known nominal compositions to assign a characteristic local-hole variation. The final step asks what averaging area of randomly positioned Sr donors could generate that distribution. Only the last step returns a radius, and it requires the geometric sharing model. Calling all three steps a direct measurement of electronic patches would hide the strongest evidence and the most important assumptions simultaneously.

The Cu and oxygen experiments constrain different parts of this chain. Cu NQR frequency selects an electric-gradient environment that includes structural disorder, while its recovery is magnetic. Oxygen's corrected spin shift is more directly electronic, but its hyperfine geometry excludes only the ideal commensurate contribution and its empirical scaling remains bounded in temperature. Their semi-quantitative agreement is valuable because different ambiguities would have to conspire to produce the same local-response interpretation. It still does not remove the need to state the calibration slope, width convention and visibility conditions.

The random-lattice calculation supplies a baseline before electronic patches are added. Nearby Sr positions produce real EFG broadening and a geometric B-line even with uniform holes. The additional sharing model then changes the count fluctuations within a circle. A smaller radius produces more spread; assigning extra structural width to holes makes the fitted Cu radius too small. The result is thus an effective lower-bound constraint in that treatment, with diameter twice the radius. Reading the three inference stages in order makes the nanoscale conclusion precise without inventing sharp domain walls or a unique spatial tiling.

Part 8

Read the failure points as part of the result

The thesis provides explicit tests of where a convenient mapping stops being reliable. If the rate-versus-composition calibration becomes flat, a small rate error produces a large local-hole uncertainty. If oxygen rate and shift no longer share the empirical relation below Tscale, the shift-width conversion lacks its required premise. If local tilt appears, Cu width includes an additional structural contribution that cannot be assigned entirely to electronic spread. If echo decay becomes too fast, the observed population no longer samples all of the specimen equally. Each failure concerns a particular inference step rather than negating every observation of local variation.

This is why the apparatus and appendices deserve inclusion in the reading route. Multilevel nuclear recovery must be separated from an electronic rate distribution; a finite frequency bin does not guarantee one electronic environment. Ringdown and receiver overload limit early-echo access, while quality factor trades sensitivity against that access. A more sensitive late echo can therefore be a less representative measurement of fast-relaxing regions. The source connects these procedural limits directly to the local-state interpretation, rather than treating them as engineering details detached from the physics.

The synthesis also retains a common superconducting response at several frequency-selected regions despite different inferred local values. Those regions should not be modeled automatically as isolated miniature bulk samples with independent transition temperatures. Local electronic variation is compatible with coupled collective behavior. The strong conclusion is a distribution of correlated local responses in the studied specimens; the inferred sharing radius, local tilt model, stripe-related narrative and proposed reasons for failed low-temperature scaling remain additional claims with their own assumptions. A complete understanding includes both the positive evidence and the exact places where quantitative interpretation must stop.

Part 9

A shared transition is compatible with varied local response

Different local nuclear environments need not behave as isolated bulk specimens. A common superconducting change across selected frequencies constrains how the heterogeneous responses participate in a coupled collective state, without eliminating the preceding local variation or determining its unique microscopic cause.

What the evidence supports—and where it stops

  • Historical 2002 thesis; statements about other probes summarize the source's cited work, not a current literature review.
  • Patch radii depend on random donor statistics, assigned hole averaging, EFG calibration and unseparated structural broadening; Cu estimates are lower bounds in the stated construction.
  • The oxygen conversion is limited to temperatures above composition-dependent Tscale and to spectral regions not obscured by apical oxygen.
  • Finite echo delays can preferentially hide nuclei with fast T₂ and correlated fast T₁; below wipeout onset the observed distribution is incomplete.
  • The thesis supports electronic inhomogeneity without uniquely choosing static stripes, impurity moments or another microscopic low-temperature mechanism in every regime.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Is a 3 nm patch radius a 3 nm domain diameter?

No. The modeled diameter is 2R = 6 nm; neither number establishes a sharp physical boundary.

Why does a Cu linewidth alone not give Δxlocal?

Sr positions and local distortions also create EFG broadening. Singer uses relaxation calibration and compares oxygen spin data.

What does a missing Cu echo mean?

The nucleus may have relaxation too fast for the acquisition delay; missing signal is not proof that Cu atoms or spins disappeared.

Why stop the oxygen conversion below Tscale?

The empirical 1/(T₁T)–Kspin correspondence fails, so the same linewidth-to-local-doping inference is no longer justified.

Why can the Cu radius be a lower bound?

Additional structural broadening makes an all-electronic width overestimate the electronic spread and underestimate the averaging area.

Can the oxygen mapping be continued below failed scaling?

No. Its required rate-shift relationship ceases to hold there.
