5. How a linewidth becomes a radius

Counting fluctuations shrink with averaging area, so a fitted hole-spread scale can constrain R within the patch model.

The research question: What assumption turns the observed local-state distribution into a nanometre length?

Build the idea ↓Original PDF · 144 pages ↗
In two minutesIn plain languageVisual storyMain resultsDetailed argumentOriginal figuresEquationsRecall

In two minutes

The whole guide at a glance

  1. Around a chosen Cu, a circle of radius R in the CuO₂ plane collects nearby Sr donors, whose holes are shared uniformly inside it.
  2. Local hole content is x_local = N_Sr / N_Cu, so a smaller circle has larger counting fluctuations: Δx ∝ √[x(1−x)] / R.
  3. Repeating the EFG calculation over random configurations and fitting the observed width gives R of a few nanometres at 600 K, larger than the average Sr spacing.
  4. Assigning all excess width to holes makes the fitted radius a lower bound; structural broadening would make the true R larger.
  5. The result is a nanoscale sharing constraint with a known direction of bias, not a picture of fixed circular puddles.

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

Counting fluctuations shrink with averaging area, so a fitted hole-spread scale can constrain R within the patch model. For a selected central Cu, Singer draws a circle of radius Rpatch in its CuO₂ plane. The number of Cu sites is approximated by its area divided by a². Sr donors assigned to the nearby La-layer sites contribute holes that are shared uniformly over that patch. The local hole concentration is the donor count divided by the Cu count. Outside the circle, the calculation uses nominal x. Different random configurations yield different local concentrations and hence different EFGs. This prescription is a controlled model of limited spatial averaging. Cu EFG broadening mixes random ionic, electronic and structural contributions.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Nominal x: Sr atoms per LSCO formula unit, used as the average hole-doping label.
  • Electric field gradient (EFG): curvature of the electric potential at a nucleus; determines the Cu quadrupole resonance.
  • Knight shift: the nuclear resonance shift caused by electronic magnetic response, with orbital and spin contributions.
  • T₁ and T₂: recovery of longitudinal nuclear magnetization and decay of transverse coherence; they answer different questions.
  • Hyperfine form factor: the wavevector-dependent weighting through which a nucleus samples electronic spin fluctuations.
  • HWHM: half width at half maximum; the thesis also defines an effective width from the second moment.
Rpatch
Radius of the assumed donor-sharing circle; its diameter is2Rpatch.
Donor-count fluctuation
Variation in the number of Sr contributions sampled inside a finite averaging area.
Lower radius bound
Extra non-electronic width makes an all-electronic fit infer an averaging circle too small.
Binomial model
Random independent donor counting that links averaging area to distribution width.
Source convention
The specified rate, width or axis definition that must accompany numerical comparison with other sources.

A descriptive explanation, step by step

Follow the full argument.

Read the full detailed argument4 parts · about 3 min

Part 1

Define an averaging region

For a selected central Cu, Singer draws a circle of radius Rpatch in its CuO₂ plane. The number of Cu sites is approximated by its area divided by a². Sr donors assigned to the nearby La-layer sites contribute holes that are shared uniformly over that patch. The local hole concentration is the donor count divided by the Cu count. Outside the circle, the calculation uses nominal x. Different random configurations yield different local concentrations and hence different EFGs. This prescription is a controlled model of limited spatial averaging. It neither gives each hole a measured circular trajectory nor identifies a physical wall separating independent patches. Since the donor count fluctuates statistically, small areas have a wider fractional spread and large areas approach uniform doping. The geometrically distinct d1 and d2 donor choices alter the quantitative Cu line conversion and are compared explicitly.

Part 2

Fit the excess width and keep the inequality

The model repeats the EFG calculation for many random donor configurations, forms a frequency histogram and adjusts R until the observed width and line shape are reproduced. At 600 K the reported characteristic radii are a few nanometres and exceed the average Sr separation in the applicable doping regime. Random ionic broadening alone, corresponding to effectively infinite hole-averaging radius, is insufficient to reproduce the full line. But the inferred Cu radius is a lower bound: if another mechanism supplied part of the measured width, less hole variation would be required and a larger averaging radius could fit. Local structural distortions are the leading example. The thesis therefore distinguishes the high-temperature tetragonal analysis from regions where orthorhombic broadening becomes significant. A sharp inferred radius drop there can reflect misassigned structural width rather than a sudden collapse of an electronic domain.

Part 3

The counting law and its failure point

The calculated hole-distribution effective width approximately follows the binomial-counting relation Δxpatch proportional to √[x(1−x)]/R. The factor √ln(4) converts a Gaussian second moment to the thesis's effective HWHM convention. It should not be replaced by an unqualified standard deviation, particularly when the small-count distribution is asymmetric. The independently relaxation-calibrated local extent is comparable to the model width in the stated regime; that is an important consistency check. The model becomes unreliable when the patch contains only about one donor and R approaches the mean Sr separation. In that dilute regime, treating a patch as a smooth locally homogeneous electronic region loses its premise. Also retain the geometry: a quoted radius 3–4 nm corresponds to diameter 6–8 nm. Neither scale is automatically the antiferromagnetic correlation length, whose temperature trend differs from the fitted averaging radius.

Part 4

Why extra broadening makes the fitted radius a lower bound

In the model, a smaller averaging circle contains fewer donor contributions and therefore has larger relative counting fluctuations. Assigning a measured width entirely to those electronic fluctuations yields the radius required to reproduce it. If some width actually comes from structural tilt or another independent contribution, the true electronic width is smaller than the assigned width. The averaging radius required for that smaller width is correspondingly larger. This is the reason the Cu-based fitted radius is a lower bound under the stated treatment, rather than merely a fitted value with symmetric uncertainty.

The bound also explains why radius and diameter should remain explicitly distinguished. A circle with radius R spans 2R, but neither quantity is an imaged sharp domain boundary. The model assumes donor sharing within a selected area and makes a statistical histogram across configurations. Agreement with a spectrum constrains that averaging construction without reconstructing a unique spatial tiling. The binomial relation also rests on donor randomness and the source width convention; its characteristic extent is not automatically a standard deviation. The thesis limits the inference when spectral changes introduce additional structural effects or when electronic calibration fails. The useful result is a nanoscale effective-sharing constraint accompanied by its direction of bias, not a photograph of fixed circular puddles.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

If an overlooked structural contribution is removed, what happens to the inferred Cu R?

Less electronic width is needed, so the patch-model averaging radius can increase; this is why the original value is a lower bound.

Does matching the full line shape prove the patches are circular?

No. The circle is the model's averaging construction; other spatial models can share comparable distribution statistics.

Which part of this unit is measured and which requires a model?

Counting fluctuations shrink with averaging area, so a fitted hole-spread scale can constrain R within the patch model. The original-page observations supply constraints; the added explanation distinguishes the calibration, electronic account or visibility limit needed for interpretation.

Why does structural width bias an all-electronic radius downward?

It overestimates electronic spread, which the model reproduces with an averaging area too small.
