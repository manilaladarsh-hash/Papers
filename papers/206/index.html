Specific heat evidence on the normal state pseudogap

Specific heat and integrated entropy distinguish a depletion of low-energy spectral weight from a simple superconducting gap that redistributes it.

The research question: From heat capacity to missing low-energy entropy?

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

The main argument uses entropy, not just a curve that looks gapped. Electronic heat capacity divided by temperature measures thermally available states, and its integral remembers states missing at lower temperatures. In the underdoped samples discussed here, that deficit persists to high temperature, unlike a simple nearby redistribution of states. The authors then ask whether the pseudogap survives in the normal state hidden below superconductivity. Different choices change the estimated condensation energy, so they compare with independent superconducting indicators and Zn substitution. Their conclusion favors a pseudogap that depletes the states available to the condensate. The guide separates this historical interpretation from directly measured quantities and explains the chemical and energy conventions.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Electronic heat capacity: The part of heat capacity attributed to electronic excitations after removing other contributions.
  • γ coefficient: Cel/T, a temperature-dependent measure sensitive to thermally accessible electronic states.
  • Entropy: The integral of γ from zero to the stated temperature, with consistent amount normalization.
  • Condensation energy: The energy gained relative to a specified nonsuperconducting normal state.

A descriptive explanation, step by step

Follow the full argument.

Part 1

1 · Translate calorimetry into the quantity being counted

The measured electronic heat capacity Cel is divided by temperature to give γ(T). Integrating γ gives the electronic entropy S(T). γ emphasizes the thermally accessible density of states, while entropy accumulates the contribution from all lower temperatures. The paper treats these as windows on low-energy spectral weight around the chemical potential. The thermal window broadens with T; neither curve measures an infinitely sharp density of states at one energy.

Experimentally one must separate electronic heat capacity from a much larger lattice contribution. This short paper reviews the resulting electronic quantities, rather than providing a complete raw-data reduction tutorial. A suppressed γ can indicate a gap-like density of states, but the shape of γ alone is not enough to identify its origin. The underlying ungapped density of states can itself vary with energy, especially in the LSCO comparison.

Part 2

2 · Compare states-conserving and depleted low-energy spectra

Both a triangular depletion and a d-wave-like gap can yield approximately linear low-temperature γ. The important difference in the authors’ argument is whether spectral weight removed near the Fermi energy is restored nearby. If it is redistributed within the relevant energy range, the entropy deficit can recover on heating. In their underdoped data the deficit remains to approximately room temperature. That observation motivates a low-energy states-nonconserving description.

The phrase states nonconserving does not mean that electrons disappear or that the total Hilbert space violates a sum rule. It refers to the low-energy density of states used in the phenomenology and the absence of recovery in the measured window. The paper also reports no resolved sharp onset in these thermodynamic curves. That absence is a statement about these observations and their sensitivity, not a proof that no possible hidden order can exist.

Part 3

3 · Read the oxygen series in Figure 1

For Y₀.₈Ca₀.₂Ba₂Cu₃O₇₋δ, increasing oxygen deficiency δ generally reduces the plane hole density; δ is not the same variable as p. Calcium helps extend the overdoped range. On the overdoped side γ above Tc is roughly common among samples and the entropy curves recover. Near the paper’s δcrit ≈ 0.25 the superconducting jump and high-temperature entropy change markedly; further underdoping produces a persistent deficit.

In Figure 2 the authors fit an underdoped sample with a nearly temperature-independent pseudogap above Tc. Figure 3 then reconstructs an equivalent d-wave gap from the entropy. An equivalent gap is a parametrization that reproduces S(T); it need not be the true superconducting order parameter. A smooth spectral energy scale across Tc therefore does not establish that the pseudogap and coherent superconductivity are the same order.

Part 4

4 · Understand why condensation energy needs a counterfactual

U₀ is obtained from the area between the normal and superconducting entropy curves below Tc. The superconducting entropy is measured, but the normal state in that range is hidden by superconductivity. It must be continued from above Tc using a model. If the normal state retains its pseudogap, its entropy is lower and the resulting condensation energy is smaller. If the entropy loss above Tc is assigned entirely to superconducting fluctuations, a different metallic continuation gives a larger area.

Calorimetry alone cannot choose that continuation independently of interpretation. The authors compare the resulting condensate strength with superfluid density, diamagnetism and irreversibility-field trends. They also note that Zn suppresses superconducting quantities without comparably removing Eg or the high-temperature entropy deficit. Their preferred reading is that the pseudogap persists and weakens the condensate. Treat this as an argued historical interpretation supported by several comparisons, rather than as a direct measurement of a unique microscopic order parameter.

Part 5

5 · Doping trends, energy scales and limits

The authors compare gap scales across YBCO, Ca-doped YBCO, Zn-substituted YBCO and LSCO. Their phenomenological Eg falls with doping on an exchange-energy scale and vanishes near pcrit ≈ 0.17–0.19 in the calibration used here. This numerical interval is not a universal modern pseudogap endpoint for every compound. Their LSCO fit Eg ≈ 1550(1 − x/0.17) K is explicitly discussed for x ≤ 0.15; extrapolating it to larger x and accepting a negative gap would be meaningless.

Entropy at fixed high temperature grows approximately linearly with added holes over the relevant range. This motivates the idea that doping supplies low-energy weight while a correlated hole–spin scale softens. Eg quoted in kelvin is an energy divided by kB, not a transition temperature. The paper links that scale to exchange physics, but it does not solve the microscopic spectral function or derive a complete theory of the pseudogap. Use the entropy comparison as the central experimental argument and the scale model as its interpretation.

Part 6

6 · The entropy comparison is the decisive diagnostic

Specific heat tells how much energy is needed to raise temperature, while entropy measures how many low-energy states have become thermally available. Because S is an integral of γ, a feature removed at a lower temperature remains visible as an entropy offset unless other states compensate for it at a higher temperature. This is why the argument does not end with a depressed γ curve. It follows the missing contribution upward through the measured range.

In a conventional states-redistributing gap picture, the low-energy depletion is balanced by added weight elsewhere within a accessible energy range. Heating sufficiently can recover the corresponding entropy. In the underdoped series discussed here, parallel high-temperature entropy curves retain an offset. The authors use that persistence to motivate a different low-energy description. The word permanent in the source should be read within its phenomenological comparison and experimental temperature window, not as an independently measured sum rule over every electronic energy.

Read the three rows of Fig. 1 together. γ can approach similar-looking values while S still differs because its integral remembers the lower-temperature loss. Dividing S by T changes the visual appearance yet preserves that information when multiplied back by temperature. This teaches a general experimental habit: distinguish a quantity, its derivative, and its integral before treating similar-looking curves as equivalent evidence.

Part 7

7 · A spectral gap is not the same object as the order parameter

The paper constructs an equivalent d-wave gap that reproduces the measured entropy. This is an inverse parametrization: choose a density-of-states model and infer an energy scale giving the same thermodynamic response. A smoothly evolving inferred scale through Tc does not establish that the same order parameter exists above and below Tc. Coherent superconductivity also requires broken phase symmetry and a condensate, while the normal-state pseudogap phenomenology can deplete states without that coherence.

This distinction drives the condensation-energy argument. If the pseudogap continues into the normal state hidden below Tc, the normal entropy continuation remains depleted. If the entire loss is assigned to superconducting precursor fluctuations, one instead chooses a more metallic hypothetical normal curve. The two choices change the area that represents the superconducting energy gain. The authors compare these contrasting results with other indicators of condensate strength rather than claiming that calorimetry alone has measured the hidden normal curve.

The Zn comparison is useful because it changes superconducting behavior without comparably removing the reported high-temperature entropy deficit. That is evidence against equating the pseudogap scale with a unique superconducting pairing scale in the authors' interpretation. It is not a proof that Zn has no other effects, nor a complete microscopic account of impurity scattering. Connecting this source with local impurity NMR makes clear how the same perturbation can constrain bulk thermodynamics and local magnetic response differently.

Part 8

8 · Work with energy units and a bounded doping model

The source often writes an energy scale in kelvin, meaning energy divided by Boltzmann's constant. A fitted Eg of several hundred kelvin is not automatically the temperature of a sharp transition. The exchange-energy comparison and the inferred spectral scale use that convenient convention. When comparing with an energy in electronvolts, multiply by kB and use consistent normalization.

The nearly linear hole-density dependence is an empirical organizing relation over the specified range, with sample-specific calibrations connecting oxygen content or Sr substitution to planar p. Calcium doping is used to access more of the overdoped YBCO range. The collapse of trends is striking precisely because the raw chemical controls differ. It does not make oxygen deficiency δ interchangeable with Sr fraction x, and the inferred endpoint carries the calibration assumptions.

The LSCO low-doping fit cannot be extrapolated until it predicts a negative gap; that would be outside its stated use. The broader comparison also includes an energy-dependent background density of states, which helps explain why γ(T) does not look identical in every family. The useful conclusion is that available low-energy spectral weight and the condensate strength are closely linked in these measurements. The short paper does not supply a unique microscopic spectral function, a complete subtraction recipe for phonons, or independent access to every hypothetical normal state below Tc.

Translate the mathematical statement

Essential equations, meaning and assumptions

γ(T) = Cel(T)/T; S(T) = ∫₀ᵀ γ(T′) dT′

Symbols and units: γ has units of energy per amount per K²; S has units of energy per amount per K. Choose the same molar or per-plane normalization before comparing samples.

Physical meaning: An integral adds the missing contributions from all lower temperatures. A persistent difference in S can remain even when γ at a particular temperature becomes similar.

PDF p. 1

U₀ = ∫₀ᵀc [Sn(T) − Ss(T)] dT

Symbols and units: The result has energy units. If plotting S/T rather than S, the integrand is T[(S/T)n − (S/T)s]; the unweighted graphical area is not U₀.

Physical meaning: This is the energy gained by the superconducting state relative to a specified normal-state continuation. Both entropy normalization and the counterfactual curve must be supplied; integrating S/T without multiplying by T is dimensionally wrong.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Can identical γ at high T coexist with different entropy?

Yes. γ is the derivative dS/dT. Parallel entropy curves can retain an offset accumulated at lower temperatures.

Why does estimating U₀ require an assumption?

The counterfactual normal entropy below Tc is not directly observed in the superconducting state and must be continued.

Does an Eg of 220 K mean a phase transition at 220 K?

No. It denotes an energy scale Eg/kB in this plot, not an observed ordering temperature.

Why can similar high-temperature γ coexist with different S?

S integrates γ over all lower temperatures. Missing area at lower temperatures leaves a lasting offset even after the instantaneous derivatives become similar.

Does an equivalent d-wave gap establish a superconducting state above Tc?

No. It is a density-of-states parametrization reproducing entropy. Phase coherence and a superconducting order parameter require separate evidence.

What does Eg quoted in kelvin mean?

It is energy divided by kB. It should not automatically be interpreted as the onset temperature of a thermodynamic transition.
