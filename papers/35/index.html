Electronic Liquid Crystal Phases of a Doped Mott Insulator

Stripes can lose their positional order while retaining a preferred direction. That intermediate state is an electronic nematic.

The research question: Can an array of conducting stripes avoid locking into an insulating charge-density wave, and instead support a conducting or superconducting liquid crystal?

In two minutes

The whole guide at a glance

  1. Conducting stripes in a doped Mott insulator have both a position (a period) and a direction, like a smectic liquid crystal.
  2. Transverse fluctuations bend neighbouring stripes differently; the growing arc-length mismatch dephases interstripe charge-density-wave locking.
  3. Josephson pair tunnelling carries no 2k_F arc-length factor, so close encounters between fluctuating stripes can strengthen it instead.
  4. With a spin gap on each stripe, the pair susceptibility is set by K_c, and Josephson coupling can then lock the stripes into a superconductor.
  5. Losing position while keeping direction gives an electronic nematic; a twofold lattice field turns that transition into a crossover.

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

Conducting stripes have two possible ways to cooperate: align their charge-wave maxima or synchronize their superconducting phases. The paper asks whether quantum bending can prevent the first while helping the second. Unequal stripe paths accumulate different finite-wavevector phases, so density locking averages away in the stated model. Pair transfer lacks that extra arc-length phase and can be strengthened by close encounters. Depending on the stripe charge exponent and coupling, the array can superconduct or retain gapless non-Fermi-liquid behavior. Losing positional order while keeping direction then motivates a nematic. The guide distinguishes the perturbative mechanism from the broader symmetry-based melting proposal and preserves the supplied 1997 preprint identity.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Smectic: A liquid-crystal state with positional organization in one direction and liquid-like behavior in another.
  • Nematic: A state retaining a preferred axis without long-range density-wave positional order.
  • Arc length: Actual distance along a bent path, rather than its projected straight distance.
  • Kc: A charge-mode exponent controlling stripe correlation powers in the stated one-dimensional framework.

A descriptive explanation, step by step

Part 1

Research question and system

Can an array of conducting stripes avoid locking into an insulating charge-density wave, and instead support a conducting or superconducting liquid crystal? A two-dimensional array of metallic stripes in a doped antiferromagnet; the main calculation assumes a spin gap on each stripe. Nd-doped LSCO, LSCO and YBCO enter as experimental motivation, not new measurements. A theory of coupled elastic strings and one-dimensional charge modes. The authors average over transverse quantum fluctuations and analyze interstripe CDW and Josephson couplings perturbatively.

PDF p. 1

Part 2

Follow the dephasing argument

The starting objects are conducting stripes with one-dimensional charge modes and transverse quantum motion. A charge modulation has a phase along the stripe’s actual arc length. Bending neighboring stripes differently produces a growing mismatch of those arc lengths. The corresponding density-wave phases then lose their interstripe locking advantage after averaging over fluctuations. This argument concerns finite-wavevector translational order; it does not erase the local charge correlations on each stripe.

Josephson pair transfer behaves differently because it locks superconducting phases without the same arc-length oscillatory factor. Close approaches of fluctuating stripes can enhance its average strength. The competition between density locking and pair transfer is therefore selective, rather than a generic statement that motion destroys every order. Follow the charge parameter Kc and the assumed spin gap before applying the superconducting criterion. A nematic can result from losing positional order while retaining orientation, but the later melting diagram uses symmetry arguments beyond the perturbative stripe calculation.

Part 3

Results and interpretation

Bending changes neighboring stripes' arc lengths differently, dephasing their density modulations. In the model, CDW phase locking is eliminated in the bulk to all orders in the stated perturbative expansion.

Close encounters enhance the average Josephson coupling without the arc-length dephasing that affects finite-wavevector CDW order. For Kc > 1/2 and nonzero pair coupling, the smectic is globally superconducting; Kc < 1/2 with sufficiently weak coupling admits a gapless, non-Fermi-liquid state.

The proposed phase diagram contains crystal, smectic, nematic and isotropic regimes. Its horizontal axis is transverse zero-point fluctuation energy, not a calibrated hole-doping axis. A twofold lattice field rounds the nematic transition into a crossover.

Authors' proposal: electronically generated stripes are light enough to fluctuate strongly, creating phases that conventional heavy molecular solids would not readily support. Their cuprate interpretation is suggestive and explicitly allows alternative readings of the neutron peaks.

Part 4

The stripe is a conducting object, not simply a frozen density band

The starting point is a stripe with its own low-energy charge modes and, in the principal calculation, a gap in the spin excitation spectrum. The surrounding antiferromagnet motivates why such conducting domain walls are considered. The paper does not calculate the formation of an arbitrary stripe from a full multiorbital Hamiltonian and then solve every mode. It begins with a physically motivated low-energy description and asks whether coupling those objects inevitably freezes their charge into a higher-dimensional crystal.

This question matters because an isolated one-dimensional conductor can have strong charge-density-wave and superconducting correlations without either becoming ordinary three-dimensional order. Their susceptibilities diverge with different exponents as temperature is reduced. Coupling neighboring stripes can turn a tendency into collective order, but which coupling wins depends on the charge parameter Kc, the assumed spin gap and the transverse motion. A large pairing-related spin scale on a stripe is not yet global superconductivity.

The elastic-string coordinates keep that motion explicit. Yj(x) tells where stripe j has moved across the array at position x. The actual distance traveled along a bent stripe is its arc length Lj(x), larger than the projected x distance. A density modulation follows that path, so neighboring bends accumulate different finite-wavevector phases even when their average orientations remain parallel.

Part 5

Arc-length mismatch selectively removes density locking

The density-wave coupling tries to align maxima on adjacent stripes. Its cosine contains both a relative charge-mode phase and an arc-length mismatch multiplied by 2kF. If the stripes bend differently along their lengths, the accumulated mismatch receives contributions of both signs. The paper compares its growth to a random walk and obtains a variance increasing with distance in the stated fluctuating-string regime.

Averaging the cosine over that mismatch suppresses its coherent part exponentially in the lowest cumulant treatment. Short close approaches can still strengthen the local coupling magnitude, yet this does not undo the accumulating phase cancellation over the bulk. That is the mechanism behind the perturbative stability claim. The statement to all orders concerns the expansion in the specified interstripe interaction around this low-energy description, rather than an exact solution of every possible doped Mott insulator.

The calculation neglects stripe overhangs and dislocations in the ordered-array regime so that a stripe can be indexed continuously. Once melting creates such defects, the subsequent symmetry discussion takes over. The guide therefore keeps the derivation of stable smectic behavior distinct from the broader nematic and isotropic phase diagram. They are connected arguments with different levels of control.

Part 6

Pair transfer does not carry the same oscillatory phase

Josephson coupling transfers a pair and locks superconducting phases between stripes. In the uniform pair channel used here, it has zero ordering wavevector and lacks the extra 2kF arc-length phase that disrupts density locking. It therefore does not average away by the same mechanism. Because pair tunnelling depends strongly on separation, nearby portions of a fluctuating pair of stripes can dominate the average even when they occupy only a small fraction of the length.

The source's average-versus-median distinction is useful. A broad distribution of local separations can give an average Josephson coupling much larger than its typical value. The Gaussian fluctuation estimate turns the exponential distance dependence into an exponential variance enhancement. This is a model statement about averaging and a specific tunnelling form, not an independently measured cross-stripe coupling in a cuprate.

For Kc > 1/2, the isolated-stripe pair susceptibility diverges strongly enough that nonzero pair coupling yields global superconductivity within the argument. Below that threshold with sufficiently weak coupling, the source admits gapless charge behavior without an ordinary Fermi-liquid description. Thus neither a stripe nor a nematic label alone fixes the electrical ground state. The susceptibility exponent and coupling strength remain essential.

Part 7

Symmetry organizes the proposed melting sequence

A fully locked crystal has positional order in both directions. A smectic remains ordered across stripes while retaining liquid-like charge motion along them. A nematic keeps only the orientation preference, and an isotropic phase restores that preference as well. The proposed transitions are organized by those symmetry losses and by analogy with liquid crystals. The horizontal coordinate of Fig. 2 is transverse zero-point fluctuation energy, not measured hole concentration.

The surrounding lattice changes this ideal picture. Twofold crystal fields already select an axis, so the nematic-to-isotropic symmetry distinction can become a crossover. Fourfold or sixfold symmetry instead leaves discrete choices and changes the transition classification. Commensurate locking and interplane couplings modify positional order and stripe rigidity. These are substantive consequences of the host crystal, not tiny corrections that can always be neglected.

The neutron motivation is correspondingly qualified. Finite-width peaks in Nd-doped LSCO can be discussed as a nematic in an orienting field, but the authors also allow a smectic alternative. Dynamical peaks in related materials motivate fluctuating analogues without giving a direct calibration between doping and the fluctuation-energy axis. The supplied file is the 1997 arXiv version; its generated 2018 header does not change that scientific source identity or convert its proposed phase diagram into a later measurement. In reading the finite-temperature discussion, keep the unbinding of positional defects separate from the orientational transition: these remove different symmetries and need not occur at the same temperature in the model.

What the evidence supports—and where it stops

  • The ordered-stripe calculation neglects dislocations and overhangs. The later nematic phase diagram uses symmetry arguments and liquid-crystal analogies, rather than solving the full cuprate Hamiltonian.
  • The superconducting result depends on the stripe charge parameter and coupling; nematic order alone does not imply superconductivity. Lattice symmetry and interplane effects change the idealized phase behavior.
