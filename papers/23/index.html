Absence of Broken Time-Reversal Symmetry in the Pseudogap State of the High Temperature La2-xSrxCuO4 Superconductor from Muon-Spin-Relaxation Measurements

Zero-field muons constrain a particular static current-order field in LSCO; they do not rule out every pseudogap mechanism.

The research question: Would the proposed circulating-current order produce detectable local magnetic fields?

In two minutes

The whole guide at a glance

  1. The test: one pseudogap explanation, an orbital-current pattern that breaks time reversal, should create a magnetic field at an implanted muon.
  2. Three LSCO crystals cross the pseudogap regime: x = 0.13 (strong), 0.19 (weaker) and 0.30 (none in the source mapping).
  3. All spectra show only the Gaussian Kubo–Toyabe response of nuclear dipoles, nearly independent of temperature and doping.
  4. The specified current pattern predicts about 41 G at the muon site; the data bound a static field to roughly 0.2 G.
  5. Fluctuations faster than about 400 MHz or a different symmetry could still evade the test, so it constrains one model, not every pseudogap order.

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

Zero-field muons constrain a particular static current-order field in LSCO; they do not rule out every pseudogap mechanism. Such a pattern breaks time reversal and should generate a magnetic field at an implanted muon. The samples are LSCO single crystals with x = 0.13, 0.19 and 0.30. The source adopts pseudogap temperatures of about 150 K, 75 K and zero from contemporary ARPES measurements. Comparing all three crystals matters: an order parameter that develops gradually in temperature should still distinguish a strong-pseudogap crystal from a crystal outside that regime. This is a material-specific and pattern-specific test, rather than a direct measurement of the pseudogap energy.

Terms to understand before starting
  • LSCO: layered La2-xSrxCuO4; nominal x is Sr per formula unit
  • Local probe: a distribution can matter even when the bulk average barely changes
Muon polarization
Ensemble average direction of implanted muon spins, inferred from positron asymmetry.
Local field distribution
Range of magnetic fields sampled at stopping sites; its width differs from its mean.
Transverse field
Applied field perpendicular to the initial muon spin, producing a precession signal.
Longitudinal field
Applied field along the initial muon spin, useful for testing weak quasistatic transverse fields.
Motional narrowing
Rapidly changing local fields average over the probe window and can reduce relaxation.
Curie response
Approximately inverse-temperature magnetic susceptibility associated with an effective moment contribution.

What was actually tested

Part 2

How the geometry avoids a false negative

Positive muons enter the crystal with a known spin polarization and decay by emitting positrons preferentially along their instantaneous spin. Detector asymmetries therefore track the ensemble polarization. The initial spin was rotated about 45 degrees relative to the beam and crystallographic c direction, and two orthogonal detector sets were used. This low-symmetry choice makes it unlikely that the local field is parallel to every muon spin at all symmetry-related stopping sites. A single spin in a static field has both a longitudinal component that remains and a transverse component that oscillates. Long-range order would give coherent precession; randomly oriented short-range static fields would instead cause rapid initial depolarization.

Part 3

The observed spectra and the numerical bound

The measured spectra do not show the predicted oscillations or strong depolarization. They fit the Gaussian Kubo-Toyabe response expected from random nuclear dipole fields. Relaxation rates are nearly independent of temperature and doping. A slight narrowing above approximately 175 K is attributed to muon diffusion, not a pseudogap transition. Taking an intentionally generous scatter of 0.01 inverse microseconds gives an upper bound of about 0.2 G for additional local fields. A thin-wire calculation of the proposed current pattern gives about 41 G at the known muon site for the smallest moment cited from YBCO neutron work. The large discrepancy is the central quantitative argument.

Part 4

What the null result does and does not exclude

Destroying long-range coherence alone would not necessarily hide this magnetic field. Small domains with zero average field can still produce a broad local distribution and rapid relaxation. The authors also consider whether the muon charge suppresses nearby order; their model leaves roughly 0.6 G after eliminating order over several nearby cells, still exceeding the quoted bound. These calculations constrain the particular assumptions they use. The paper explicitly does not resolve fields as tiny as those reported in some YBCO studies, does not exclude a quantum critical endpoint of the pseudogap, and notes that reflection-symmetric current patterns can generate much smaller fields at the muon site.

Part 5

Why this connects to the other muon papers

The source leaves rapid fluctuations as another possible reconciliation: a Gaussian 41 G field distribution fluctuating faster than about 400 MHz could be motionally narrowed below detection while appearing static to a much faster neutron measurement. Different cuprate structures might also stabilize different order. This distinction prevents a misleading comparison with field-induced broadening in overdoped LSCO: a response created or stabilized by an external field is not spontaneous zero-field time-reversal breaking. Likewise, Sonier later probes x = 0.33 down to 0.02 K, beyond this composition and temperature range. Keep the sample, field, measurement window and tested order pattern attached to every conclusion.

Part 6

How to compare the evidence

To compare this result with a positive magnetic observation, first ask whether the field was applied, what doping was used, and what counted as static. A transverse-field linewidth in source 26 can grow while the zero-field relaxation here remains nuclear-like: an induced response need not survive when the applied field is removed. Likewise, source 27 studies x = 0.33 at temperatures far below those accessible here. Its dilute weak frozen fields do not restore the much larger predicted current-pattern field in these three crystals. Finally, a bulk magnetometer averaging opposite domains is not equivalent to a local muon: cancellation of the mean does not cancel local depolarization. This sequence prevents three distinct experiments from being forced into one yes-or-no statement about magnetism.

Part 7

Read polarization before naming the magnetic state

A muon is a tiny local magnetic compass, but the recorded signal combines many compasses. The measured asymmetry follows their average spin polarization as a function of time, usually in microseconds. A static field makes an individual spin precess; a collection of different static fields makes those spins gradually lose phase agreement. The resulting envelope can therefore decay even if each field is perfectly stationary. Conversely, rapidly fluctuating fields can average away and produce weak relaxation. These possibilities explain why the words relaxation, freezing and order cannot be substituted for one another. The fitting function describes a distribution and a time window before it identifies a microscopic arrangement.

The initial polarization direction and the applied field direction matter separately. A field transverse to the spin produces precession; a field parallel to the spin changes sensitivity to weak transverse components and can decouple a static distribution. The paper chooses a particular geometry to test a particular question. Reading the time traces requires keeping that geometry attached to the fitted rate. Also separate the mean precession frequency from the envelope: the former tracks an average field, while the latter tracks its spread or time dependence. A narrow mean shift can coexist with a substantial local distribution.

Part 8

Connect a local distribution to bulk response

A magnetometer adds contributions across the specimen, while the muon experiment detects the distribution of fields at stopping sites. Opposite contributions can cancel in the bulk sum and still leave a large field at some individual sites. A small average response therefore does not imply that every microscopic environment is nonmagnetic. Equally, a broad muon distribution is not a photograph of where the moments sit. Converting it into a cluster size, moment concentration or real-space pattern requires a model of stopping sites, couplings and spatial arrangement. The supplied article compares such models with several independent observations rather than treating one damping rate as a complete structural solution.

The most useful reading order is measurement, fit, model and conclusion. First identify what actually changes in the asymmetry trace under the stated control. Next identify which fit component captures the change. Then ask what physical distribution could produce that component, and which competing distributions the other data disfavor. This prevents a descriptive temperature fit from becoming an unearned claim about a unique ordered state. Temperature, Sr composition, field, holder background and accessible time range all belong to the result. Comparisons with another paper become meaningful only after matching those conditions; a positive induced response and a zero-field null can both be correct.

Part 9

A stringent null requires a definite predicted signal

A null result becomes useful when a concrete model predicts a signal above the experimental sensitivity. Here the chain is explicit: the specified circulating-current pattern carries a moment, that pattern generates a calculated field at the known muon stopping site, and such a field should produce precession or depolarization. The comparison is then between that predicted response and the nuclear-like measured spectra. The approximately 41 G model field is much larger than the roughly 0.2 G upper bound assigned to additional fields. This is a substantial mismatch under the calculation’s assumptions, rather than a statement that a detector saw absolutely nothing. Nuclear relaxation is present and provides the background against which a new electronic contribution would be identified.

The authors deliberately compare compositions with different pseudogap assignments. An order that lacks a sharp temperature onset could otherwise be mistaken for a constant background in one specimen. A substantial effect linked to the pseudogap should still differ between the strong-pseudogap and outside-pseudogap compositions. The absence of the expected contrast strengthens the test. It does not directly measure the pseudogap density of states, determine its energy, or independently establish the ARPES temperature mapping used to select the samples.

Part 10

Work through the loopholes without discarding the result

Several apparent loopholes have different physical consequences. Opposite domains might cancel a bulk magnetization, but a local probe still sees their nonzero fields, so domain cancellation alone does not remove muon depolarization. A field parallel to one initial spin could evade that component, but the tilted initial polarization and two detector orientations reduce the geometrical blind spot. The positive muon might disturb nearby electronic order; the paper estimates a residual field in a particular suppression model and compares that estimate with its bound. These are concrete tests of particular evasions, not a proof that no perturbation of any kind is possible.

Rapid fluctuations provide a more substantial distinction between probes. A field can appear approximately static in a fast measurement yet be averaged by a slower local compass. The source’s greater-than-400-MHz example concerns its assumed 41 G Gaussian distribution and is not a universal frequency limit for all magnetic configurations. Different current symmetries may also place a much smaller field at the muon site. Consequently the null restricts an important proposed pattern while leaving some quantum-critical scenarios and other order parameters viable. The high-temperature narrowing attributed to muon diffusion should also be separated from an electronic pseudogap transition. The lesson is to state the tested pattern and sensitivity whenever interpreting the title’s broad language.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Is this a proof that LSCO has no pseudogap?

No. It constrains static magnetic fields expected for a particular pseudogap current pattern.

Why use x = 0.30?

It supplies a nonpseudogap comparison crystal under the source ARPES mapping.

Why can neutrons and muons disagree about static behavior?

Their measurement windows differ; a fluctuation can be slow for one probe and fast for another.

Does a nuclear-like spectrum mean that no magnetic fields exist?

No. Nuclear fields generate the baseline; the bound concerns an extra electronic contribution under the stated sensitivity.

Why is comparing three compositions helpful even without a sharp onset?

A pseudogap-linked field should still distinguish strongly pseudogapped from outside-pseudogap samples if it is large under the tested model.

Why is the 400 MHz estimate conditional?

It is calculated for a particular assumed 41 G Gaussian distribution and motionally narrowed response; changing the distribution or coupling changes the condition.
