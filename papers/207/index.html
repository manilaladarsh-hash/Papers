Doping dependence of the spatially modulated dynamical spin correlations and the superconducting-transition temperature in La2-xSrxCuO4

Low-energy neutron peaks separate with doping and then saturate; their separation can survive pair breaking that destroys superconductivity.

The research question: How are spin incommensurability, coherence length and superconducting transition temperature related across LSCO?

In two minutes

The whole guide at a glance

  1. Neutron scattering on floating-zone LSCO crystals from x = 0 to 0.25 maps low-energy incommensurate spin correlations at (½, ½ ± δ).
  2. Peak separation (2δ) measures a modulation wave vector; peak width measures a dynamical coherence length — two different quantities.
  3. δ ≈ x from about 0.06 to 0.12, then tends to saturate near 1/8 while the Sr content keeps rising.
  4. Removing oxygen (x_eff ≈ 0.106) nearly kills superconducting diamagnetism, yet δ still resembles x ≈ 0.10: δ follows effective doping, not Tc.
  5. Tc ∝ δ holds only as an upper envelope up to optimal doping; it does not prove a pairing mechanism.

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

Low-energy neutron peaks separate with doping and then saturate; their separation can survive pair breaking that destroys superconductivity. Their aim is a quantitative comparison of low-energy dynamical spin correlations across the superconducting dome. Composition, oxygen stoichiometry, lattice constants, structural transition and diamagnetism were checked. Electron-probe measurements and transition rounding constrain macroscopic Sr gradients; this does not establish a perfectly uniform electronic distribution at nanometre scales. The x = 0.18 and 0.25 samples have Tc approximately 36 and 15 K. Common preparation reduces extrinsic relative errors, making the changes in neutron peak position more informative than comparing unrelated specimens.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Neutron momentum scan: distinguish peak position from peak width
  • Incommensurability δ is half the peak-to-peak separation about the AF point
  • Oxygen vacancies change effective holes and can also introduce disorder
Uniform susceptibility
Static magnetic response to a spatially uniform field, at zero wave vector.
Antiferromagnetic correlation
Tendency of neighboring spins to oppose each other; it need not imply long-range order.
Correlation length ξ
Spatial distance over which the chosen spin correlations remain correlated in a model.
Exchange J
Energy scale for coupling neighboring spins; distinct from an ordering temperature.
Spin stiffness ρs
Energy cost of slowly twisting a correlated spin configuration.
Crossover
Operational change of behavior over a temperature interval, without necessarily a singular phase transition.
Weiss offset
Temperature parameter in a fitted Curie-Weiss-like expression; interpretation depends on the model.

Picture 4 / 8

Test the causal leap

Look for: Tc scales with δ only up to optimal doping in well-treated samples.

Removing oxygen from an x = 0.15 crystal reduces effective holes under xeff = x − 2y for La2−xSrxCuO4−y. At xeff approximately 0.106, superconducting diamagnetism is strongly suppressed yet the neutron splitting resembles stoichiometric x approximately 0.10. This demonstrates that δ tracks effective doping more robustly than the actual Tc under pair breaking. Stronger removal toward xeff approximately 0.079 broadens and destabilizes the split response. Oxygen vacancies change both carrier count and the random potential, so this experiment does not isolate a pure carrier-count effect.

The relation concerns an upper envelope of Tc and does not prove a pairing mechanism.

Picture 5 / 8

Read three independent peak properties

Look for: The neutron scan can reveal several changing quantities at once.

The neutron scan can reveal several changing quantities at once. The displacement δ describes the peak position relative to the commensurate antiferromagnetic wave vector. The separation of a symmetric pair is twice that displacement. Peak width describes how sharply the correlations are defined in momentum and, under the stated resolution treatment, constrains a dynamical coherence length. Intensity concerns the scattering weight within the measured energy and geometry. A narrower peak need not have a larger displacement, and a weaker peak need not return to the commensurate position.

These distinctions are necessary to understand why the special one-eighth region can show enhanced coherence while the broader doping trend concerns saturation of δ.

Part 2

What δ and the peak width actually mean

Scans pass through reciprocal positions (1/2, 1/2 ± δ, 0), indexed in the high-temperature tetragonal cell. Their separation is 2δ; δ is the offset from the commensurate antiferromagnetic position. The fitted half width kw, after accounting for instrumental resolution, reflects the inverse dynamical coherence length at the measured energy. These two quantities answer different questions: modulation period versus spatial coherence. Incommensurate low-energy peaks occur across x = 0.06 to 0.25. The source resolves only a commensurate peak at x = 0.04 in its conditions, with an upper bound on any unresolved δ. That historical low-energy result must not be converted into a general claim that all lightly doped LSCO spin correlations are commensurate under every condition.

Part 3

Doping dependence and the special one-eighth region

Between approximately 0.06 and 0.12, δ follows the empirical relation δ ≈ x. Beyond x near 0.12, it tends to saturate around 1/8 while Sr content continues to increase. The authors argue that oxygen-vacancy-induced saturation of the chemical hole count cannot explain this earlier saturation in their stoichiometric range. Peak widths vary weakly over much of the underdoped and near-optimal region, with anomalously narrow peaks near x = 0.12 and broader, weaker response at x = 0.25. Width also depends on energy and temperature, so different scans cannot be compared without their conditions. The one-eighth enhancement of coherence is a source observation; a full theory of the accompanying suppression of Tc is not established here.

Part 4

The oxygen-removal control is decisive but not simple

Removing oxygen from an x = 0.15 crystal reduces effective holes under xeff = x − 2y for La2−xSrxCuO4−y. At xeff approximately 0.106, superconducting diamagnetism is strongly suppressed yet the neutron splitting resembles stoichiometric x approximately 0.10. This demonstrates that δ tracks effective doping more robustly than the actual Tc under pair breaking. Stronger removal toward xeff approximately 0.079 broadens and destabilizes the split response. Oxygen vacancies change both carrier count and the random potential, so this experiment does not isolate a pure carrier-count effect. Zn comparison data, which leave nominal hole count nearly unchanged to first order, support the separate robustness of modulation against pair breaking.

Part 5

Correlation with Tc and the proposed scenarios

For stoichiometric samples up to optimal doping, Tc scales approximately linearly with δ; oxygen-added comparison materials also fall near that relation. Overdoped crystals deviate because Tc decreases while δ saturates, and pair-broken crystals can retain δ without bulk superconductivity. The paper therefore interprets the relation as one involving the maximum attainable Tc at a given doping. It discusses Fermi-surface nesting, stripe pictures and carrier-character crossover. A proposed change from oxygen-like to copper-like carriers, supported indirectly through optical-weight comparisons, is explicitly speculative. Incommensurate spin response alone neither demonstrates static charge stripes nor selects a unique pairing interaction. Its durable constraint is that any account of the superconducting dome must accommodate both the modulation trend and its survival when superconductivity is degraded.

Part 6

How to compare the evidence

When reading a two-peak neutron scan, mark the midpoint first, then the offset of either peak from it, and finally each peak width. The peak-to-peak separation is 2δ, whereas δ is the paper’s incommensurability; the width describes a different correlation scale. A broad pair can therefore have a similar δ but shorter dynamical coherence than a narrow pair. The oxygen-reduction experiment reinforces this distinction: effective hole count changes, while disorder and superconductivity also change. A finite incommensurability in a nonsuperconducting specimen shows that these spin correlations are not simply the superconducting order parameter. Conversely, the relation between maximum attainable Tc and δ is an empirical envelope, not an equation predicting the actual Tc of every disordered or overdoped sample.

Part 7

Uniform response is not the antiferromagnetic response

An antiferromagnet can have large opposing local spin correlations while its uniform magnetization is modest. Uniform susceptibility describes the response to a spatially uniform applied field, corresponding to zero wave vector. Nuclear spin-lattice relaxation instead sums dissipative spin fluctuations over wave vector with a hyperfine weighting and samples them near the nuclear resonance frequency. The article uses this distinction to relate Cu relaxation to enhanced antiferromagnetic correlations while treating the uniform contribution separately. A suppression in the weighted rate therefore constrains the low-frequency response available to that probe; it does not by itself provide a full energy gap or a complete momentum map.

It is helpful to imagine changing the height, width or characteristic frequency of an antiferromagnetic fluctuation peak. Each change can alter the nuclear rate, and different probes respond differently to those changes. A static susceptibility measurement constrains another part of the response, while a transverse indirect-coupling measurement can supply additional spatial-correlation information. The measured rates are complementary rather than interchangeable. A model becomes persuasive when several channels agree under the same assumptions. Matching one temperature curve with adjustable parameters is less restrictive than also matching its absolute scale, orientation dependence and ratios to other channels.

Part 8

Read temperature transformations before locating a crossover

The same recovery data can look very different when displayed as 1/T1, 1/(T1T) or T1T. Dividing by T removes an explicit thermal factor in the conventional fluctuation formula; taking the reciprocal changes a Curie-Weiss-like rate into an approximately straight baseline. A maximum in one representation is therefore not necessarily a maximum in another. The paper defines its comparison using a specific plotted quantity and temperature interval, which must stay attached to the claimed crossover. Without that information, a reader might mistake a smooth baseline departure for a sharply measured thermodynamic transition.

The source also distinguishes model parameters from measured temperatures. An exchange energy, a stiffness, a Weiss offset and a departure temperature can all be expressed in kelvin, but they describe different physical things. Similar units do not make them the same scale. Doping may leave a high-temperature local exchange scale approximately intact while changing long-distance correlations and the low-temperature response substantially. Likewise, a fitted offset can depend on the chosen background subtraction. The correct reading asks which scale comes directly from the curve, which comes from a fit, and which is interpreted through a microscopic model. This preserves the useful comparison while preventing a phenomenological fit from becoming an exact phase diagram.

Part 9

Keep position, width and intensity in separate boxes

The neutron scan can reveal several changing quantities at once. The displacement δ describes the peak position relative to the commensurate antiferromagnetic wave vector. The separation of a symmetric pair is twice that displacement. Peak width describes how sharply the correlations are defined in momentum and, under the stated resolution treatment, constrains a dynamical coherence length. Intensity concerns the scattering weight within the measured energy and geometry. A narrower peak need not have a larger displacement, and a weaker peak need not return to the commensurate position. These distinctions are necessary to understand why the special one-eighth region can show enhanced coherence while the broader doping trend concerns saturation of δ.

Instrumental conditions enter each comparison. Resolution broadens an intrinsically sharp feature, and the measured widths also change with energy and temperature. The x = 0.25 scan uses different conditions from some other compositions, so a direct visual comparison of raw curves would not isolate intrinsic coherence. The paper fits and compares these observables with their conditions attached. Its low-x commensurate result is therefore a finite-resolution observation in the stated experiment, not a timeless assertion that every lightly doped specimen lacks incommensurate correlations.

Part 10

The pair-breaking control separates correlation from sufficiency

A correlation between Tc and δ in well-treated underdoped samples might tempt a reader to conclude that finite incommensurability guarantees superconductivity. The oxygen-removal and Zn controls show why that conclusion fails. Superconductivity can be strongly degraded while a displaced spin response remains. The modulation wave vector therefore survives a disturbance that suppresses pairing, and it cannot by itself be a sufficient condition for a superconducting transition. The source describes the observed Tc relationship as an upper envelope associated with the best attainable transition at a given doping, rather than an equation for every disturbed sample.

Oxygen reduction is not a clean one-variable operation: in the ionic-counting description it lowers effective holes, while the vacancies also add disorder. The corresponding splitting resembles an appropriate lower-doping stoichiometric sample, but the suppression of superconductivity carries both changes. Zn offers a complementary perturbation because nominal carrier count is much less altered to first order, yet its own scattering and induced response remain relevant. Combining controls is more informative than pretending either changes only one physical parameter. The direct result is a robust range-limited modulation trend and its survival under pair breaking; nesting, stripes and changing carrier character remain candidate microscopic explanations.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

Does narrower kw imply larger δ?

No. Width gives coherence and offset gives modulation; they are independent fitted observables.

Why use identical growth procedures?

They reduce relative systematic uncertainty from impurities, oxygen content and composition.

Does this directly observe static charge stripes?

No. It measures low-energy dynamical spin correlations in reciprocal space.

What is the separation between peaks at QAF−δ and QAF+δ?

It is 2δ; δ is the offset of either peak from the commensurate center.

Does a narrower peak imply a larger modulation wave vector?

No. Width and displacement are different observables; coherence can increase without a larger δ.

What logical claim does survival under pair breaking reject?

It rejects the idea that a finite spin modulation alone is sufficient to guarantee superconductivity.
