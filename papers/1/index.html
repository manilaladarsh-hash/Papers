Quantum Theory of a Nematic Fermi Fluid

A metal can become nematic by spontaneously distorting its Fermi surface. Its soft orientational mode can strongly disrupt ordinary quasiparticles.

The research question: What happens to collective modes and single-electron excitations when an isotropic Fermi liquid becomes a nematic metal?

The whole argument in everyday language

First, see what this work is trying to explain.

This paper reaches nematicity from a metal whose Fermi surface can distort, rather than from already formed stripes. The distortion chooses an axis without making a density wave. Its orientational fluctuations interact with gapless electron–hole excitations and are mostly overdamped. In the Gaussian critical theory that damping gives a z = 3 scale, and the one-loop electron width becomes strongly direction dependent. Most directions cease to fit a simple sharp-quasiparticle picture, while special axes are exceptions. A real lattice can pin the orientation and change the lowest-energy behavior. The guide distinguishes the symmetry result, the calculated approximation and the unresolved final phase.

Terms to understand before starting
  • Director: An axis unchanged by reversing its orientation.
  • Quadrupolar distortion: A twofold deformation of a momentum distribution or response without its isotropic component.
  • Landau damping: Loss of collective-mode coherence through coupling to gapless particle–hole excitations.
  • Goldstone mode: A soft fluctuation associated with spontaneously broken continuous symmetry.

A descriptive explanation, step by step

Follow the full argument.

Part 1

Research question and system

What happens to collective modes and single-electron excitations when an isotropic Fermi liquid becomes a nematic metal? Primarily spinless fermions in a rotationally invariant two-dimensional continuum with a quadrupolar interaction. Three-dimensional and lattice extensions are discussed; no particular cuprate composition is calculated. Introduce a symmetric traceless quadrupolar field, integrate out fermions, find saddle points, compute Gaussian collective modes, then calculate the one-loop electron self-energy.

PDF p. 1

Part 2

Follow the instability into unusual dynamics

A quadrupolar deformation changes the shape of the Fermi surface while preserving translation symmetry. The important distinction is between the static energetic cost of the deformation and the dynamics of a spatially varying fluctuation. Integrating over gapless particle–hole excitations produces Landau damping. Consequently a soft collective mode need not be a sharp propagating wave; its spectral weight can be broad even near zero frequency.

The z = 3 scaling follows the balance of stiffness and damping in the critical theory. The one-loop electron scattering rate with a two-thirds power falls more slowly with energy than an ordinary quasiparticle rate, so the lifetime can become inadequate for a simple Landau description. The angular factor matters: the special axes are exceptions, not proof that the generic rate applies everywhere. Continuous rotational symmetry supports an orientational Goldstone mode; a lattice supplies an orientational restoring force and a low-energy crossover. Keep those continuum and crystal statements separate when relating the theory to experiment.

Part 3

Results and interpretation

In this paper's normalization, the isotropic saddle point is stable for 2 NF F2 > −1. Violating that condition produces a quadrupolar Fermi-surface distortion and nematic order.

The soft longitudinal critical mode has dynamic exponent z = 3. In the ordered continuum nematic, the orientational Goldstone mode is Landau overdamped for generic propagation directions, with special symmetry directions supporting longer-lived modes.

At one loop, the generic scattering rate scales as |ε|^(2/3), with a strong angular factor. Along principal axes the rate instead scales as |ε|^(3/2). Lattice anisotropy gaps the orientational mode and can restore an anisotropic Fermi liquid below a crossover energy.

Authors' conclusion: damping by the particle-hole continuum signals breakdown of the Fermi-liquid expansion over most of the continuum nematic Fermi surface. They do not settle whether the ultimate low-energy state remains a non-Fermi liquid or develops another order.

Part 4

Define an electronic director without turning electrons into rods

The nematic order parameter is a symmetric traceless rank-two tensor. Symmetric means it records an axis rather than an oriented arrow; traceless removes the isotropic part. In two dimensions its components can be written using an amplitude and a double-angle phase. Rotating the axis by 180 degrees leaves the same director. This is why the liquid-crystal language can describe point-like electrons: what becomes directional is their collective momentum distribution and response.

In the weakly distorted metallic example, the Fermi line becomes elliptical. The state remains spatially uniform, so translations need not be broken. Its shape and effective masses distinguish axes, and the order is even under time reversal in the principal spinless model. This route starts from an isotropic Fermi liquid rather than from a melted density-wave array. The shared nematic symmetry does not require the same microscopic mechanism as the earlier stripe paper.

At the saddle point, a sufficiently negative quadrupolar Landau interaction overcomes the cost of distortion. The inequality uses the paper's normalization of F₂ and density of states NF. Importing a differently normalized Landau parameter without conversion can change a displayed factor of two. The subsequent field treatment is a method for computing collective response about that state, rather than direct proof that a particular cuprate satisfies the instability condition.

Part 5

A gapless mode can be slow without being a propagating wave

A continuous rotational symmetry broken by the nematic produces a soft orientational mode. Its restoring force tends to zero at long wavelength, but softness does not guarantee a sharp oscillation. The electrons retain gapless particle–hole excitations, and the mode can lose coherence by creating those excitations. This Landau damping is generated by the same metallic environment that supports the distorted Fermi surface.

At the critical point, the inverse longitudinal response contains a stiffness proportional to q² and a damping term proportional to frequency divided by q. Balancing their scale gives frequency proportional to q³ and hence z = 3 in the Gaussian treatment. The transverse critical mode has a different higher-energy character and does not control that low-energy scaling. Naming the polarizations correctly matters because the source first defines them relative to the fluctuation momentum in the isotropic state and then discusses amplitude/orientation mixtures in the ordered state.

Inside the nematic, generic propagation directions mix amplitude and orientation dynamics. Special crystal-free symmetry directions suppress parts of the damping and can support long-lived modes. Figure 1 plots several propagation angles separately and offsets the spectra vertically. The sharp special-angle peak is an exception within a mainly overdamped response, not evidence that every director fluctuation is an ordinary undamped wave.

Part 6

The electron lifetime becomes direction dependent

The one-loop calculation asks how the fluctuating director changes an electron excitation on the Fermi surface. At generic angles, the imaginary self-energy has a two-thirds energy power, multiplied by a strongly angle-dependent factor. A rate falling more slowly than the excitation energy undermines the assumption of a sharp long-lived quasiparticle as energy is reduced. Along the principal axes, the leading generic angular factor vanishes and a different three-halves power allows longer-lived excitations.

This contrast is central rather than incidental. One measurement sampling a special direction can find a more coherent response while most of the Fermi surface remains strongly scattered in the calculation. The guide therefore labels angular exceptions beside the exponent, rather than presenting one exponent as a uniform rate for every electron. The self-energy is an energy-width quantity in the source convention; a physical time rate requires the appropriate ħ conversion.

The paper also states the perturbative limitation. At fixed excitation energy the calculation improves for its chosen large-range parameter, but at a fixed finite parameter the expansion can fail at sufficiently low energy. It does not settle whether the eventual state is a stable non-Fermi liquid or develops another order such as superconductivity. The result demonstrates a breakdown of the starting Fermi-liquid expansion in that regime, while the final ground-state classification remains open.

Part 7

A lattice changes the low-energy symmetry question

The ideal model has full continuous rotations; an actual crystal does not. A lattice can select directions and provide a restoring force for rotating the director, giving the would-be Goldstone mode a gap. The source argues that this can recover an anisotropic Fermi-liquid regime below a low crossover scale. Above that scale, continuum-like anomalous response can still be relevant if the lattice anisotropy is weak.

The distinction prevents a frequent overextension. The paper's statement of a gapless director throughout the ordered continuum phase is not a universal prediction that every nematic crystal has an exactly gapless mode. Likewise a finite low-temperature transport anisotropy does not by itself determine whether the fluctuations were spontaneous or explicitly lattice biased. The related resistivity-to-order expression uses the model's assumptions and simple transport arguments; domain scattering or more complex bands can change the comparison.

The three-dimensional extension has another symmetry change: a cubic invariant in the tensor free energy is allowed, making a first-order transition generic in that discussion. Spinful and hexatic extensions are proposed possibilities. These extensions broaden the conceptual framework but are not additional experimental discoveries reported by the paper. The guide therefore connects them through symmetry rather than treating every speculative state as observed.

Essential equations, meaning and assumptions

Q11 + iQ12 = Q exp(2iθ)

Symbols and units: Q is director amplitude; θ is its orientation. Tensor components share the order-parameter normalization.

Physical meaning: The double angle makes the director invariant under θ → θ + π, expressing an axis rather than an arrow.

PDF p. 1

Isotropic saddle stable when 2NF F₂ > −1

Symbols and units: NF is the Fermi-level density of states and F₂ the quadrupolar interaction in this paper's convention.

Physical meaning: Crossing the boundary favors a quadrupolar deformation at saddle-point level; other normalizations require conversion.

PDF p. 2

L++ ≈ κq² + δ − iω/(vFq)

Symbols and units: q is inverse length, vF velocity, κ a stiffness coefficient and δ the distance parameter. The source uses a consistent reduced response normalization.

Physical meaning: At δ = 0 the leading stiffness and damping balance as ω ∝ q³. L is an inverse collective propagator, not the electron self-energy.

Symbols and units: k lies on the Fermi line; omitted coefficients supply energy units and depend on the source parameters.

What the evidence supports—and where it stops

  • The Gaussian and one-loop results are approximation-dependent. The paper warns that fixed-parameter perturbation theory may fail at very low energy; its conclusions are not an exact solution of all nematic metals.
  • A continuum Goldstone mode is different from an Ising nematic on a crystal lattice. The cuprate ARPES comparison is speculative, and the authors note an orientation mismatch in their simplest picture.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud, then open the explanation.

What is the central research question?

What happens to collective modes and single-electron excitations when an isotropic Fermi liquid becomes a nematic metal?

What is measured or assumed?

Introduce a symmetric traceless quadrupolar field, integrate out fermions, find saddle points, compute Gaussian collective modes, then calculate the one-loop electron self-energy.

What limits the interpretation?

The Gaussian and one-loop results are approximation-dependent. The paper warns that fixed-parameter perturbation theory may fail at very low energy; its conclusions are not an exact solution of all nematic metals. A continuum Goldstone mode is different from an Ising nematic on a crystal lattice. The cuprate ARPES comparison is speculative, and the authors note an orientation mismatch in their simplest picture.

Why does the tensor use twice the angle?

A nematic director is an axis. Rotating by π returns the same state, so exp(2iθ) expresses the correct identification.

How does z = 3 follow from the inverse response?

At criticality the restoring term scales as q² while damping scales as ω/q. Balancing them gives ω proportional to q³.

Why does a lattice change the Goldstone conclusion?

It explicitly selects directions and supplies an orientational restoring force. The mode can be gapped, with a lower-energy anisotropic-metal crossover.
