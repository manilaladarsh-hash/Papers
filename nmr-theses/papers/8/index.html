Single Crystal NMR of High Temperature Superconductors

In this 1989 thesis, a tiny single crystal separates plane-Cu local-moment signatures from chain-Cu relaxation dominated by oxygen holes; isotope echoes then constrain an indirect nuclear coupling.

The question: What do direction-resolved copper NMR tensors reveal about the Cu orbitals, local moments and oxygen holes of superconducting YBa₂Cu₃O₇−δ?

Before you start: the terms you need
  • Plane Cu / chain Cu: the two crystallographic copper sites in YBCO
  • EFG: electric field gradient, which couples to a nuclear quadrupole moment
  • Magnetic shift: the field-proportional frequency change after quadrupole contributions are separated
  • W₁: the thesis’s elementary satellite population-transition rate, not automatically 1/T₁
  • Redfield contribution: echo decay generated by the fluctuating fields also responsible for population recovery
  • Transferred hyperfine coupling: a nuclear spin senses electronic spins through orbital mixing or intervening atoms

The question belongs to the 1989 normal-state problem

Pennington studies why the normal state of a 90 K cuprate superconductor differs from a simple conventional metal. The experiment uses copper nuclear magnetic resonance on a roughly 1 mg YBa₂Cu₃O₇−δ single crystal, with the static field successively along the crystal axes. This is a microscopic study, rather than a demonstration of useful superconducting transport. Its central advantage is that the two Cu sites and the field directions can be distinguished. The thesis measures lineshapes, population recovery and echo decay, turning these into electric-field-gradient, magnetic-shift, spin-lattice-relaxation and transverse-relaxation tensors. The key temperature-resolved single-crystal results are at 100 and 200 K, both above the sample’s approximately 90 K transition. Superconducting-state shifts used later are measurements from other work discussed in the thesis. These distinctions matter: the resulting interpretation combines directly acquired tensors, atomic-physics inputs and external constraints. Historical introductory arguments about pairing mechanisms or neutron experiments describe the evidence available in 1989, and should not be read as a current survey of cuprate physics.

Start by determining which resonance belongs to which Cu

Two zero-field Cu resonances near 31.5 and 22 MHz had conflicting site assignments. A zero-field powder NQR frequency alone does not identify a direction in the crystal. The single-crystal field-dependent spectrum supplies the missing information. Plane Cu is approximately fourfold symmetric about c, while chain Cu has an approximately fourfold local coordination about a. The measured shift and EFG symmetries therefore associate one family of lines with plane Cu; its fitted EFG predicts the 31.5 MHz NQR line. The chain family predicts approximately 22 MHz. This is a concrete inference from crystal symmetry and a spin Hamiltonian fitted to several transitions, rather than an assignment made by choosing the narrower or faster-relaxing line. Twinning produces simultaneous a- and b-oriented domains when the field lies in the plane. The plane central shifts nearly overlap for those directions, but some satellite lines split. The thesis explicitly allows reversal of certain a/b satellite labels. Reliable learning therefore keeps the robust plane-versus-chain assignment separate from that smaller label ambiguity.

A Cu²⁺-like orbital is an EFG interpretation

The thesis then asks how charge is distributed. Assigning Y³⁺, Ba²⁺ and seven O²⁻ ions would require total Cu charge +7, suggesting two plane Cu²⁺ ions and one chain Cu³⁺ ion. The EFG analysis instead accounts for both sites using a single 3d hole: plane x²−y² and chain y²−z², with x, y and z corresponding to a, b and c. The extra formal charge is assigned to oxygen holes. This conclusion uses more than charge counting. A 3d hole contributes a strongly anisotropic EFG, while nearby ions contribute a lattice term enhanced by a fitted Sternheimer factor. Using atomic Cu²⁺ parameters and a point-charge lattice, the author finds a plausible fit for the measured tensors. A preferred oxygen configuration puts the additional hole on chain and bridge oxygens, but the thesis does not rule out all configurations containing plane-oxygen holes. Thus the learning result is a source-specific Cu²⁺-like orbital/valence model, not a direct unique map of every oxygen’s charge or a statement that covalent solids have exact isolated-ion charges.

Plane and chain nuclei weight different electronic channels

For plane Cu at 100 K, W₁a and W₁b are 4.7±0.3 ms⁻¹ and W₁c is 1.05±0.1 ms⁻¹. The rate ratio is about 4.48. The thesis explains the directional relaxation and magnetic shifts using a nearly permanent Cu local moment, anisotropic on-site hyperfine coupling and transferred coupling to neighboring Cu spins. A rapid fluctuating moment can shift a line without producing a static ordered moment: its time-averaged polarization determines a spin shift, while its fluctuating field produces relaxation. Adding superconducting-state shift constraints requires a small oxygen-hole contribution to the plane shift, even though its inferred population-relaxation contribution is almost negligible. The chain site behaves differently. Its W₁ is approximately isotropic within error and roughly linear in temperature. Combining its relaxation with the separated isotropic Knight shift supports transferred coupling to a relatively weakly interacting oxygen-hole band in the author’s model. This does not prove the absence of a chain-Cu moment. The thesis considers that moment plausible but less directly observed, because this particular nucleus is dominated by the oxygen-hole channel.

Echo decay adds evidence that population recovery alone misses

Population recovery is not the whole story. Redfield theory relates the measured fast fluctuating fields to a baseline exponential transverse decay, subject to an extrapolation of their spectrum toward zero frequency. The plane Cu echo for H₀∥c additionally has a Gaussian component. Its isotope dependence includes both gyromagnetic ratios and the probabilities of finding like-isotope neighbors; that behavior fits coupling between nuclei that are both flipped by the echo pulses. In a SEDOR experiment, applying a pulse to ⁶³Cu changes the measured ⁶⁵Cu echo. This is a much more direct test of coupling than merely fitting a Gaussian. The observed effect is stronger than the direct nuclear magnetic dipole interaction, motivating an electron-mediated coupling model. Under its approximations, the worked calculation and conclusion give J_eff≈1700 cm⁻¹. This is an effective scale defined through the many-electron response, and is compared with a nearest-neighbor exchange J≈950 cm⁻¹ in oxygen-depleted antiferromagnetic YBCO. Their orders of magnitude are comparable; the two definitions and materials are not identical.

How to read and assess the full thesis

Read the guide in the order experiment → tensor assignment → recovery model → electronic interpretation. First inspect the original line positions and rate tables rather than only the final narrative. Next compare the central and satellite recoveries: different sums of exponentials agree with one shared W₁, supporting a fast magnetic relaxation mechanism across 61–124 MHz. Then compare the measured echo/isotope response with the particular indirect-coupling model. Finally revisit the iteration diagrams that combine normal-state tensors, antiferromagnetic resonance information and superconducting-state shift constraints. Their parameter plots vary a core-polarization input; their horizontal axis is not temperature or doping. The appendices explain exact spin-Hamiltonian fitting, orbital shifts, Redfield rates, multilevel recovery and pulse-control artifacts. They make the apparent simplicity of a line, T₁ or T₂ into a set of assumptions you can inspect. The scan contains an internal exchange-number inconsistency: § 7.33 opens with 2400 cm⁻¹, while its worked result, abstract and conclusion say 1700 cm⁻¹. The guide reports this rather than silently harmonizing the source.

Read the evidence Guided original source pages

What the evidence supports—and where it stops

  • The supplied work is a 1989 thesis, not a current consensus review. Introductory upper-Tc and pairing arguments retain that date.
  • Single-crystal population and echo measurements primarily compare 100 and 200 K at 81.2 kG; superconducting-state shifts and antiferromagnetic resonance constraints come from other work discussed in the thesis.
  • Cu valence/orbitals and oxygen-hole distribution are model-based EFG interpretations using a point-charge lattice, atomic inputs and fitted enhancement.
  • The local-moment analysis uses exponential fast fluctuations, neighbor-coupling assumptions and atomic-physics input ranges; a fit does not uniquely determine the full electronic theory.
  • Chain local moments are proposed less directly than plane moments, and the chain’s additional echo-decay mechanism is unresolved.
  • The exchange estimate is internally inconsistent: § 7.33 opening says 2400 cm⁻¹; the worked calculation, abstract and conclusion say 1700 cm⁻¹.
  • The OCR is private reading assistance. Equations and selected original figures were checked against the scans; not every appendix algebra line was independently rederived.

Explain it back.

Try answering aloud before opening the explanation.

Why was a tiny single crystal worth the difficult signal averaging?

It retained field-direction information and separated crystallographic site responses that overlap in powder measurements.

Does Cu²⁺ in the model mean the oxygen-hole location is uniquely known?

No. The EFG analysis favors a Cu²⁺-like single-hole configuration, but point-charge approximations and several oxygen distributions leave uncertainty.

Does Gaussian echo decay alone prove electronic exchange?

No. Isotope abundance scaling and SEDOR establish nuclear coupling; its strength and a microscopic model support the electron-mediated interpretation.

Are 1700 and 950 cm⁻¹ two measurements of the same J?

No. J_eff is defined by an indirect-coupling model in the superconducting composition, while the comparison J is a nearest-neighbor exchange from an oxygen-depleted antiferromagnet.
